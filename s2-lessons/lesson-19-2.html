Lesson 19.2
Frequency and Relative Frequency Tables
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Counting is the oldest statistical act — and dividing by the total is what makes counts comparable.
Tallying, frequency tables, relative frequency, and cumulative relative frequency
Reading & work: Read every section in order, work the examples alongside the text, practice the Desmos and Excel entries, and complete the three activities at the bottom. Plan on about 65 minutes.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to build a frequency table from raw data with a tally and a total check.
2
I will be able to compute relative frequencies and percents and verify that they add to 1 (or 100%, allowing for rounding).
3
I will be able to use a cumulative relative frequency column to answer “at most” questions for numerical data.
4
I will be able to choose relative frequency, not raw counts, to compare groups of different sizes.

📑 FROM A PILE OF ANSWERS TO A TABLE
Suppose you ask 25 students, “How do you usually get to school?” and write the answers in the order they arrive:
Car, Bus, Walk, Bus, Car, Bike, Car, Bus, Bus, Walk, Car, Car, Bike, Bus, Car, Walk, Bus, Car, Bus, Car, Car, Walk, Bus, Car, Bike
That is raw data: honest, complete, and nearly unreadable. The first move — the move John Graunt made with the Bills of Mortality — is to sort and count. The number of times a value occurs is its frequency. A frequency table lists each value or category beside its frequency. (The data here is a constructed example.)
✍️ HOW TO TALLY WITHOUT LOSING ANYONE
Counting by eye through twenty-five words invites a skipped or double-counted answer. Use the discipline of a careful clerk:
▶ List the categories once, down the left side.
▶ Go through the raw list one entry at a time, in order, and make one tally mark by the matching category as you cross the entry out.
▶ Count the marks. Then check: the frequencies must add up to \(n\), the number of individuals. If they do not, someone was lost or counted twice.
Way to schoolFrequency
Car10
Bus8
Walk4
Bike3
Total25
Ten plus eight plus four plus three is 25 — every student accounted for. That total check is not decoration; it is the habit that catches errors before they become conclusions.
📈 RELATIVE FREQUENCY — ANSWERING “WHAT FRACTION?”
“Ten students take the car” is a fact about this class. “Two-fifths of students take the car” is a statement we can compare with other classes. The relative frequency of a value is its frequency divided by the total:
\[ \text{relative frequency} = \frac{\text{frequency}}{n} \]
Multiply by 100 to express it as a percent. Worked example. For the car riders, \(\tfrac{10}{25} = 0.40 = 40\%\). Do each category:
Way to schoolFrequencyRelative frequencyPercent
Car1010/25 = 0.4040%
Bus88/25 = 0.3232%
Walk44/25 = 0.1616%
Bike33/25 = 0.1212%
Total251.00100%
The second check: relative frequencies add to 1 (percents to 100%). Here \(0.40 + 0.32 + 0.16 + 0.12 = 1.00\). Why must they? Because together the categories contain every individual — each individual is counted once, so the pieces of the whole add to the whole.
⚠️ WHEN ROUNDING SPOILS THE TOTAL
Not every division comes out clean. Suppose 30 students choose among four clubs with frequencies 5, 6, 8, and 11. As percents to one decimal place: \(\tfrac{5}{30} = 16.7\%\), \(\tfrac{6}{30} = 20.0\%\), \(\tfrac{8}{30} = 26.7\%\), \(\tfrac{11}{30} = 36.7\%\). Add them: \(16.7 + 20.0 + 26.7 + 36.7 = 100.1\%\).
Nothing is wrong. Each value was rounded, and rounding errors can pile up a tenth of a percent. The honest practice has two parts: round only at the end (keep exact fractions while you work), and if a published table totals 100.1% or 99.9%, say so in a note (“percents may not sum to 100 because of rounding”) instead of quietly nudging one entry. A total that is off by a visible mistake, on the other hand, such as 108%, means an error you must find.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE — A NUMERICAL VARIABLE AND THE CUMULATIVE COLUMN
Frequency tables work for numerical variables too, when the values repeat. Twenty students report how many books they read last month (constructed example). Each value is a row:
Books readFrequencyRelative frequencyCumulative relative frequency
020.100.10
150.250.35
270.350.70
340.200.90
420.101.00
Total201.00
The cumulative relative frequency is a running total of the relative frequencies from the top: \(0.10\), then \(0.10 + 0.25 = 0.35\), then \(0.35 + 0.35 = 0.70\), and so on, ending at 1.00. Its job is to answer “at most” questions. What fraction of these students read at most 2 books? Read the row for 2: 0.70, or 70%. (Check by counting: \(2 + 5 + 7 = 14\) students, and \(\tfrac{14}{20} = 0.70\).) Cumulative columns only make sense when the values have a natural order, so they belong to numerical data, not to categories like “car” and “bus.”
⚖️ WHY RELATIVE FREQUENCY IS THE HONEST WAY TO COMPARE
Section A has 20 students and 8 say they prefer taking a course online. Section B has 50 students and 15 say the same. Which section is more in favor? By raw counts, Section B (15 beats 8). By relative frequency: Section A, \(\tfrac{8}{20} = 40\%\); Section B, \(\tfrac{15}{50} = 30\%\). Section A is the more favorable group, even though it contributed fewer students.
When groups differ in size, comparing frequencies compares the sizes of the groups as much as their opinions. Relative frequencies put every group on the same footing — and reporting which denominator you used is part of telling the truth about a table.
💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · RELATIVE FREQUENCIES IN DESMOS AND EXCEL
In Desmos: type 10/25. Desmos answers with the exact fraction \(\tfrac{2}{5}\); click the fraction-to-decimal toggle to the left of the result to see 0.4. To get a percent, type 10/25*100 → 40. Check a whole table at once: 10/25+8/25+4/25+3/25 → 1. Keep fractions exact until the last step, then round once.

In Excel: put the categories in A2:A5 and the frequencies in B2:B5, with Frequency in B1 and Relative frequency in C1.
1. Click cell B6 and type =SUM(B2:B5) then Enter — this is \(n\).
2. Click C2 and type =B2/SUM($B$2:$B$5) then Enter. (The dollar signs lock the total in place so it does not slide when you copy the formula down.)
3. With C2 selected, drag the small square at its lower-right corner down to C5.
4. Select C2:C5, go to the Home tab, and in the Number group click the Percent Style (%) button to display percents.
5. In C6 type =SUM(C2:C5) — you should see 100%. That is your second check, done by the machine.
🤖 AI TRAINING · PROMPT → PREDICT → VERIFY
Prompt. Paste this raw list into an AI tool and ask it to build a frequency and relative frequency table: “Dog, Cat, Dog, Fish, Dog, Cat, Bird, Dog, Cat, Dog, Dog, Fish, Cat, Dog, Cat, Bird, Dog, Fish, Cat, Dog.”
Predict. Tally it yourself first, crossing off as you go. Dog 9, Cat 6, Fish 3, Bird 2 — total 20. Relative frequencies: 0.45, 0.30, 0.15, 0.10.
Verify. Check the AI's frequencies against yours, then run both checks: do its frequencies add to 20, and do its relative frequencies add to 1? Language tools are known to miscount items in a long list. If it got a count wrong, notice whether the table's own totals would have caught the error — and whether the AI noticed.

2
Compare Honestly
15 min
1
Morning section: 40 students, 14 take the bus. Afternoon section: 25 students, 11 take the bus. Which section has more bus riders? Which has the larger share of bus riders? Show the relative frequencies.
2
In two sentences, explain to a classmate why the second question is the fairer comparison.
▶  Check
By count the morning section has more bus riders (14 versus 11). By share: 14/40 = 0.35 (35%) versus 11/25 = 0.44 (44%). The afternoon section has the larger share. Raw counts reflect section size; relative frequency removes that.
3
Find the Error
15 min
1
A student's table (n = 25): Category A, frequency 9, relative frequency 0.36; B, 7, 0.28; C, 6, 0.24; D, 3, 0.16. The student reports the total relative frequency as 1.04. Find the entry that is wrong, say what the correct value is, and explain how the total check exposed it.
2
Then write one sentence on how a 100.1% total from rounding differs from this 104%.
▶  Check
D is wrong: 3/25 = 0.12, not 0.16, so the total becomes 0.36 + 0.28 + 0.24 + 0.12 = 1.00. The total check flagged the problem because relative frequencies must add to 1. A rounding total is off by a tenth or so and every entry is individually right; 1.04 is a real mistake.
