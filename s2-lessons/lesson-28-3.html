Correlation Is Not Causation
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A strong pattern tells you that two things go together. It does not tell you why.
Learn the standard explanations for an association, test one with real arithmetic, and practice reporting a pattern without claiming more than the data can bear.
Reading & work: Read the worked examples, run the within-group check in Excel, and rewrite two overclaiming headlines. Plan on about 75 minutes. Every dataset, headline, and scenario on this page is constructed (made up) unless the page says otherwise.

✨ RESPONSIBILITY: DO NOT CLAIM WHAT YOU HAVE NOT SHOWN
Last week you learned to draw a line through data and to predict with it. This week you learn how far a line’s story can honestly go. A scatter plot can be tight and a correlation can be strong, and still the data may say nothing about why the two variables go together. The most important habit in this module is a habit of speech: say what the pattern shows, and stop there.
A careless claim has a cost. A town that bans ice cream to prevent pool accidents, or a family that changes its life because of a headline, has acted on a story the data never told. Reporting a pattern carefully is a way of being fair to the people who will read it and act on it.
🔍 FOUR WAYS TO EXPLAIN AN ASSOCIATION
Suppose two variables x and y show a strong association. There are four standard explanations. A responsible analyst keeps all four on the table until the evidence removes some of them.
▶ 1. x causes y. Changing x really does change y. Sometimes this is the truth. The trouble is that the data alone cannot establish it.
▶ 2. y causes x (reverse direction). The arrow runs the other way. Constructed example: in a made-up clinic, patients who take more pain medicine report more pain. Does the medicine cause pain? More likely, more pain leads people to take more medicine.
▶ 3. A third variable drives both (a confounder, or lurking variable). Something not in your scatter plot pushes x and y up and down together. Constructed example: in a made-up group of children, shoe size and reading score rise together. Shoe size does not teach reading, and reading does not stretch feet. Age drives both.
▶ 4. Coincidence (chance). With only a few points, or after checking a great many pairs of variables, some pairs will line up by luck. Constructed example: five made-up students have these (letters in first name, quiz score) pairs: (3, 62), (4, 70), (5, 68), (6, 80), (7, 85). The correlation is about +0.95, very strong. No one believes that a longer name raises a quiz score. With five points, and with the freedom to try many pairs of variables, a striking pattern can be an accident.
These can also combine. A pattern can be partly causal and partly confounded. Your job is not to guess one answer, but to say which are still possible.
🍦 WORKED EXAMPLE 1 — A THIRD VARIABLE, TESTED WITH ARITHMETIC

Constructed (made-up) data.
▶ Step 1, all ten towns together. The correlation is r = +0.88: a strong positive association. Towns that sold more ice cream had more pool incidents. The least-squares line has slope about 0.20, so each extra hundred cones goes with about 0.2 more incidents.
▶ Step 2, a tempting claim. “Ice cream causes pool incidents. Ban ice cream near pools and incidents will drop.” Notice what this claim does: it takes a pattern and recommends an action. The data have not earned that.
▶ Step 3, name a possible confounder. Weather. A hot week sends people to the pool, which gives more incidents, and sends people to the ice cream stand, which gives more cones. Weather could drive both. The data have a weather column, so we can test the idea.
▶ Step 4, split by weather and recompute r inside each group. Warm towns only: x is 18, 22, 25, 29, 33 and y is 2, 5, 6, 3, 4, giving r = +0.19. Hot towns only: x is 50, 53, 57, 60, 66 and y is 10, 12, 13, 9, 11, giving r = −0.08. Inside each group the association is weak, and it does not even point the same way.
▶ Step 5, see where the big r came from. The warm towns average 25.4 hundred cones and 4 incidents. The hot towns average 57.2 hundred cones and 11 incidents. The overall association comes almost entirely from the gap between the two groups: hot towns are higher on both variables. Once weather is held about the same, ice cream sales hardly predict incidents.
▶ Step 6, say it responsibly. “In these ten made-up towns, weeks with more ice cream sales tended to have more pool incidents. The relationship nearly disappears inside each weather group, which fits the idea that hot weather drives both. This does not show that ice cream has no effect, and it does not prove weather is the only factor.”
What did the check show, and what did it not show? It showed that a third variable can fully account for a strong association, and that the association is not a reliable guide for action. It did not prove that ice cream is harmless. Each group has only five towns, so these small within-group correlations are themselves rough. Responsible conclusions are written as “consistent with,” not “proved.”
🔧 HOW TO TEST A SUSPECTED CONFOUNDER
You just did it. The method is short enough to keep in your head.
▶ Name it. Ask: what else could push both variables? Weather, age, size, wealth, time, and how people were chosen are the usual suspects.
▶ Measure it. You can only test a lurking variable you have data on. If you did not collect it, you cannot rule it in or out.
▶ Compare inside groups that are alike on it. Split the data by the suspect (warm versus hot) and recompute r within each group. If the association shrinks sharply, the suspect is a likely explanation. If it stays about as strong, the suspect does not explain it, though another one might.
▶ Report what you did and did not rule out. A check removes some explanations. It rarely removes all of them.
There is a stronger tool, and it is the subject of the next section.
🧪 WHAT CAN SUPPORT A CAUSAL CLAIM?
In an observational study the researcher watches and records what happens, and the subjects end up in their groups by their own choices or circumstances. Every example in this lesson so far is observational. Subjects who choose their own habits may differ in many other ways, so confounders are always possible.
In a randomized experiment the researcher assigns the treatments, and does it by chance (a coin flip, a random number draw). Because chance decides who gets what, the groups tend to be alike on everything else, including factors nobody thought to measure. If the groups then end up different, the treatment becomes the leading explanation. That is why a well-run randomized experiment can support a cause-and-effect claim in a way a scatter plot cannot.
Constructed illustration. Instead of recording which made-up students choose to sleep eight hours, a researcher randomly assigns 200 volunteers to a regular-bedtime schedule or to their usual routine, then compares test scores. The random assignment, not the students’ choices, creates the two groups.
Randomized experiments are not always possible or ethical. You cannot randomly assign people to smoke or to live in poverty. In those cases people build a careful case from many studies and many checks. You will study how experiments, random assignment, and observational studies work in Module 11. For now, remember the sentence: observational data can show an association; random assignment is what lets us talk about cause.
🏭 WHERE PEOPLE USE THIS · CHOCOLATE AND NOBEL PRIZES
A widely discussed published analysis compared countries’ chocolate consumption per person with the number of Nobel laureates per person, and found a strong positive association. It has become a commonly used classroom example of why an association across countries does not show that chocolate makes people win prizes. Think about what else differs between countries. National wealth and research funding are plausible confounders: a richer country may both buy more chocolate and spend more on universities and laboratories. Also notice the unit of analysis. The data are about whole countries, so they say nothing about whether any single person who eats more chocolate becomes more likely to win a prize. (This callout gives no figures on purpose. If you want the details, look the analysis up and check exactly what it claims before you repeat it.)
💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · EXCEL: CORREL OVER TWO RANGES
Excel is the star. =CORREL(C2:C11,D2:D11) gives r for the whole column pair. To find r inside one group, point the same function at a shorter pair of ranges, for example =CORREL(C2:C6,D2:D6). The two ranges must be the same length and must list the same towns in the same order. A third variable shows up when the within-group r is much smaller than the overall r. The full click-by-click steps are in Activity 2.

Desmos Scientific is for plain arithmetic only. Check a group mean by typing (18+22+25+29+33)/5 and reading 25.4, and (50+53+57+60+66)/5 and reading 57.2.
🤖 AI TRAINING · PROMPT → PREDICT → VERIFY (THREE CONFOUNDERS, RANKED)
Prompt. Ask an AI tool exactly this: “Here is a made-up headline: ‘Towns With More Street Musicians Have Higher House Prices, Study Finds.’ Give me three possible confounders for this association, and tell me how I could check each one.”
Predict. Before you read its answer, write your own list of three confounders. For each, decide which of the two variables it could plausibly push.
Verify. Now compare. Then rank the AI’s three confounders yourself, from most to least plausible, and defend your ranking in two or three sentences. A strong defense says how the confounder could affect both variables and what data would test it. Also critique the AI: Did it treat a possible confounder as a proven cause? Did it mention reverse direction or chance, or only a third variable? Did it invent statistics or details about a “study” that does not exist? Did it suggest a check you could actually carry out? Report what you kept, what you changed, and why.
📰 REWRITING A HEADLINE RESPONSIBLY
Most causal overclaims in the news are not in the data. They are in the verbs. Watch for these words:
▶ Overclaiming: causes, leads to, boosts, ruins, prevents, makes, because of, the secret to, proves.
▶ Careful: is associated with, tends to go with, was linked to, was higher among, in this sample.
A good rewrite has four parts: who was studied (the sample), what was measured, the pattern in careful words, and what the data cannot say.
Constructed headline. “Study: Late Bedtimes Make Teens Fail Math.” Suppose the made-up survey asked 120 teens when they go to bed and recorded their math grades, and nobody was assigned a bedtime.
▶ What is wrong: “make” claims cause. The teens chose their own bedtimes, so this is an observational study. Alternatives: a heavy workload or an after-school job could cause both late bedtimes and lower grades (a confounder), or struggling with math could cause stress and late nights (reverse direction).
▶ Careful rewrite: “In a made-up survey of 120 teens, those who reported later bedtimes tended to have lower math grades. The survey cannot say why.”
Notice that the careful version is a little longer and a little less exciting. That is the price of being accurate, and a responsible writer pays it.

Spot the Confounder
20 min
1
For each constructed scenario below, (i) say which of the four explanations is most likely: x causes y, y causes x, a third variable drives both, or chance; (ii) name a plausible confounder or the reverse-direction story; (iii) say how you would check it.
2
(a) In a made-up school, students who eat breakfast have higher test scores.
(b) In made-up fire records, fires that had more fire trucks sent to them had more damage.
(c) In a made-up group of children aged 5 to 12, those with bigger shoe sizes have higher reading scores.
(d) In made-up city records, cities with more police officers have more crime.
3
Pick one scenario and write one sentence that a careful reporter could print about it.
▶  Check
(a) A third variable is likely: for example sleep, or family circumstances, could affect both breakfast and test scores. A check: compare breakfast and scores among students who sleep about the same hours. (b) A third variable: the size of the fire. Bigger fires get more trucks and also cause more damage; sending trucks is not what creates the damage. Check: compare fires of about the same size. (c) A third variable: age. Older children have bigger feet and also read better. Check: compare children of the same age. (d) Two stories are plausible. City size (a third variable) means larger cities have more officers and more crimes in total; and reverse direction, since cities with more crime may hire more officers. A check: compare rates per person, and look at what happens over time. A good printed sentence uses “is associated with” or “tend to,” says the sample, and avoids “causes.” Answers may differ; accept any confounder that could plausibly push both variables and any check that holds the confounder about the same.

The Within-Group Check in Excel
25 min
1
Open a Blank workbook. Press Ctrl+S, name it Wk28_Confounder_YourName.xlsx, and save it in your OneDrive Optima → Math folder.
2
Type the headings: A1 Town, B1 Weather, C1 Cones (hundreds), D1 Pool incidents.
3
Type the data from Worked Example 1 into A2:D11: the five warm towns in rows 2 to 6 and the five hot towns in rows 7 to 11. Keep all warm towns above all hot towns.
4
In F1 type r, all ten towns. In G1 type =CORREL(C2:C11,D2:D11) and press Enter. Expect about 0.88.
5
In F2 type r, warm towns only. In G2 type =CORREL(C2:C6,D2:D6) and press Enter. This uses two separate, shorter ranges. Expect about 0.19.
6
In F3 type r, hot towns only. In G3 type =CORREL(C7:C11,D7:D11) and press Enter. Expect about −0.08.
7
Select G1:G3, and on the Home tab click Decrease Decimal until two decimals show. (If a cell shows #N/A or #DIV/0!, check that both ranges in the formula have the same number of cells.)
8
Optional: select C1:D11, open the Insert tab, and choose Scatter (markers only). Look for the two clusters.
9
In one or two sentences, answer: why is the overall r so much larger than either within-group r? Then write what the check cannot show.
▶  Check
G1 is 0.88, G2 is 0.19, G3 is −0.08. The overall r is large because the hot towns sit high on both variables and the warm towns sit low on both; the gap between the two clusters creates most of the association, while inside each cluster the points show little straight-line pattern. The check cannot show that ice cream has no effect (each group has only five towns), and it cannot rule out other confounders; it shows only that weather alone can account for the strong overall association.
3
Headline Rewrite
20 min
1
Rewrite each constructed headline in careful words. Use the four parts: who was studied, what was measured, the pattern in careful words, and what the data cannot say.
2
(a) “Study: Playing Chess Makes Kids Smarter.” (Made up: 80 children were surveyed; their families decided whether they played chess.)
(b) “New Data Proves Bigger Libraries Boost Town Incomes.” (Made up: a comparison of 40 towns.)
3
For each, name one confounder or a reverse-direction story that the original headline ignores.
4
In one sentence, say what design would let a researcher make a causal claim about chess.
▶  Check
(a) Sample rewrite: “In a made-up survey of 80 children, those who played chess tended to score higher on a thinking test; the survey cannot say whether chess is the reason.” Alternatives: families with more time and money may both enrol children in chess and support their schoolwork (confounder), or children who already like puzzles may be more drawn to chess (reverse direction). (b) Sample rewrite: “In a made-up comparison of 40 towns, those with bigger libraries tended to have higher median incomes. The comparison cannot say why.” Alternatives: richer towns may fund both larger libraries and other services, or wealthy residents may demand bigger libraries (reverse direction). Design: randomly assign children to learn chess or to an equally time-consuming activity, then compare thinking-test scores. Look for: no cause verbs, a stated sample, and an honest limit.
