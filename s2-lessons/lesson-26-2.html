✨ A WONDER TO START WITH
Picture a tall board studded with rows of pegs. You drop one ball at the top. It strikes a peg, bounces left or right, strikes another peg, bounces again, and finally tumbles into a slot at the bottom. Where will it land? Nobody can say. Drop the same ball again and it will probably land somewhere else.
Now drop a thousand balls. They do not scatter evenly. They pile up tall in the middle slots and thin out toward the edges, in a smooth, symmetric bell, and a second thousand balls builds nearly the same bell again. No ball was told where to go, and no peg knows anything about bells. Each bounce is a plain gamble, and yet the crowd of gambles obeys a shape you can predict in advance. Order out of randomness: this is the wonder of the whole module.
Today you will see why, and the answer is a friend from Semester 1. The slot a ball lands in is a binomial count, and a binomial count, given enough trials, traces a normal curve. That link lets you answer binomial questions that would take pages of arithmetic with one quick normal area. You will also learn when the shortcut can be trusted and when it cannot.
A note on honesty. Every setting and number in this lesson is constructed to teach the idea. The Galton board is a simulation: its balls are random, so your piles will differ a little from anyone else's. Judge the pattern, not any one ball.
🎲 THE PATH MODEL: ONE BALL, n CHOICES
Here is the whole idea in one sentence. A ball falling through n rows of pegs makes n small choices, left or right, and the slot it lands in is set by how many times it went right.
Look at that with Semester 1 eyes. There is a fixed number of trials (n rows). Each trial has two outcomes (left or right). The chance of going right, which we call p, is the same at every peg. And one bounce does not influence the next. Those are the conditions for a binomial setting, so:
X = number of rights = the slot number    ⇒    X ~ Binomial(n, p)
You already know the chance of any one slot, P(X = k) = C(n, k) pk(1 − p)n−k, and the two numbers that summarize a binomial count:
mean = np      standard deviation = √( np(1 − p) )
The mean says where the heap is centered: on 10 rows with a 50% chance of going right, the middle of the pile is slot 10 × 0.5 = 5. The SD says how wide the heap is. Notice that the SD has a square root, a common place to slip, and that it contains (1 − p) as well as p.
Why a bell? Each peg adds one small push, a little to the left or a little to the right, and the slot is the total of all those pushes. Very few paths lead to an edge slot (the ball would have to go right at every peg), while a great many different paths lead to the middle. Many small independent pushes added together give the same smooth, symmetric shape again and again. This is the idea de Moivre uncovered in 1733 (Week 25): as n grows, the jagged bar graph of binomial probabilities fits a normal curve more and more closely.
⚠️ WHEN THE HEAP IS NOT A BELL
The bell needs room. Two things can leave the heap lopsided:
▶ Too few rows. With only 2 or 3 rows there are only a few slots, and the pile looks like a staircase, not a curve.
▶ A chance far from 50%. Suppose the chance of going right is only 10% and there are 6 rows. Then P(X = 0) = 0.96 ≈ 0.531: over half the balls never go right at all and pile into slot 0. The heap leans against the left wall, because a count cannot go below zero. The mean is np = 0.6, and a bell centered near 0.6 would have to spill into slots that do not exist.
The cure is more rows. At a 10% chance, with enough rows the heap pulls away from the wall and rounds into a bell. The farther p is from 0.5, the more rows it takes. You will watch this happen in the board below.

📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 1: THE MEAN AND SD OF A BINOMIAL COUNT
Problem. A constructed board has n = 40 rows and a 50% chance of going right. A second constructed setting has n = 80 trials and p = 0.25. Find the mean and standard deviation of the count X in each.
Teacher's voice: I need two numbers for each: np for the center, and the square root of np(1 − p) for the spread. I will find the number under the root first, then take the root last, so I do not forget it.
▶ Setting 1. Mean = 40 × 0.5 = 20. Under the root: 40 × 0.5 × 0.5 = 10. SD = √10 ≈ 3.162.
▶ Setting 2. Mean = 80 × 0.25 = 20. Under the root: 80 × 0.25 × 0.75 = 15. SD = √15 ≈ 3.873.
▶ Compare. Same center, but setting 2 is wider (3.873 against 3.162), because it has twice as many trials adding up small pushes.
Desmos check. Type 40*0.5 and you get 20. Type sqrt(40*0.5*0.5) and you get about 3.1623. For setting 2, type 80*0.25 (20) and sqrt(80*0.25*0.75) (about 3.873). If typing sqrt does not give you a root sign on your screen, use the square-root key on the on-screen keypad and type the numbers under it.
✅ WHEN IS A NORMAL CURVE A FAIR STAND-IN?
The normal approximation replaces the jagged binomial bars with a normal curve that has the same mean and SD: X is treated as roughly N(np, √(np(1−p))). It is only a model, and like every model it fits in some cases and not in others. The question to ask is whether the heap has room to be a bell, and the check is:
both np ≥ 10    and    n(1 − p) ≥ 10
This is a common rule of thumb; some sources use 5 in place of 10. Think about what the two numbers mean: np is the typical number of “rights,” and n(1 − p) is the typical number of “lefts.” If either is small, the heap is jammed against a wall (slot 0 or slot n) and cannot be symmetric. Both must be comfortably large before a symmetric bell makes sense.
It is a screening check, not a guarantee. A pass means the approximation is usually reasonable; a fail means you should use the exact binomial formula (or Excel's BINOM.DIST) instead.
📐 THE CONTINUITY CORRECTION
There is one more idea. A binomial count is a whole number: 23, 24, 25, never 24.3. A normal curve is continuous: it assigns area to every stretch of the number line. To compare them, think of each binomial bar as centered on its whole number and one unit wide. The bar for 24 covers everything from 23.5 to 24.5.
So when you want P(X ≤ 24), you want the bars for 24, 23, 22, and so on, and the bar for 24 reaches up to 24.5. The matching normal area is the area below 24.5, not below 24. Using 24 chops the last bar in half and loses area. This half-unit nudge is the continuity correction. Here is how it translates every case:
Binomial questionNormal areaExample
P(X ≤ k)area below k + 0.5P(X ≤ 24): below 24.5
P(X < k)area below k − 0.5P(X < 24): below 23.5
P(X ≥ k)area above k − 0.5P(X ≥ 24): above 23.5
P(X > k)area above k + 0.5P(X > 24): above 24.5
P(X = k)area between k − 0.5 and k + 0.5P(X = 24): between 23.5 and 24.5
P(a ≤ X ≤ b)area between a − 0.5 and b + 0.5P(18 ≤ X ≤ 22): between 17.5 and 22.5
A way to remember it: ask which whole numbers you want included, then go half a unit past the outermost one, outward from the values you keep.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 2: ONE BINOMIAL, THREE ANSWERS
Problem. A constructed board has 40 rows and a 50% chance of going right, so X ~ Binomial(40, 0.5). Find P(X ≤ 24) exactly, then by the normal approximation without and with the continuity correction.
Teacher's voice: First I check that the approximation is allowed: np = 20 and n(1 − p) = 20, both at least 10. Good. The center is 20 and the SD is √10 ≈ 3.162 from Example 1.
▶ Exact. Adding the binomial probabilities for k = 0 through 24 (a spreadsheet does this in one cell, see the Excel steps) gives 0.9231.
▶ Normal, no correction. z = (24 − 20) ÷ 3.162 = 1.265. The area below is about 0.8970. That is 0.026 too low.
▶ Normal, with the correction. z = (24.5 − 20) ÷ 3.162 = 1.423. The area below is about 0.9226. That is within 0.0005 of the exact answer.
▶ Sense check. The mean is 20 and 24 is above it, so the probability of being at or below 24 should be well over one half. All three answers agree with that, and the corrected one is almost on the exact value.
The same trick works for a single value and for a range. For P(X = 24), the normal area between 23.5 and 24.5 is about 0.0568, and the exact value is 0.0572. For P(18 ≤ X ≤ 22), the area between 17.5 and 22.5 is about 0.5708 (z = ±0.79), and the exact value is 0.5704.
🛑 WORKED EXAMPLE 3: WHEN THE APPROXIMATION FAILS
Problem. A constructed setting has n = 10 and p = 0.1. Check whether the approximation is appropriate, then see what it does.
Teacher's voice: Before using any normal area, I run the check.
▶ The check. np = 10 × 0.1 = 1, and n(1 − p) = 9. The first number is far below 10 (and below 5 as well), so the check fails. The mean is 1 and the SD is √0.9 ≈ 0.949.
▶ The exact distribution for X = 0, 1, 2, 3 is 0.3487, 0.3874, 0.1937, 0.0574, then tiny values. It is tall at 0 and 1 and has a long tail to the right: lopsided, not symmetric.
▶ The approximation is off. P(X = 0): exact 0.3487; normal area between −0.5 and 0.5 (z = −1.58 to −0.53), about 0.2422, roughly 30% too small. P(X ≤ 1): exact 0.7361; normal area below 1.5, about 0.7009.
▶ It reaches into impossible territory. The bell centered at 1 with SD 0.949 puts area at negative values. The area below −0.5 (z = −1.58) is about 0.057, so about 5.7% of the normal curve sits where a count can never be. A count of balls cannot be negative, but the normal curve does not know that.
The lesson is not “the approximation is bad.” It is that the approximation is only as good as the heap's room to be a bell, and the check np ≥ 10 and n(1 − p) ≥ 10 is how you ask. Here you would use the exact binomial formula.
📜 A MOMENT IN THE HISTORY OF MATHEMATICS · GALTON'S BOARD
Francis Galton (1822–1911) was an English polymath, a person whose curiosity ranged over many fields. In the late 1800s he devised a pegboard in which balls fall through rows of pegs and gather in a bell-shaped heap at the bottom. The device is called the quincunx or the Galton board. Galton built it to illustrate an idea: that many small, independent chance effects, each pushing a little one way or the other, add up to a normal shape. The mathematics behind the heap was older. Abraham de Moivre had shown in 1733 (you met him in Week 25) that the binomial pattern approaches the bell curve as the number of trials grows. What the board added was something you could watch. A single ball behaves unpredictably, yet the crowd builds the same smooth shape, time after time. Every ball on the board is making exactly the left-or-right choices you studied in the binomial setting, so the board is a picture of this very lesson: a binomial count, repeated many times, drawing a normal curve. And here is a thought on wonder: nothing in the board “knows” about bells, only about left and right, and the bell appears anyway. This is a short sketch; historians add many details beyond it.
💻 EXCEL: BINOM.DIST VERSUS NORM.DIST, CLICK BY CLICK
You will compare the exact binomial answer with the normal approximation, and then prove that the cumulative function is just the sum of the single-value probabilities. Keep this page open beside Excel.
▶ Open Excel and start a Blank workbook. (This one is practice; you do not upload it. If you keep it, save it as Wk26_L2_YourName.)
▶ Click A1, type n, press Enter. Click B1, type 40, Enter. Click A2, type p, Enter. Click B2, type 0.5, Enter.
▶ Click A3, type Mean, Enter. Click B3, type =B1*B2, Enter. You should see 20. Click A4, type SD, Enter. Click B4, type =SQRT(B1*B2*(1-B2)), Enter. You should see about 3.162.
▶ The exact answer in one cell. Click A6, type Exact (BINOM.DIST), Enter. Click B6 and type =BINOM.DIST(24,B1,B2,TRUE), then Enter. You should see about 0.9231. The inputs are the largest count to include (24), the number of trials, the chance of success, and TRUE, which means “add up everything from 0 through 24.”
▶ The normal approximation with the correction. Click A7, type Normal, with 0.5, Enter. Click B7 and type =NORM.DIST(24.5,B3,B4,TRUE), Enter. You should see about 0.9226. The inputs are the cutoff 24.5, the mean, the SD, and TRUE for “area below.”
▶ Without the correction. Click A8, type Normal, no 0.5, Enter. Click B8 and type =NORM.DIST(24,B3,B4,TRUE), Enter. You should see about 0.8970. Compare the three numbers in B6, B7, B8.
▶ Prove that the cumulative version is the sum of the terms. Click D1, type k, Enter. Click E1, type P(X = k), Enter. Click D2 and type 0, Enter; click D3 and type 1, Enter. Select D2 and D3 together, then drag the small square in the bottom-right corner of the selection (the fill handle) down to row 26. The column now holds 0, 1, 2, and so on up to 24.
▶ Click E2 and type =BINOM.DIST(D2,$B$1,$B$2,FALSE), then Enter. The last argument is FALSE, which means “just this one value.” The dollar signs lock the number of trials and the chance so they do not move when you copy down. Click E2 again and drag its fill handle down to E26.
▶ Click A9, type Sum of terms, Enter. Click B9 and type =SUM(E2:E26), Enter. You should see about 0.9231, the same as B6. That equality is the point: the cumulative BINOM.DIST with TRUE is exactly the sum of the single-value terms with FALSE.
▶ Try it your way. Change B1 to 100 and B2 to 0.5, and change the 24 in B6 to 55 and the 24.5 in B7 to 55.5. (The term table in D and E is for the 40-trial case, so ignore B9 now.) Compare B6 and B7, and say in a sentence what happened to the gap between them.
Honest note. Menu names and the look of the window differ a little between desktop Excel and Excel for the web, but typing a formula into a cell works the same in both. A #NAME? error usually means a misspelled function name; a #NUM! error from BINOM.DIST usually means the count is larger than the number of trials or the chance is outside 0 to 1.
💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · np AND √(np(1−p)) IN DESMOS, AREAS IN EXCEL
Desmos Scientific does plain arithmetic, so type the formulas the way you write them. For the mean, type 40*0.5 and you get 20. For the SD, type sqrt(40*0.5*0.5) and you get about 3.1623. Build the number under the root first and wrap the whole product, so the root covers everything. For a z-score with the continuity correction, type (24.5-20)/sqrt(10) and you get about 1.423 (the subtraction goes in its own parentheses so the division applies to the whole difference). For the rule-of-thumb check, type 40*0.5 and 40*(1-0.5). If typing sqrt does not make a root sign on your screen, use the square-root key on the on-screen keypad. A common slip is to leave out the root, which gives 10 as the SD of the 40-trial board instead of 3.162.

Excel does the areas: =BINOM.DIST(24,40,0.5,TRUE) gives the exact cumulative probability, =NORM.DIST(24.5,20,SQRT(40*0.5*0.5),TRUE) gives the normal area with the correction, and =BINOM.DIST(k,n,p,FALSE) (with FALSE) gives the chance of a single count. Summing the single-count terms, as you did above, reproduces the cumulative answer exactly.
🤖 AI TRAINING · PROMPT → PREDICT → VERIFY
Prompt. Ask an AI tool: “What does a Galton board show, and why do the balls form a bell shape? For a board with 12 rows and a 50% chance of going right, what are the mean and standard deviation of the slot a ball lands in?”
Predict. Before you read its answer, write your own: what is X, what are n and p, and what do np and √(np(1−p)) give? (You should get a mean of 6 and an SD of about 1.73.) Also write down in a sentence why the heap should look like a bell.
Verify. Check its numbers with 12*0.5 (6) and sqrt(12*0.5*0.5) (about 1.7321) in Desmos. Then set the Galton board to 12 rows and a 50% chance, drop 1000 or more balls, and compare the observed mean and SD in the table with your numbers. Now judge the AI. Watch for these common errors: saying the balls “prefer” the middle, as if the board steers them (every peg is an even gamble; the middle wins only because far more paths lead there); saying that dropping more balls makes the pile wider or narrower (the spread is set by n and p; more balls only make the shape smoother and less jumpy); leaving the square root out of the SD, or using np in place of np(1−p); and claiming the normal curve is an exact match for every board, even one with few rows or a chance near 10%. Flag anything wrong, vague, or too confident, and say what you would ask next.
The verified answer, for comparison: each ball makes 12 independent left-or-right choices, so its slot is Binomial(12, 0.5), with mean 12 × 0.5 = 6 and SD √(12 × 0.5 × 0.5) = √3 ≈ 1.73. The heap forms a bell because many small independent pushes add up, and more rows make the fit closer.
