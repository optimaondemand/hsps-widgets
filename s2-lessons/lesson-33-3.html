Lesson 33.3
Confidence Intervals and Confidence Levels
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A single estimate hides how far it could be off. An interval estimate shows it, and the confidence level tells you how reliable the method is.
Interval estimates for a proportion and for a mean (with a known standard deviation); z* for 80%, 90%, 95%, and 99%; what “95% confident” really means; width versus confidence level and versus sample size; reading an interval next to a benchmark value
Reading & work: Read every section in order, work each example on paper before you read its solution, use the Confidence Interval Simulator, follow the Excel steps, and complete the three activities at the bottom. The reading, interactive, and Excel steps take about 35 minutes and the activities about 40 more, so plan on about 75 minutes in all.

1
I will be able to build an interval estimate for a population proportion, p-hat ± z* × SE, and for a population mean with a known standard deviation, x-bar ± z* × σ÷√n, using z* = 1.282, 1.645, 1.960, or 2.576 for 80%, 90%, 95%, or 99%.
2

3

4
I will be able to check the conditions for a proportion interval and read an interval next to a benchmark value (such as 50%) without overclaiming.

✨ A WONDER TO START WITH
Suppose a town board must decide whether to fund a new park. A random poll of 200 residents finds that 56% favor it. The board member who commissioned the poll says, “So a majority supports it.” Another member says, “With only 200 people, that 56% could easily be 50%.” Who is right? The honest answer is that a single number cannot settle it. What the board needs is the number and an honest statement of how far off it could be.
In the last two lessons you saw two pieces of that answer. In Sampling Distributions and the Central Limit Theorem, you saw that sample estimates pile up in a bell shape around the truth, with a spread called the standard error (SE). In Margin of Error: How Far Can a Poll Miss?, you turned that spread into a margin of error (MOE). Today you put the two pieces together into a single tool, the confidence interval, and you learn exactly what it does and does not promise.
Service, this module’s virtue, shows up here. People who make decisions for a community (a school board, a health clinic, a city council) deserve numbers that tell them how much to trust the numbers. An interval is a small act of service: it hands the decision-maker the estimate and its honest limits.
A note on honesty. Every population, percent, and sample in this lesson is constructed (made up) or simulated by a computer to teach the idea. None of it is real survey data.
🎲 FROM ONE ESTIMATE TO AN INTERVAL ESTIMATE
A point estimate is a single number from a sample, such as p-hat = 0.56. It is our best single guess, but it almost never equals the true value, because of the sampling variation you studied earlier this module. An interval estimate (also called a confidence interval) replaces the single guess with a range:
estimate ± z* × SE
The piece after the ± sign, z* × SE, is the margin of error you met in the last lesson. The number z* (read “z-star”) says how many standard errors we reach out from the estimate. It is set by the confidence level we choose. Here are the four levels you will use, from the normal model you learned in Weeks 25 and 26:
Confidence levelz* (reach out this many SEs)Excel formula that produces it
80%1.282=NORM.S.INV(0.90)
90%1.645=NORM.S.INV(0.95)
95%1.960=NORM.S.INV(0.975)
99%2.576=NORM.S.INV(0.995)
Why those inputs? A level of C leaves (1 − C) in the two tails together, so the upper cut-off has C + half of the leftover below it: (1 + C) ÷ 2. For 95% that is (1 + 0.95) ÷ 2 = 0.975, and for 99% it is 0.995.
The standard error depends on what we are estimating.
▶ Proportion: SE = √( p-hat(1 − p-hat) ÷ n ). We use p-hat because the true p is the very thing we do not know.
▶ Mean: SE = σ ÷ √n, where σ is the population standard deviation. One honest caution: in real studies nobody knows σ ahead of time, so statisticians use a slightly different method that gives a slightly wider interval. You will meet that idea later. For now, every mean problem in this course will tell you σ, which keeps the main idea clear.
Conditions to check first. The method is trustworthy when (1) the data come from a random sample (or a randomized experiment), (2) the observations are roughly independent, which a sample that is a small part of the population (a common guideline is under about 10%) supports, and (3) for a proportion, both counts are large enough that the normal model fits: n × p-hat and n × (1 − p-hat) are each at least 10 (some sources use 5). For a mean, the Central Limit Theorem from Lesson 33.1 does the work: a larger sample, and especially one from a roughly symmetric population, makes the normal model more trustworthy. A confidence interval cannot repair a biased sample. If the sample is not random, the interval can be tight and still miss badly.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 1: A PROPORTION INTERVAL
Problem. The made-up Hollow Creek town board polls a random sample of n = 200 residents. Of them, y = 112 favor the new park. Build a 95% confidence interval for the proportion of all residents who favor it.
Teacher’s voice: Step 1, the estimate. p-hat = 112 ÷ 200 = 0.56. Step 2, check the conditions. The sample is random (given), and 200 is a small part of the town. n × p-hat = 112 and n × (1 − p-hat) = 88 are both at least 10, so we may proceed. Step 3, the standard error. SE = √(0.56 × 0.44 ÷ 200) = √(0.2464 ÷ 200) = √0.001232 = 0.0351. Step 4, the margin of error. For 95%, z* = 1.960, so the margin is 1.960 × 0.0351 = 0.0688. Step 5, the interval. 0.56 − 0.0688 = 0.491 and 0.56 + 0.0688 = 0.629.
Say it in a sentence. “We are 95% confident that the true proportion of Hollow Creek residents who favor the park is between 0.491 and 0.629, or about 49.1% to 62.9%.” Notice that the interval runs from just below 50% to well above it. The section on benchmarks below returns to what that means. (A sentence that begins “We are 95% confident” is shorthand. The next section says exactly what the shorthand stands for.)
💭 WHAT “95% CONFIDENT” REALLY MEANS
Here is the idea that most people get wrong, so read it slowly. The true proportion is one fixed number. It does not wiggle. What wiggles is our interval, because each random sample gives a different p-hat and so a different interval. Some intervals land on the truth and some miss it.
The confidence level describes the method, not any one interval: if we built intervals this way again and again from fresh random samples, about 95 out of every 100 of them would capture the true value. The interval we actually have either did capture it or did not, and we cannot tell which. The 95% is our confidence in the procedure.
Three common misreadings (the interactive below prints these too, so you can compare):
▶ Misreading 1. “There is a 95% chance the true value is inside this interval.” Once the interval is built, the truth is either in it or not. The 95% belongs to the method.
▶ Misreading 2. “95% of individual values fall inside the interval.” The interval is about the population mean or proportion, not about where individual people or plants fall. Individual values spread out far more than an average does.
▶ Misreading 3. “The next sample’s estimate will land inside this interval 95% of the time.” The next sample has its own p-hat, centered on the truth, not on our particular interval.
The best way to believe this is to watch it happen. Use the interactive next.

📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 2: SAME SAMPLE, FOUR CONFIDENCE LEVELS
Problem. Use the Hollow Creek sample again (112 of 200, p-hat = 0.56, SE = 0.0351). Build intervals at 80%, 90%, 95%, and 99%, and describe what happens to the width as the level rises.
Teacher’s voice: Only z* changes. The margin at each level is z* × 0.0351, and the interval is 0.56 ± margin.
Levelz*Margin (z* × SE)LowerUpperWidth (2 × margin)
80%1.2820.04500.5150.6050.0900
90%1.6450.05770.5020.6180.1155
95%1.9600.06880.4910.6290.1376
99%2.5760.09040.4700.6500.1808
Every step up in confidence makes the interval wider: from about 0.090 wide at 80% to about 0.181 wide at 99%. This is the price of confidence. To be more sure your method catches the truth, you must cast a wider net, and a wider net says less. A 100% interval would have to be 0 to 1, which tells the board nothing. Choosing a level is a judgment about how costly a miss would be.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 3: FOUR TIMES THE PEOPLE, HALF THE WIDTH
Problem. The board funds a bigger poll: n = 800, and the same share, 448 of 800, favor the park (p-hat = 0.56 again). Build a 95% interval and compare its width with the n = 200 interval.
Teacher’s voice: SE = √(0.56 × 0.44 ÷ 800) = √0.000308 = 0.01755. The margin is 1.960 × 0.01755 = 0.0344. The interval is 0.56 − 0.0344 = 0.526 to 0.56 + 0.0344 = 0.594. The width is 0.0688, exactly half of the n = 200 width of 0.1376. Because SE has √n in the denominator, multiplying n by 4 divides SE (and so the width) by √4 = 2. This is the same “four times the people, half the margin” pattern you saw with the margin of error.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 4: A MEAN INTERVAL WITH A KNOWN STANDARD DEVIATION
Problem. A made-up gardener measures n = 36 randomly chosen bean plants in a large greenhouse after six weeks. The sample mean height is x-bar = 31.4 cm. Suppose the population standard deviation is known to be σ = 6 cm (made up). Build a 95% interval for the mean height of all the plants.
Teacher’s voice: Standard error. SE = σ ÷ √n = 6 ÷ √36 = 6 ÷ 6 = 1.0 cm. Margin. 1.960 × 1.0 = 1.96 cm. Interval. 31.4 − 1.96 = 29.44 and 31.4 + 1.96 = 33.36. We are 95% confident that the mean height of all the plants is between about 29.4 cm and 33.4 cm. At 90%, z* = 1.645 and the interval is 29.76 to 33.04 cm, a little narrower.
Two reminders. The interval is for the mean of all plants, not for individual plants, which vary much more (a single plant could easily be 25 cm or 38 cm). And in a real greenhouse you would not know σ; you would estimate it from the sample and use the slightly wider method mentioned earlier.
💭 READING AN INTERVAL NEXT TO A BENCHMARK
Often there is a benchmark value in the conversation: “Is half the town in favor?” or “The seed packet says the average is 30 cm.” An interval helps you compare, but it takes care.
Look back at Worked Example 1. The 95% interval for the park is 0.491 to 0.629, and 0.50 is inside it. What can we say? The values in the interval are the ones the data leave plausible, so a true share of 50% is plausible: with 200 people we cannot rule it out. What can we not say? We cannot say the share is 50%, and we should not say the data “accept” 50%; every value in the interval, from 0.491 to 0.629, is just as consistent with the poll. We also should not announce “a majority supports the park,” because values below 0.50 are plausible too.
Now look at Worked Example 3. With 800 people the 95% interval is 0.526 to 0.594, and 0.50 is outside it. Now the data point more clearly to a share above half. (Even then, the correct sentence is “we are 95% confident the true share is between 0.526 and 0.594,” not “we have proved a majority.”) The very same p-hat of 0.56 told a much weaker story at n = 200 than at n = 800. A bigger sample did not change the estimate; it narrowed the plausible range.
The level matters too. At 90% the interval from n = 200 is 0.502 to 0.618, which just barely leaves out 0.50, while the 95% interval includes it. That is a warning not to treat the edge of an interval as a bright line. For now, simply state what the interval shows and what it does not.
Keep in mind (from Weeks 30 and 31). An interval only accounts for the chance variation of random sampling. It does not repair bias, nonresponse, or bad question wording. A narrow interval from a biased sample is precisely wrong.

💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · INTERVAL ENDPOINTS IN EXCEL; STANDARD ERROR AND MARGIN IN DESMOS
Excel. =NORM.S.INV((1+C)/2) gives z* for any confidence level C (for example, =NORM.S.INV(0.975) gives 1.96). Then =z*SE is the margin and =p-m and =p+m are the interval endpoints. Fill the same row formulas down to repeat for several levels. For a mean with a known σ, use =sigma/SQRT(n) as the standard error.
Desmos Scientific handles plain arithmetic only here (it has no z* lookup, so take z* from the table above or from Excel). For the standard error in Worked Example 1, type 0.56 × 0.44 ÷ 200 and then use the square-root button on the result; you should see about 0.0351. Multiply by 1.96 for the margin (about 0.0688), then subtract and add it to 0.56 for the endpoints.
🤖 AI TRAINING · ASK AN AI WHAT “95% CONFIDENT” MEANS; GRADE IT AGAINST THE CORRECT READING
Prompt. Ask an AI tool: “A poll reports a 95% confidence interval of 0.49 to 0.63 for the share of voters who favor a plan. What does 95% confident mean? Explain in plain language.”
Predict. Before you read its answer, decide what a careful answer must say: the 95% describes the method (about 95 of 100 intervals built this way would capture the true value); this particular interval either did or did not; it is not a 95% probability that the truth lies in this interval; it is not about individual voters; and it does not cover bias or nonresponse.
Verify. Read the AI’s answer sentence by sentence. AI tools very often say “there is a 95% probability the true value is in this interval,” which is Misreading 1. Did it? Then test its claim yourself: in the Confidence Interval Simulator draw 100 intervals at 95% and count how many missed, then press Hide the truth and see that you cannot tell which ones they were. Did the AI say anything that is wrong or hand-wavy? Rewrite its worst sentence so that it is true. Verdict. A fluent answer is not a correct answer; the simulation is the referee.

1
Predict, Run, Record
15 min
1
Use the Confidence Interval Simulator, Proportion tab, walkers scenario, n about 400. Predict first: out of 100 intervals, about how many will miss the true value at the 80% level, at the 95% level, and at the 99% level? Write your three predictions in a table with a blank “actual” column.
2
Run Draw 100 intervals at each level (press Clear between runs) and record the number of misses (the number of dashed intervals, or 100 minus the captured count). Do each level twice if you can.
3
Reflect. (a) Were your actual counts close to your predictions? Were they exactly equal? Why not? (b) You saw that the 99% intervals are wider than the 80% intervals. Explain, using your miss counts, why the extra width is worth it for some decisions and not for others. (c) At 95%, you drew 100 intervals and counted 8 misses. Does that mean the method is broken? Explain.
▶  Check
1. About 20 misses at 80%, about 5 at 95%, about 1 at 99%. 2. Your counts will differ each run, because the samples are random. Most runs of 100 intervals land at roughly 12 to 28 misses at 80%, roughly 1 to 10 at 95%, and roughly 0 to 3 at 99% (these ranges are the middle 95% of a binomial count, computed by script), so exact matches are rare but counts near the prediction are normal. 3. (a) Counts near the prediction, not exact; sampling variation again. (b) A higher level means fewer misses (the method catches the truth more often) but wider, less specific intervals; if a miss would be costly (for example a safety decision) a higher level can be worth it, and if you need a sharp estimate and can tolerate more misses, a lower level may do. (c) No. For a 95% method, 8 misses in 100 is well inside the usual wiggle (anything from about 1 to 10 is ordinary). The method is not broken; the count is just a random sample of intervals.

Reading or Misreading?
10 min
1
Here are six statements about confidence intervals. For each one, write C if it is a correct reading, or write M and name the misreading (1: a probability that this interval holds the truth; 2: a claim about individual values; 3: a claim about the next sample’s estimate; or O: some other overclaim).
(a) “If we built intervals this way again and again from fresh random samples, about 95 in 100 would capture the true proportion.”
(b) “There is a 95% chance the true proportion of residents who favor the park is between 0.491 and 0.629.”
(c) “The 95% interval for mean bean-plant height is 29.44 to 33.36 cm, so 95% of the individual plants are between 29.44 and 33.36 cm tall.”
(d) “If we drew another random sample of 200 residents, its p-hat would land between 0.491 and 0.629 about 95% of the time.”
(e) “The method that produced 0.491 to 0.629 captures the true value about 95% of the time. We do not know whether this one did.”
(f) “Because the interval 0.491 to 0.629 contains 0.50, the data show that exactly 50% of residents favor the park.”
2
Take one statement that you marked M and rewrite it into a correct sentence about the same interval.
▶  Check
1. (a) C. (b) M, Misreading 1: the truth is one fixed number; the 95% belongs to the method, not to a probability for this interval. (c) M, Misreading 2: the interval is for the mean of all plants, and individual plants vary much more (with σ = 6 cm, individual heights spread far wider than 3.9 cm). (d) M, Misreading 3: future estimates are centered on the true value, not on this particular interval, and the share of future estimates inside it need not be 95%. (e) C. (f) M, an overclaim (O): 0.50 is only one of many plausible values; the data cannot rule it out, but they do not show it is exact. 2. Example rewrite of (b): “We used a method that captures the true proportion about 95% of the time, and it gave 0.491 to 0.629. ▶  Check
1. p-hat = 135 ÷ 300 = 0.45. Conditions: random sample; 135 and 165 are both at least 10; 300 is a small part of the school (assumed).
