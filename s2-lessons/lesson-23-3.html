📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Excel will do the arithmetic in a second — and the judging is still yours.
A spreadsheet lab: a full summary of two groups, a check by hand, a predict-then-check, and an honest four-sentence description
Reading & work: Keep this page open beside Excel. Work through Parts A to G in order, build the workbook, and upload it to Canvas. Plan on about 65 minutes.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to build a summary table in Excel from typed formulas (COUNT, SUM, AVERAGE, MEDIAN, MIN, MAX, QUARTILE.INC, STDEV.S, STDEV.P) using absolute and mixed references and fill.
2
I will be able to choose STDEV.S or STDEV.P for a given situation and check a standard deviation by hand and in Excel.
3
I will be able to predict which statistics change when one value changes, and test the prediction.
4
I will be able to describe a distribution in four sentences (shape, center, spread, unusual) in context, choosing median and IQR or mean and standard deviation and saying why.

💻 THE LAB
Today's lesson is a lab. Over the last three lessons you have measured center and spread by hand. Today a spreadsheet does the arithmetic and you do the judging. You will build a summary of two made-up groups of students, check the spreadsheet against a small hand computation, predict what a typing mistake would do before you look, and then write a four-sentence description of the data. You finish with one workbook, which you upload to the Canvas assignment. Excel work happens outside Canvas: keep this page open beside Excel and follow the steps.
A summary number is a courtesy or a discourtesy to the data and to the reader. The spreadsheet will produce fifteen numbers in a few seconds. Whether they are fair to the data — whether the reader learns what the data are really like — is not something Excel can decide.
📝 THE DATA — CONSTRUCTED, NOT REAL
A made-up school asked students how many minutes of homework they did last night. Group A is 20 students in one grade; Group B is 10 students in a smaller class. These numbers were constructed for this lab; they are not from a real survey. Treat each group as a sample from a larger group of students. That matters when we choose between STDEV.S and STDEV.P.

About the dollar signs. In Data!A$2:A$21 the $ in front of the row numbers locks the rows: when we copy the formula to the right in Part C, the rows stay 2 to 21, but the column letter is allowed to move from A to B. The formulas in B10, B13, B14, B15, and B18 use other Summary cells, so they update by themselves if the data change.

Reading the B-minus-A column. A difference of two counts or two sums is not a comparison, because the groups have different sizes (20 and 10). Ignore the Count, Sum, Minimum, and Maximum rows when you compare the groups, and focus on center and spread: the mean, the median, the IQR, and the standard deviations. A positive number means Group B is larger on that row; a negative number means Group A is larger.

▶ Click C2 and drag its fill handle down to C21. Look down column C: exactly one cell, C21 (next to the 150), should say outlier. (Alternative: select C2:C21 and press Ctrl + D to fill down.)
▶ Click D2 and type exactly: =IF(OR(B2<Summary!$C$14,B2>Summary!$C$15),"outlier","") (it uses the Group B fences, $C$14 and $C$15). Fill it down to D11 only. All of column D should stay blank: Group B has no flagged values.
▶ Click the Summary tab, click B19, and type =COUNTIF(Data!C$2:C$21,"outlier"). Drag the fill handle right to C19 (it becomes =COUNTIF(Data!D$2:D$21,"outlier")), and type =C19-B19 in D19. You should see 1, 0, and −1.
📐 WHICH STANDARD DEVIATION: STDEV.S OR STDEV.P?
Both functions measure the typical distance from the mean: they square each deviation, add, divide, and take the square root. They differ only in the divisor. \(\text{STDEV.P} = \sqrt{\dfrac{\sum (x-\mu)^2}{n}}\) divides by \(n\); \(\text{STDEV.S} = \sqrt{\dfrac{\sum (x-\bar{x})^2}{n-1}}\) divides by \(n-1\).
▶ Use STDEV.P when your data are the whole group you care about: all 20 students in a classroom if you only want to describe that classroom.
▶ Use STDEV.S when your data are a sample from a bigger group and you want to say something about that bigger group. Course default: when the data are a sample, use STDEV.S.
Why \(n-1\)? The sample mean \(\bar{x}\) is computed from the same values we are measuring, so it sits a little nearer to them than the true center of the whole group would. Squared distances to \(\bar{x}\) therefore tend to run small. Dividing by \(n-1\) instead of \(n\) makes the answer a little larger and corrects for that, on average. It is a correction with a reason, not a rule to memorize. (The proof is for a later course.) For big samples it hardly matters: Group A has 20 values and the two functions give 26.70 and 26.02; for five values the gap is larger, as you will see in the AI Training task below.
✍️ PART E — CHECK EXCEL AGAINST A HAND COMPUTATION
Never trust a formula you have not checked once. Take the first five values of Group A (Data!A2:A6): 45, 60, 30, 50, 65.
▶ By hand, in your notebook. The mean is \(\dfrac{45+60+30+50+65}{5} = 50\). Write each deviation \(x - 50\): \(-5, 10, -20, 0, 15\). Square them: \(25, 100, 400, 0, 225\). Add: 750.
▶ In Desmos Scientific, once, to see inside the formula. Type sqrt((25+100+400+0+225)/4) → 13.69 (sample, divide by \(n-1 = 4\)). Then type sqrt((25+100+400+0+225)/5) → 12.25 (population, divide by \(n = 5\)). Desmos is doing plain arithmetic here; the list work is yours.
▶ In Excel. On the Summary sheet type Check: first five of Group A in F3. In F4 type Mean and in G4 =AVERAGE(Data!A2:A6). In F5 type STDEV.S and in G5 =STDEV.S(Data!A2:A6). In F6 type STDEV.P and in G6 =STDEV.P(Data!A2:A6). Format G4:G6 with two decimal places.
▶ Compare. Excel should say 50.00, 13.69, and 12.25. They match the hand work, so you now know that STDEV.S really does divide by \(n-1\) and STDEV.P by \(n\).
If Excel and your hand work disagree, one of them has a slip: check the squares first, then the divisor, then the cell range.
🧠 PART F — PREDICT, THEN CHECK, THEN UNDO
Imagine a data-entry slip: someone types 1500 instead of 150 in A21 — a stray extra zero. Before you touch the workbook, look at the Group A column of the Summary and decide which of the 15 statistics (and the flag count) should change and which should stay the same. Write your prediction in your notebook. Think about which statistics are built from sizes and which are built from positions.
▶ Now go to the Data sheet, click A21, type 1500, and press Enter. Look at the Summary sheet. Which numbers moved? Which did not?
▶ Record the new values for Sum, Mean, Maximum, Range, sample SD, population SD, and mean minus median, and the old ones beside them.
▶ Undo. Go back to A21, type 150, and press Enter (or press Ctrl + Z immediately). Confirm that the Summary matches your Part C check again before you go on. Your saved workbook must have 150 in A21.
What you should find. Sum, mean, maximum, range, both standard deviations, and the mean-minus-median gap all change a great deal. Count, median, minimum, Q1, Q3, IQR, both fences, and the number flagged stay exactly the same. That is the lesson of Week 22 returning: quartiles count positions, so a bigger top value does not move them; sums and squared distances listen to sizes, so they shout. A standard deviation is not resistant.
📝 PART G — THE NOTES SHEET: DESCRIBE THE DATA IN FOUR SENTENCES
Click the Notes tab. In A1 type Notes: Wk23 Lab. Then build this layout (type the labels in column A, your sentences in column B; widen column B and use Wrap Text on the Home tab so you can read them):
CellWhat goes there
A3 / B3Shape: one sentence on the shape of Group A (skewed right, symmetric, or other) and the evidence from the Summary (the mean-minus-median gap and the distance from the median to each end).
A4 / B4Center: one sentence giving the center you chose, with its value and units, and why that center and not the other.
A5 / B5Spread: one sentence giving the matching spread (IQR, or the standard deviation you pair with the mean), with its value and units, and why.
A6 / B6Unusual: one sentence naming any flagged value (the rule and the fence it passed) and what you would do before reporting it.

A13 / B13Hand check (Part E): the sum of squared deviations (750), and the sample and population SD you got by hand and in Excel.
Choosing the pair. The mean and standard deviation go together (both use every value and both are pulled by extreme values); the median and IQR go together (both are built from positions and resist extreme values). Use median and IQR when the data are skewed or have flagged outliers, and mean and standard deviation when the data are roughly symmetric with no flagged outliers. Say which you chose and why. Write each sentence in context: minutes of homework, students, last night, constructed data — not just “the median is 50.”
The courtesy question. Group A and Group B have almost the same mean (55.50 and 56.00). If that were all you reported, a reader would think the groups look alike. They do not: the medians are 50 and 56, and the IQRs are 21.25 and 7. A fair summary tells the reader what the single number hides. Then save the workbook and upload the .xlsx file to the Canvas assignment. Do not paste screenshots in its place; the teacher needs the live workbook to see the formulas.

🤖 AI TRAINING · PROMPT → PREDICT → VERIFY
Prompt. Ask an AI tool: “What is the difference between STDEV.P and STDEV.S in Excel, and when do I use each?” Then give it the five made-up values 2, 4, 4, 6, 9 and ask it for both results.
Predict. Predict before you read: which function will give the larger number on the same five values? Which divides by \(n\) and which by \(n-1\)? Does the AI say which one to use for a sample?
Verify. Check by hand: the mean is 5; the deviations are \(-3, -1, -1, 1, 4\); their squares \(9, 1, 1, 1, 16\) add to 28. STDEV.S \(= \sqrt{28/4} = \sqrt{7} \approx 2.65\); STDEV.P \(= \sqrt{28/5} = \sqrt{5.6} \approx 2.37\). Check them in Excel: type the five values in A1:A5 and type =STDEV.S(A1:A5) and =STDEV.P(A1:A5) in two other cells. Then grade the AI's claims: (1) .P divides by \(n\) and .S by \(n-1\); (2) .S is the bigger one on the same data (here by the factor \(\sqrt{5/4} \approx 1.118\)); (3) use .S for a sample from a larger group and .P for the whole group. If the AI reversed the two, or gave results that do not match your hand and Excel values, it is wrong, and now you can prove it.

▶  Sample answer (yours will differ in wording)
Shape: Group A is skewed right: most of the 20 students did between 40 and about 60 minutes of homework, but the high end stretches out to 150, and the mean (55.50 minutes) sits above the median (50 minutes). Center: The median, 50 minutes, is the better center, because the skew and the 150 pull the mean up and the median does not move. Spread: The IQR, 21.25 minutes, describes the middle half of the students, and I use it with the median because the sample standard deviation (26.70 minutes) is inflated by the one very large value.
