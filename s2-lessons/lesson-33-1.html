Lesson 33.1
Sampling Distributions and the Central Limit Theorem
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Averages of many samples pile up in a bell shape, even when the individual values do not, and the pile has a spread we can calculate.
Sampling distribution; parent, sample, and sampling distribution; standard error (σ ÷ √n for a mean, √(p(1 − p) ÷ n) for a proportion); the Central Limit Theorem; probabilities for a sample mean or sample proportion
Reading & work: Read every section in order, work each example on paper before you read its solution, use the Sampling Distribution Explorer, follow the Excel steps, and complete the three activities at the bottom. Plan on about 75 minutes: roughly 15 for the reading and examples, 12 for the explorer, 8 for Excel, and 38 for the activities.

1
I will be able to tell apart three different piles of numbers: the parent distribution (every individual value), the distribution of one sample, and the sampling distribution of a statistic across many samples.
2

✨ A WONDER TO START WITH
A made-up community group wants to know how long people wait for the city bus. They cannot ask everyone, so they ask 30 riders and find an average wait of 11.2 minutes. A second group asks 30 different riders and finds 8.9 minutes. Both groups worked honestly, and both numbers are real averages of real riders (in our story, which is constructed). So which group is right?
Neither is wrong, and neither is exactly right. Each average is one draw from a whole pile of averages that different groups could have gotten. In Week 31 you saw that such piles have a center, a spread, and a shape. Today we find out what decides all three. The surprise is this: waiting times are lopsided, with many short waits and a few very long ones, yet the pile of averages is a smooth, symmetric bell. Order comes out of lopsided randomness.
Service, this module’s virtue, looks like this: when you hand a community a number, you also owe them an honest account of how far a number like it could have strayed. The tool that gives you that account is the sampling distribution, and it is the foundation of everything else in this module.
A note on honesty. Every population, average, and sample in this lesson is constructed (made up) or simulated by a computer to teach the idea. None of it is real data.
📊 THREE PILES OF NUMBERS: DO NOT MIX THEM UP
Almost every mistake in this topic comes from mixing up three different piles. Each pile is a distribution, but each is made of different things.

💭 CENTER, SPREAD, AND SHAPE OF A SAMPLING DISTRIBUTION
Read the numbers in the caption above. The parent has an SD of about 10 minutes, but the 1,000 sample means have an SD of only about 1.8. The pile of averages is far narrower than the pile of individuals. That narrowing is not an accident, and it has a formula.
A sampling distribution is the distribution of a statistic (a sample mean, a sample proportion) over all the random samples of the same size that we could draw from the same population. We describe it the way we describe any distribution.
▶ Center. The sampling distribution of the sample mean is centered at the population mean μ. Fair random samples do not lean high or low, so over many samples the highs and lows balance. For a sample proportion, the center is the true proportion p.
▶ Spread. The spread of a sampling distribution has its own name: the standard error (SE). It is the standard deviation of the pile of sample statistics, and it answers “how far does a statistic like this typically land from the center?” (In Week 31 you called this the typical swing; standard error is the formal name.) For a sample mean, SE = σ ÷ √n, where σ is the population standard deviation. For a sample proportion, SE = √(p(1 − p) ÷ n). You already met the second formula in Week 31, in the 0.0980, 0.0490, 0.0245 pattern for p = 0.40.
▶ Shape. This is where the Central Limit Theorem comes in, in the next section.
Two ideas that sound alike but are different. The law of large numbers says that as a single sample gets larger, its mean settles closer to μ. The Central Limit Theorem describes the whole pile of sample means for a fixed n: how they are scattered around μ and what shape they make. The two fit together. Because SE = σ ÷ √n shrinks as n grows, the pile crowds closer to μ (in the spirit of the law of large numbers), and the pile has a bell shape (the Central Limit Theorem).
🎲 THE CENTRAL LIMIT THEOREM, IN WORDS
The Central Limit Theorem (CLT). Take random samples of size n from a population with mean μ and standard deviation σ. When n is large enough, the sample means pile up in a distribution that is approximately normal, with mean μ and standard deviation σ ÷ √n. This holds whatever the shape of the population.
Look at what each part of that sentence does. “Approximately normal” is the shape. “Mean μ” is the center. “σ ÷ √n” is the spread. “Whatever the shape of the population” is the surprise.
How large is “large enough”? There is no single magic number. A common rule of thumb is that n of about 30 is enough when the population is only moderately skewed. A population with a heavier tail or more extreme skew needs a bigger n, and a population that is already bell-shaped gives bell-shaped sample means at any n. For a sample proportion, you met the matching check in Week 26: the normal model works well when np and n(1 − p) are both at least 10 (some sources use 5).
Conditions. The samples must be random, and the individual observations must be independent. A common guideline for independence when sampling without replacement is that the sample should be a small part of the population (many sources say less than about 10%).
Look back to Week 25. De Moivre’s bell curve for coin flips was a special case: a count of heads is a sum of many 0s and 1s. The CLT says the same bell appears for sums and averages of many other kinds of values too.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 1: HOW MUCH NARROWER IS THE PILE OF AVERAGES?
Problem. The Sampling Distribution Explorer (below) has a made-up, strongly right-skewed population of waiting times with mean about 9.73 and standard deviation σ about 9.47. Use SE = σ ÷ √n to predict the standard error at n = 1, 5, 30, and 100. Then compare with how lopsided the pile of means still is at each n.
Teacher’s voice: At n = 1 each “sample” is a single person, so the pile of means is just the parent: SE = 9.47 ÷ 1 = 9.47. At n = 5: √5 ≈ 2.236, so SE = 9.47 ÷ 2.236 ≈ 4.24. At n = 30: √30 ≈ 5.477, so SE ≈ 1.73. At n = 100: √100 = 10, so SE = 0.95. The table’s last column uses skew, a number that measures lopsidedness: 0 means symmetric, and a positive number means a long right tail. The skew values come from checking 6,000 simulated sample means at each n.

Answer. Going from n = 1 to n = 100, the standard error falls from 9.47 to 0.95, one tenth as large, because √100 = 10. At the same time the skew falls from about 1.63 to about 0.19. 📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 2: THE PROBABILITY THAT A SAMPLE MEAN LANDS IN A RANGE
Problem. A made-up daily puzzle takes players a mean of μ = 42 minutes with a standard deviation σ = 9 minutes. The times are moderately skewed. A club picks a random sample of n = 36 players. What is the probability that the sample mean is between 40 and 45 minutes?
Teacher’s voice: Step 1, check the conditions. The sample is random, and n = 36 is at least 30 for a population that is only moderately skewed, so the CLT says the sample means are approximately normal. Step 2, find the center and spread of the sampling distribution. Center: μ = 42. Standard error: SE = 9 ÷ √36 = 9 ÷ 6 = 1.5 minutes. Step 3, standardize. For 45: z = (45 − 42) ÷ 1.5 = 2. For 40: z = (40 − 42) ÷ 1.5 = −1.33. Step 4, find the area between them. In Excel: =NORM.DIST(45,42,1.5,TRUE)-NORM.DIST(40,42,1.5,TRUE). The two areas are 0.9772 and 0.0912, and the difference is 0.8860.
Answer. About 88.6%. In plain words: if the club kept drawing random samples of 36 players, about 89 in 100 of the sample means would land between 40 and 45 minutes. Notice what this does not say: it is not a claim about individual players. A single player’s time can easily be 55 minutes.
A common error. If you used 9 (the standard deviation of individuals) as the spread instead of 1.5, the same Excel formula would give 0.2185, about 22%. That number answers a different question (for a bell-shaped population, the chance that one player is between 40 and 45) and is far too small for a sample mean. The sampling distribution is narrower than the parent. Always divide by √n.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 3: THE SAME IDEA FOR A SAMPLE PROPORTION
Problem. In a made-up school, 62% of students ride the bus (so p = 0.62). A random sample of n = 150 students is chosen. What is the probability that the sample proportion who ride the bus is less than 0.55?
Teacher’s voice: Check the condition. np = 150 × 0.62 = 93 and n(1 − p) = 150 × 0.38 = 57, both at least 10, so the sampling distribution of p-hat is approximately normal. Center: 0.62. Spread: SE = √(0.62 × 0.38 ÷ 150) = √0.001571 ≈ 0.0396. Standardize: z = (0.55 − 0.62) ÷ 0.0396 ≈ -1.77. Find the area in Excel: =NORM.DIST(0.55,0.62,SQRT(0.62*0.38/150),TRUE), which gives 0.0387.
Answer. About 3.9%, roughly 1 chance in 26. A sample of 150 from this school would rarely show a bus share as low as 55% if the true share is 62%. If one did, that would be a hint to look again at the claim that 62% ride the bus. (Reasoning like this is the seed of ideas you will keep building in this module.)
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 4: FOUR TIMES AS MANY, HALF THE STANDARD ERROR
Problem. In Worked Example 2 the club used n = 36. Suppose it uses n = 144 instead. Find the new standard error, and find the probability that the sample mean lands within 1 minute of μ = 42 (between 41 and 43) at each sample size.
Teacher’s voice: Since 144 = 4 × 36 and √144 = 12, SE = 9 ÷ 12 = 0.75, exactly half of 1.5. Now the probability. With n = 36 and SE 1.5: =NORM.DIST(43,42,1.5,TRUE)-NORM.DIST(41,42,1.5,TRUE) gives 0.4950. With n = 144 and SE 0.75: =NORM.DIST(43,42,0.75,TRUE)-NORM.DIST(41,42,0.75,TRUE) gives 0.8176.
Answer. Quadrupling the sample size halves the standard error (1.5 to 0.75). The chance that a sample mean is within 1 minute of the truth rises from about 50% to about 82%. Bigger samples buy precision, but with diminishing returns: to cut the standard error to a third you would need nine times as many players. The next lesson turns this square-root pattern into a poll’s margin of error.
⚠️ THREE COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS
“The CLT says my data become normal when I take a big sample.” No. A large sample of lopsided waiting times is still lopsided; its histogram looks like the parent. It is the sample means, collected over many samples, that become bell-shaped.
“Once n = 30, the means are always normal.” No. Thirty is a rule of thumb for moderately skewed populations. In the explorer’s heavy-tail population the means at n = 30 still show a noticeable right lean (a skew of about 0.53 in our check), and at small n the skewed populations are far from bell-shaped.
“The standard error is just the standard deviation.” No. The standard deviation σ describes how individuals vary. The standard error σ ÷ √n describes how sample means vary. They are different piles, and the second is smaller.
📜 A MOMENT IN THE HISTORY OF MATHEMATICS · FROM DE MOIVRE TO LAPLACE: THE LONG ROAD TO THE CLT
You met Abraham de Moivre in Week 25. In the 1730s he found that the pattern of heads in many coin flips (the binomial pattern) is well approximated by a bell-shaped curve. That was a result about one special chance process, and it raised an obvious question: is the bell shape a quirk of coins, or something deeper? Pierre-Simon Laplace, a French mathematician, took the idea much further. In work usually dated to around 1810, he is generally credited with the first general central-limit result, showing that the bell shape appears for sums and averages of many chance effects, not only coin flips. Historians usually add that the arguments of that era were not as careful as modern mathematics demands. The name “central limit theorem” and the polished modern proofs came later, in the twentieth century, and many mathematicians contributed along the way. What took generations of pencil-and-paper work you can now watch in seconds: when you press Draw 1000 in the explorer, the computer does the averaging for you, and the bell appears. And here is a thought on wonder: nothing in the population “knows” about bells, yet averaging pulls a bell out of almost any shape. This is a short sketch; historians would add many names and details.

The TRUE asks for the area to the left of the number, and the third item, B5, is the standard error, not the population SD.
▶ Subtract. In A8 type P(40 to 45). In B8 type =B6-B7 and press Enter.
▶ Show the answers as percents. Select B6:B8. On the Home tab, in the Number group, click the % (Percent Style) button, and then click Increase Decimal once or twice.
▶ Change the sample size. Click B4, type 144, and press Enter. The standard error should drop to 0.75 and B8 should change to about 99.6%. Type 36 to put it back.
▶ A proportion. On the same sheet, type in D1 Proportion version. In D2 type True p and in E2 type 0.62. In D3 type n and in E3 type 150. In D4 type Standard error and in E4 type =SQRT(E2*(1-E2)/E3). In D5 type P(p-hat below 0.55) and in E5 type =NORM.DIST(0.55,E2,E4,TRUE). In D6 type n times p and in E6 type =E3*E2. In D7 type n times (1-p) and in E7 type =E3*(1-E2).
Check yourself. No random draws are involved, so everyone’s numbers should match. B5 = 1.5; B6 ≈ 0.9772; B7 ≈ 0.0912; B8 ≈ 0.8860 (88.6%). With n = 144, B8 ≈ 0.9961. For the proportion: E4 ≈ 0.0396; E5 ≈ 0.0387; E6 = 93; E7 = 57 (both at least 10, so the normal model is reasonable). If your answers differ, the usual culprits are using B3 (the SD) instead of B5 (the standard error) inside NORM.DIST, or leaving out TRUE.
💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · STANDARD ERROR AND A SAMPLE-MEAN Z-SCORE IN DESMOS; SAMPLE-MEAN AND SAMPLE-PROPORTION AREAS IN EXCEL
Desmos Scientific is plain arithmetic here. For the standard error of a mean, find the square root of n first with the square-root button (for example, √36 = 6), then divide: 9 ÷ 6 = 1.5. For the z-score of a sample mean, use the standard error as the divisor: type (45-42)/1.5 and you should get 2. Compare with the wrong version (45-42)/9, which gives 0.33. For a proportion, work inside the root: 0.62 × 0.38 ÷ 150, then take the square root of that result. Desmos Scientific does not give normal areas in this course, so we use Excel for those.
Excel. =NORM.DIST(x, mean, sigma/SQRT(n), TRUE) gives the area to the left of x for a sample mean. For a sample proportion use =NORM.DIST(x, p, SQRT(p*(1-p)/n), TRUE). For the area between two values, subtract one result from the other. The two traps: the third item is the standard error, not σ, and the last item must be TRUE.

Predict. Before you read its answer, decide what a careful answer must say: it is the sample means (not the data) that become approximately normal; the center stays at μ; the spread is σ ÷ √n; and 30 is a rule of thumb for moderate skew, not a guarantee.

Did it mix up the standard deviation with the standard error (wrong)? Did it give the spread as σ ÷ √n (right)? Rewrite its worst sentence so that it is true. Verdict. A confident tone is not evidence; the simulation is.
