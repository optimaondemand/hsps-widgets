Lesson 31.3
Is the Model Consistent with the Data?
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Someone says a coin, a spinner, or a seed packet works a certain way. You have one result. Could that claim have produced it?
Describe a claim as a model that makes data, simulate the model to see what is typical, and judge honestly whether a result fits, saying only what the evidence allows.
Reading & work: Read the worked examples, run the model checker, simulate a coin in Excel, and write careful conclusions in your own words. Plan on about 90 minutes. Every coin, spinner, die, and seed packet on this page is constructed (made up).

✨ RESPONSIBILITY: SAY NO MORE THAN THE EVIDENCE ALLOWS
A result in hand is tempting. “We saw 36 heads, so the coin is rigged.” “We saw 28 heads, so the coin is fair.” Both sentences claim more than the data can carry. Responsibility with evidence means asking two questions before speaking: how often would chance alone produce a result like this? and what am I actually entitled to say?
Today you will learn a careful way to answer both. The skill is also a kindness to everyone who hears your claim: a person who says “this result is hard to square with the claim” or “this result fits the claim, though it does not settle it” can be trusted, because the words match the evidence.
🎲 A MODEL IS A RULE FOR HOW THE DATA COULD HAVE BEEN MADE
A data-generating process is whatever produces the numbers you see. When we write down a rule for that process, with its chances, we have a model. Four models you can picture:
▶ A fair coin. Each flip lands heads with chance 50%, and flips do not affect each other. In 50 flips we expect 50 × 0.5 = 25 heads.
▶ A spinner with 4 equal sections. Each spin lands on section A with chance 25%. In 50 spins we expect 50 × 0.25 = 12.5 landings on A. (An expected count need not be a whole number. It is a long-run average, not a prediction of any single run.)
▶ A die. If it is fair, each roll shows a six with chance 1/6. In 60 rolls we expect 60 × (1/6) = 10 sixes.
▶ “30% of these seeds sprout.” Each planted seed sprouts with chance 30%. Of 50 seeds we expect 50 × 0.3 = 15 sprouts.
Every one of these models gives a claimed chance of success and a number of trials. The model does not say “exactly 25 heads.” Real runs wobble around the expected count. The question this lesson asks is: how far from expected is too far for the model to be believable?
🔄 SIMULATE THE PROCESS, THEN LOOK AT THE SPREAD
We cannot ask the coin how it feels. But we can run the model: flip a pretend fair coin 50 times, count the heads, and write the count down. That is one simulated run. Do it 1000 times and you have a picture of what the model typically does: most runs land near 25, fewer land far away, and very few land very far away.
Now place your real result on that picture. If it sits in the crowded middle, the model produces results like yours all the time. If it sits far out in a thin tail, the model rarely produces a result like yours.
A rule of thumb for “unusual.” Count the simulated runs that are at least as far from the expected count as your result, on either side. If that happens in fewer than 5 runs out of 100 (fewer than 50 of 1000), call the result unusual under the claimed model. Otherwise it is not unusual. The “5 in 100” line is a convention people agree to, a place to draw a line so we can talk to each other. It is not a law of nature, and you will see results that sit right on it.
Link to Semester 1. In Semester 1 you asked how surprising a result would be if a claim were true, and you marked results that fell far outside the middle range. This is the same question. Here you answer it by running the model many times and counting, instead of by a formula. Write the idea in your own words in Activity 1.
🪙 WORKED EXAMPLE 1 — A FAIR COIN: 36 HEADS VERSUS 28 HEADS IN 50 FLIPS
Constructed data, not real. A made-up game claims its coin is fair. We flip it 50 times. The expected count is 25 heads.
Case A: 36 heads. That is 11 above 25. “At least as far from expected” means 36 or more heads, or 14 or fewer (also 11 away, on the low side). Using the exact binomial chances for 50 flips of a fair coin:
▶ Chance of 36 or more heads = 0.001301.
▶ By symmetry, chance of 14 or fewer = 0.001301 as well.
▶ Chance of a result at least this far from 25, on either side: 0.001301 + 0.001301 = 0.002602, about 1 in 384 (1 ÷ 0.002602 ≈ 384).
A simulation of 1000 runs would show about 1000 × 0.002602 ≈ 2.6 such runs, and usually between 0 and 6. That is far below the cutoff of 50. The result is unusual under the claimed model. So there is good reason to doubt that the coin is fair. We have not proved it is rigged: a fair coin can produce this, about once in 384 tries.
Case B: 28 heads. That is 3 above 25. At least as far means 28 or more, or 22 or fewer. The exact chance of a result at least this far on either side is 0.480. A simulation of 1000 runs would show about 480 such runs, give or take about 16, so a typical reading is somewhere from about 450 to 510. That is nowhere near unusual. The result is not unusual, and the claim that the coin is fair is consistent with it.
A note on the simulator’s number. Every time the simulator runs 1000 new runs, the count it shows changes a little, because it is a fresh random sample of runs. It fluctuates around the exact chance times 1000. The exact values above are the targets it wobbles around.
⚖️ WORKED EXAMPLE 2 — RESULTS THAT SIT ON THE LINE
Constructed data, not real. The rule of thumb is a line, and some results land close to it. Here are four, each recomputed with the exact binomial.

Look at the 60-heads row and the 19-of-50 row. Their chances, 0.0569 and 0.0481, are only about one percentage point apart, yet the rule of thumb puts them on opposite sides. Do not treat the line as a wall. A careful sentence for these two is: “This result is borderline. It is a little harder to square with the claim than I would like, but not strongly so.”
Two more ideas from this table. First, the same distance means different things at different sample sizes: a count 5 above expected is much more unusual in 50 flips than in 100 flips, because more flips give a steadier result. Second, when the simulator shows a count like 52 or 47 out of 1000 for the borderline rows, both are fair readings of the same underlying chance near 0.05, and one can land on either side of the cutoff. When a result is this close, run it a few times and report it as borderline.
🌱 WORKED EXAMPLE 3 — “30% OF THESE SEEDS SPROUT”
Constructed data, not real. A made-up seed packet says 30% of its seeds sprout. A student plants 50 and counts sprouts. Expected: 50 × 0.3 = 15.
Result: 21 sprouts. That is 6 above expected. Counts at least as far are 21 or more, or 9 or fewer (also 6 below 15). The exact chance of a result at least this far on either side is 0.0880. A simulation of 1000 runs would show about 88, give or take about 9. That is well above 50, so the result is not unusual under the claimed model: the packet’s claim is consistent with 21 sprouts.
What we may say. “21 of 50 is a bit high, but a 30% packet would give a result at least this far from 15 about 9 times in 100, so we have no good reason to doubt the claim.” What we may not say. “This proves 30% of the seeds sprout.” A packet whose true rate is 35% or 40% could produce 21 sprouts easily too. One run of 50 seeds cannot tell those models apart from 30%.
🎲 WORKED EXAMPLE 4 — A DIE THAT MIGHT BE LOADED
Constructed data, not real. A made-up game shop is selling a die and claims it is fair. We roll it 60 times and count sixes. The model says each roll is a six with chance 1/6, so we expect 60 × (1/6) = 10 sixes. We observe 17 sixes.
That is seven above expected. At least as far means 17 or more sixes, or 3 or fewer (also 7 away). Using the exact binomial for 60 rolls with chance 1/6:
▶ Chance of 3 or fewer sixes = 0.00635.
▶ Chance of 17 or more sixes = 0.01645.
▶ Sum: 0.00635 + 0.01645 = 0.0228, about 2.3 in 100.
Notice that the two tails are not equal here (about 0.006 on the low side, 0.016 on the high side), because a chance of 1/6 is not a 50-50 chance. That is why we compute each tail rather than doubling one. A simulation of 1000 runs would show about 23 such runs, give or take about 5, so readings from about 13 to 32 are normal. All are below 50, so the result is unusual under the claimed model.
The careful conclusion: “If this die were fair, a result this far from 10 sixes would happen about 2 times in 100. That is hard to square with a fair die, so I doubt the claim. I have not proved the die is loaded. To be surer, I would roll it many more times.” (The model checker’s custom claim takes whole-number percents, so enter 17% as a close stand-in for 1/6. Its numbers will differ very slightly from the exact ones above.)
⚠️ CONSISTENT IS NOT PROVED, AND UNUSUAL IS NOT WRONG
These two sentences are the heart of the lesson. Keep them in your own words.
What you foundWhat you may sayWhat you may not say
Not unusual under the claimed modelThe result is consistent with the claim. We have no good reason to drop it. We stick with the model for now.“The claim is proved,” or “the claim is true.” Other models fit too: a coin that lands heads 55% of the time has an expected 27.5 heads in 50 flips, so 28 heads fits it well.
Unusual under the claimed modelEither we saw a rare stroke of luck or the claim is off. The rarer the result, the more reason we have to doubt the model.“The claim is proved wrong.” Rare things happen: about 1 in 384 fair coins would give 36 heads in 50 (or 14 or fewer).
Why both directions are limited. A simulation tells us how a model behaves. It does not tell us the model is the one that made our data. A result can fit several different models, and a surprising result can come from the very model we doubt. What we can do is describe how well the claim and the data agree, and let the strength of the evidence set the strength of our language: “very hard to square,” “borderline,” “fits well.”
One more caution: multiple looks. If you check one true model against one result, the chance of landing in the unusual 5% is about 5%. Suppose instead you check 20 separate true models, each on its own independent result. The chance that none falls in its 5% zone is 0.9520 ≈ 0.358. So the chance that at least one does is 1 − 0.9520 = 0.642, about 64%, even though every model is true. (This calculation assumes the 20 checks are independent and each unusual zone has chance 5%. Real checks often are not independent, which changes the number but not the lesson.) If you look at many models or many results and report only the one that looks odd, you are being misleading, even if every number you report is correct. Honest reporting says how many things you checked. With 5 independent checks the same arithmetic gives 1 − 0.955 = 0.226.

Anyone handed a claimed rate (a sprouting rate, a defect rate, a “fair” game) and a count of what actually happened can run this kind of check. Methods and cutoffs differ from one field to the next, so look up how a particular organization makes the call before relying on its numbers.
💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · EXCEL: SIMULATE A COIN AND CHECK IT EXACTLY
Simulate one run of 50 flips. One way: in a cell type =BINOM.INV(50,0.5,RAND()) and press Enter. It returns a random head count for 50 flips of a fair coin. Coin by coin instead: in 50 cells type =IF(RANDBETWEEN(0,1)=1,1,0) (1 = heads) and add them with =SUM(range).

Repeat 100 times. Type the BINOM.INV formula in A1, click the Name Box and enter A1:A100, then press Ctrl+D to fill down. You now have 100 simulated runs. Press F9 to redraw all 100 at once.

Count runs at least as extreme as 36 heads. =COUNTIF(A1:A100,">=36")+COUNTIF(A1:A100,"<=14"). Expect 0 almost every time: the exact chance is 0.002602, so 100 runs would show only about 0.26 such runs. A milder test shows more: =COUNTIF(A1:A100,">=31")+COUNTIF(A1:A100,"<=19") counts runs at least 6 from 25, which has exact chance 0.1189, so expect about 12.

Exact check. =BINOM.DIST(35,50,0.5,TRUE) gives the chance of 35 or fewer heads (0.998699). So =1-BINOM.DIST(35,50,0.5,TRUE) is the chance of 36 or more (0.001301). For the fair coin the low tail matches the high tail by symmetry, so double it: =2*(1-BINOM.DIST(35,50,0.5,TRUE)) = 0.002602.

Symmetry caveat. Doubling works only when the claimed chance is 0.5. For any other claim, work out each tail. The spinner example (19 of 50, claimed chance 0.25) is =BINOM.DIST(6,50,0.25,TRUE)+1-BINOM.DIST(18,50,0.25,TRUE) = 0.0481, and the die example (17 sixes in 60) is =BINOM.DIST(3,60,1/6,TRUE)+1-BINOM.DIST(16,60,1/6,TRUE) = 0.0228.

Desmos Scientific is for plain arithmetic only. Type 50*0.5 for the expected heads (25), 60/6 for expected sixes (10), and 1-0.95^20 for the multiple-looks chance (0.642). The binomial tail chances come from Excel.
🤖 AI TRAINING · GIVE AN AI SIMULATED DATA AND ASK IF THE MODEL FITS (PROMPT → PREDICT → VERIFY)
Predict first. A made-up coin is claimed to be fair. In 50 flips it showed 33 heads. Before you open any AI tool, decide: is this result unusual under a fair coin by the 5-in-100 rule? Write your guess and one sentence of reasoning.
Prompt. Give an AI tool exactly this: “A coin is claimed to be fair. We flipped it 50 times and got 33 heads. Is the claimed model consistent with the data? Explain how a simulation of the claimed model would show this, and give the chance of a result at least this far from expected on either side.”
Verify. Open the model checker, choose A fair coin (chance of heads 50%), set Number of trials n to 50 and Observed count of successes to 33, and read the count out of 1000 and the verdict. Compare with the AI’s numbers. Check three things: did it count both sides rather than only one tail, does its number agree with the simulation (it will match only roughly, not exactly), and did it avoid language like “proves the coin is biased” or “proves the model is correct”? Report what it got right, where it was wrong or too confident, and what you did about it.
▶  Answer key for the AI Training task
The expected count is 25 and 33 is 8 above, so at least as far means 33 or more, or 17 or fewer. The exact chance of 33 or more is 0.0164, so both sides together are 0.0328 (about 3.3 in 100). A simulation of 1000 runs reads about 33, give or take 6. Under the 5-in-100 rule this is unusual: a modest reason to doubt the coin is fair, not proof. A one-sided reading (only 33 or more) gives 0.0164, half as big; an AI that reports only one tail makes the result look more surprising than the either-side rule does. A response that says the result “proves” the coin is biased, or that a result that is not unusual “proves” a coin is fair, has overclaimed.
