✨ RESPONSIBILITY: CLAIM ONLY WHAT YOUR DESIGN CAN CARRY
Two honest researchers can collect the same number of measurements and be entitled to very different sentences. One may say, “Shoe A made these runners faster.” Another, with the same size of data, may only say, “Runners who wear Shoe A tend to be faster.” The difference is not modesty or boldness. It is how the study was built.
The virtue here is responsibility: to design a study that can answer the question you actually care about, and then to write a conclusion no bigger than the design can carry. Overclaiming is the most common way good data gets turned into a bad headline.
🔍 THREE DESIGNS, SORTED BY WHAT THE RESEARCHERS DO
Ask one question about any study: what did the researchers do to the people or things in it? There are three answers.
DesignWhat the researchers doA made-up example
Sample surveyAsk. Select some members of a population and record their answers. Nothing is changed for anyone.A school office asks 150 of its 900 students how they get to school.
Observational studyMeasure or watch. Record what people or things are already doing, with no treatment imposed. People end up in groups by their own choices or circumstances.A grower records which trees in her orchard were fertilized with Mix A in past years (the crew chose which) and how much fruit each tree gave.
ExperimentImpose a treatment. The researchers decide which unit gets which condition, then measure the result.A grower decides which of 24 trees get Mix A and which get Mix B, then weighs the apples at harvest.
A survey and an observational study both leave everyone as they are; the survey asks questions, and the observational study often measures. Only an experiment changes something on purpose. A treatment is the condition the researchers impose: a fertilizer, a study method, a shoe, an oven temperature.
📝 THE WORDS YOU NEED: UNITS, VARIABLES, TREATMENTS
Every study has the same cast of characters, whatever the design.
TermMeaningIn the orchard experiment
Experimental unit (or subject, if it is a person)The individual thing that receives a treatment and is measured.One tree. There are 24 units.
Explanatory variableThe variable we think may explain or influence the outcome. In an experiment, it is the treatment.Which fertilizer: Mix A or Mix B.
Response variableThe outcome we measure to see if the explanatory variable matters.Weight of apples from the tree, in kilograms.
TreatmentsThe specific conditions compared.Two treatments: Mix A and Mix B.
These words work in observational studies too, except that nobody imposes the explanatory variable; the researchers only observe it. In the grower’s records, “which mix the crew used” is still the explanatory variable and apple weight is still the response, but the crew chose the mix, not the researcher.
🍎 WORKED EXAMPLE 1: THE SAME ORCHARD, TWO DESIGNS
Constructed scenario, not real data. A made-up orchard has 24 apple trees and two fertilizers, Mix A and Mix B. The grower wants to know whether the choice of mix affects the weight of apples per tree. Here are two ways to study it.
Design 1, an observational study. She looks back through the farm logbook. In past years the crew gave Mix A to the trees nearest the barn, because they were easy to reach, and Mix B to the trees farther out. She records each tree’s mix and its yield.
▶ What the researcher did: read records. She imposed nothing. This is observational, and because it looks back at records of the past, it is retrospective.
▶ Explanatory variable: mix used. Response variable: apple weight per tree. Units: the 24 trees.
▶ Trouble: trees near the barn may also get more shade, more water, or more attention. If barn-side trees yield more, the grower cannot tell whether the mix helped or the location did.
Design 2, an experiment. Next season she numbers the trees 1 to 24, draws 12 numbers from a hat, and gives those 12 trees Mix A. The other 12 get Mix B. She weighs the apples from each tree at harvest.
▶ What the researcher did: decided which tree gets which mix, by chance. She imposed the treatment. This is an experiment.
▶ Why chance helps: barn-side trees and far-side trees are likely to be spread across both groups. Whatever else differs among trees (shade, soil, age) tends to be spread about evenly, so a large gap in yield is hard to blame on anything but the mix.
What each design may conclude. From Design 2: Mix A (or B) likely caused a difference in yield for these 24 trees. From Design 1: Mix and yield are associated, but the study cannot say that the mix caused it.
🔄 WHY OBSERVATIONAL STUDIES LEAVE ROOM FOR CONFOUNDING
A confounding variable is a third variable that is tied to both the explanatory variable and the response, so its effect gets mixed up with the effect you are trying to measure. In the logbook study, the position of the tree is a confounder: it is linked to which mix the tree got and it may affect yield.
You have met this before. In the cones-and-pool-incidents example (Week 28 Lesson 3), towns that sold more ice cream cones also had more pool incidents. Selling cones did not cause incidents, and incidents did not cause cones. Hot weather pushed both up. The weather was the confounder, and the strong correlation said nothing about cause.
Observational studies have this problem in general, because people sort themselves into groups for reasons of their own. Students who choose optional tutoring may be more motivated. Runners who choose a particular shoe may be more experienced. We can sometimes measure those variables and adjust for them, but we can never be sure we have thought of all of them. Correlation is not causation, and an observational study can show association only.
🎲 RANDOM SAMPLING AND RANDOM ASSIGNMENT ARE DIFFERENT JOBS
Chance can enter a study in two separate places, and students often blur them. Keep them apart:
▶ Random sampling decides who is in the study. It is how the researchers pick people from the population. Its job is to make the sample represent the population, so what you find can be generalized to that population.
▶ Random assignment decides who gets which treatment. It is how the researchers split the people already in the study into groups. Its job is to make the groups alike before the treatment, so a difference afterward can be credited to the treatment: cause.
Each can be present or absent, so there are four combinations. Every example below is made up.
Random assignmentNo random assignment
Random samplingBoth. A district has 1,200 ninth graders. The office draws 60 at random, then draws names from a hat to put 30 on flashcards and 30 on practice quizzes, and compares next week’s test scores.
Can support: cause, and generalizing to the 1,200.		Sampling only. The same office draws 60 students at random and records each student’s weekly hours of sleep and last test score. Nobody is told to change anything.
Can support: generalizing an association to the 1,200. Not cause.
No random sampling (volunteers, convenience)Assignment only. A bakery asks for volunteers to taste-test. Forty sign up, and names drawn from a hat put 20 on Recipe X and 20 on Recipe Y.
Can support: cause among these volunteers. Not generalizing to all customers.		Neither. A shop owner notes the ratings of the first 20 customers through the door, each of whom picked a recipe on their own.
Can support: a description of those 20 people. Neither generalizing nor cause.
Rule of thumb: random sampling lets you talk about the population; random assignment lets you talk about cause. A study needs both to claim both, and many good studies have only one. Random assignment is also called randomization or randomized allocation, and a study that uses it is a randomized experiment.
⏰ LOOKING FORWARD OR LOOKING BACK (A LIGHT TOUCH)
Observational studies come in two timings. A prospective study chooses its people now and follows them forward in time, recording as things happen. A retrospective study looks back at records or memories of what already happened. The grower’s logbook study was retrospective.
Both are observational: nobody imposes a treatment, so both leave room for confounding. A prospective study can usually measure things more carefully, since the researchers are there while it happens. A retrospective study is quicker and cheaper but depends on what was written down, or on what people remember.
📊 WHAT EACH DESIGN CAN HONESTLY CLAIM

Honesty about generalizing: even a well-run random sample can suffer from nonresponse, as you saw in Week 31, so “generalize” means the plan supports it, not that the work is done.
🏛️ WHERE PEOPLE USE THIS · COMPARING TWO VERSIONS
Many medical trials and website A/B tests use random assignment to compare two options, although the details differ from one study to the next, so look up how a particular study was run before trusting its headline.
💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · EXCEL: SUMMARIZING TWO GROUPS WITH AVERAGEIF AND COUNTIF
The data layout. An experiment ends with a table where each row is one unit, with a column that says which group it was in. In a blank sheet type these headings in row 1: A Runner, B Shoe, C Time (min), D Beat personal best?. Put the twelve runners in rows 2 to 13, sorted so the six with Shoe A are in rows 2 to 7 and the six with Shoe B are in rows 8 to 13.

Mean for one group. =AVERAGEIF(B2:B13,"A",C2:C13) looks down the Shoe column B for the text A and averages the matching times in column C. Change the A to B for the other group. If the group label is typed in a cell, such as F2, use =AVERAGEIF($B$2:$B$13,F2,$C$2:$C$13) so the formula can be filled down.

Count one result in one group. =COUNTIF(D2:D7,"Yes") counts the Yes answers among the Shoe A rows. For the Shoe B rows use =COUNTIF(D8:D13,"Yes"). To find a group size use =COUNTIF($B$2:$B$13,F2). The share is the count divided by the group size.

Compare the groups. Subtract one mean from the other in a new cell, for example =G3-G2. A difference in means describes the data; it is the random assignment, not the formula, that lets you credit the shoe.

Desmos Scientific is for plain arithmetic only. Type 156/6 for 26, 165/6 for 27.5, and 27.5-26 for 1.5. A share is 4/6, about 0.667.
🤖 AI TRAINING · GRADE AN AI ON FIVE STUDY DESCRIPTIONS (PROMPT → PREDICT → VERIFY)
Predict first. Before you open any AI tool, label each of the five made-up studies below as survey, observational, or experiment. For each, also write one line: can it support a conclusion about cause, about a population, both, or neither?

(a) A made-up bakery draws 200 of its 2,000 customers at random and asks each which bread they prefer.
(b) A made-up school asks for volunteers for a study-habits project. Sixty students volunteer, and names drawn from a hat split them into two groups of 30: one uses flashcards, the other uses practice quizzes. Next week’s test scores are compared.
(c) A made-up researcher draws 500 adults at random from a city list, and records whether each eats breakfast every day and their results on a fitness test.
(d) A made-up orchard crew gives the new fertilizer to the 10 trees with the best-looking soil and the old fertilizer to 10 other trees, then compares the apple weight per tree.
(e) A made-up researcher reads three years of school records for 90 students, noting which ones joined the after-school club and how their grades changed.

Prompt. Give an AI tool all five descriptions and ask: “For each study, say whether it is a survey, an observational study, or an experiment. Then say whether the results can be generalized to a larger population and whether they show that one thing caused another. Explain each answer in two sentences.”
Verify. Compare the AI’s labels with your own and with the key below. Grade it out of 5 for the labels. Then separately mark every place where it confuses random sampling with random assignment: that is where AI answers most often go wrong, and a correct label can still come with a wrong claim about cause or generalizing. Record the number of labels it got right, the number of sampling-versus-assignment slips, and one sentence on how much you would trust it next time.
▶  Answer key for the five studies
(a) Survey. The bakery asks and imposes nothing. Because the 200 were chosen at random, the answer may be generalized to the 2,000 customers, with the usual sampling variation and a watch for nonresponse.

(b) Experiment. The researchers decided who got which method, and by random assignment, so cause is supported for the volunteers. The sample was volunteers, not random, so the result does not generalize to the whole school. The trap: an AI may say “random, so it applies to all students.” Random assignment is not random sampling.

(c) Observational study (nobody changes breakfast habits; the groups are the people’s own choices). Random sampling lets the association be generalized to the city’s adults, but there is no random assignment, so cause is not supported. The trap: an AI may say “random, so breakfast causes better fitness.” Random sampling is not random assignment.

(d) Experiment without random assignment. The crew imposed the treatment, so it is an experiment, but it hand-picked the best soil for the new mix. Soil quality is confounded with the mix, so cause is not established. An AI may mislabel it “observational”; the design is an experiment, just a weak one.

(e) Observational, retrospective. The researcher looked back at records and imposed nothing. Students chose the club, so motivation and other things may be confounders. Association only.

Your AI’s answers may differ on wording or on a case it handles correctly. If it gets everything right, say so, and say how you checked.
