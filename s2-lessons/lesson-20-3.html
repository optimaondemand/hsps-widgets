🧭 THE QUESTION BEHIND EVERY DISPLAY
You now have six displays: bar chart, circle graph, line plot, stem-and-leaf plot, histogram, and box plot. (In Week 21 you will add the scatter plot and the line graph for two variables.) Knowing how to draw each is half the skill. The other half is choosing — and the choice is never “which is prettiest?” It is “what question am I answering, with what kind of data, for how many values?”
Honesty is this module's virtue, and display choice is where honesty gets practical: the same numbers can be made to say different things by the picture you choose. A good statistician picks the display that lets the data speak, and says what the display cannot show.
📑 THREE QUESTIONS TO ASK
▶ 1. What kind of variable? Categorical (groups) → bar chart or circle graph. Numerical (measurements or counts) → line plot, stem-and-leaf, histogram, or box plot.
▶ 2. How many values? Fewer than about 50 → line plot or stem-and-leaf plot can show every value. Dozens to thousands → histogram or box plot.
▶ 3. What do you want the reader to see? Shape, clusters, and gaps → histogram. A quick five-number summary or a side-by-side comparison of several groups → box plots. Exact values → stem-and-leaf. Parts of a whole → circle graph. Comparing categories → bar chart.
Every choice involves a trade-off. Here is the whole table, in one place to keep:
DisplayBest forStrengthWeakness
Bar chartCategorical, any number of categoriesEasy to compare lengthsMisleads if the axis does not start at 0

📝 WORKED EXAMPLE — COMPARING TWO CLASSES
Two classes take the same 100-point test. The teacher wants to compare how the scores are spread, not just the middle. Five-number summaries (constructed): Class P: 52, 64, 72, 80, 95. Class Q: 60, 68, 72, 76, 88. Teacher's voice: the question is about comparing two groups' spreads, so side-by-side box plots on a single axis are the natural choice.

The two classes have the same median, 72. But Class P's IQR is \(80 - 64 = 16\) and Class Q's is \(76 - 68 = 8\). The middle half of Class Q's scores fits in a box half as wide, so Class Q is more consistent, while Class P has more students at both the high and low ends. What the box plots cannot tell us is why, or whether either class has clusters inside the box. If the teacher wanted that, a histogram of each class would be the next display to draw.
📜 A MOMENT IN THE HISTORY OF MATHEMATICS · FLORENCE NIGHTINGALE'S ROSE DIAGRAMS
Florence Nightingale (1820–1910) is remembered as the nurse of the Crimean War, but she was also a statistician — in 1858 she became the first woman elected to the Statistical Society of London (today's Royal Statistical Society). After the war she wanted to persuade British officials that most soldiers' deaths in the military hospitals had been caused by disease that better sanitation could prevent, not by wounds in battle. A table of numbers had not moved them, so she and her collaborators designed a new picture: a circular diagram, now called a rose or polar-area diagram, in which each month of the war is a wedge and the wedge's area shows the number of deaths, with colors separating deaths from preventable disease, from wounds, and from other causes. As the story is usually told, the wedges for preventable disease were so much larger than the others in the early months that the message could be read from across a room. She was choosing a display to fit a question and an audience — exactly today's skill. It is also worth noticing that people judge area in wedges less accurately than length in bars, so a modern statistician might draw the same data as a bar chart. The lesson is not that one display is right forever, but that the choice of display is part of the argument.

💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · EXCEL AND DESMOS: YOUR WEEK 20 TOOLKIT
Excel — COUNTIF on text. COUNTIF also counts matching words. If the lunch choices are typed in A2:A31, the formula =COUNTIF($A$2:$A$31,"Pizza") counts the pizzas. Better: put the category names in a column, point the formula at them (=COUNTIF($A$2:$A$31,D2)) and copy down, as you did for numbers; check that the counts total 30.
Excel — COUNTIFS for bins (Lesson 20.2) works on any numerical column: two conditions, left-in, right-out.
Desmos. The IQR comparison above is one subtraction each. Write the subtraction so you can see it: 80-64 → 16 and 76-68 → 8.
🤖 AI TRAINING · PROMPT → PREDICT → VERIFY
Prompt. Describe this dataset to an AI tool and ask for the single best display, then make it defend the choice against one alternative: “The heights in centimeters of 300 students.”
Predict. Decide first. Numerical, one variable, many values: a histogram shows the shape; a box plot would summarize; a stem-and-leaf plot would be far too long for 300 values.
Verify. Ask the AI to defend a stem-and-leaf plot for all 300 values and judge whether it can. Did it answer your actual question, or merely produce a confident-sounding paragraph? Whatever it recommends, you are still the one who must be able to say why.

2
In one sentence, say how a bin width that is too wide can mislead.
▶  Check
Seeing only the box plots, a reader would think the two datasets look alike and might guess Set B also has one hump. A histogram (bins of width 5) shows that Set B has two clusters. A bin that is too wide merges neighboring clusters into one bar and can erase a real gap.

Read the Rose

1

▶  Check
A picture lets a viewer take in the comparison in seconds, where a table needs study; she was persuading busy officials. An honest reader should still ask where the numbers came from, how reliable the recorded causes were, and whether wedge areas are being judged fairly. Answers for the second part will vary; look for a strength and a weakness that are both specific to that display.
