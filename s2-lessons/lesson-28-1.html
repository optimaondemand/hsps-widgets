Lesson 28.1
Residuals: Assessing a Linear Fit
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A line is only as good as what it misses. Look at the misses.
Residuals (observed minus predicted) and what their signs mean; residual plots; the biggest miss; outliers and points that pull the line; residuals that add to zero and the sum of squared residuals; a residual column in Excel; a good fit is still only an association
Reading & work: Read every section in order, work each example on paper before you read its solution, use the Fit the Line interactive, follow the Excel steps, and complete the three activities at the bottom. Plan on about 75 minutes.

1
I will be able to compute a residual (observed minus predicted) and say what its sign means in context.
2
I will be able to read a residual plot and decide whether a straight line is adequate (random scatter) or not (a curve or a fan shape).
3

4
I will be able to explain that the residuals of a least-squares line add to zero (and check it on small data), and that the line makes the sum of squared residuals as small as possible.

✨ A WONDER TO START WITH
Last lesson you learned a rule for picking one best line: make the sum of squared misses as small as possible. But a rule that picks the best line does not promise that the line is good. Even the best straight line through a curved cloud is a poor description of it. How would you find out?
The answer is to stop staring at the line and look at what is left over: the leftovers, point by point. If the line has captured the real pattern, the leftovers should look like nothing at all, just random noise. If the leftovers have a shape, the line has missed something, and the shape tells you what. Honest modelers do this check every time, because it is easy to love a line and hard to admit it is wrong.
That honesty is this module's virtue, responsibility. Today you learn to ask of every fitted line: what did it miss, and where?
A note on honesty. Every scenario, dataset, and equation in this lesson is constructed to teach the idea. None of it is real data about real people, teams, or plants.
📏 THE RESIDUAL: OBSERVED MINUS PREDICTED
Every data point has an observed value, y. The line gives a predicted value, ŷ. The difference is called the residual:
residual = y − ŷ    (observed minus predicted)
The order matters, and it is always observed minus predicted. (Last lesson called this the “miss.” Residual is the name statisticians use.) The sign tells you the direction of the miss:
▶ Positive residual. The observed value is above the line. The model underpredicted: the actual value was higher than predicted.
▶ Negative residual. The observed value is below the line. The model overpredicted: the actual value was lower than predicted.
▶ Zero residual. The point sits exactly on the line.
The size tells you how far off the model was, in the same units as y. A residual of −5.6 quiz points is a miss of about 5.6 points, in the downward direction.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 1: ONE RESIDUAL, AND WHAT ITS SIGN MEANS
Problem. The least-squares line for the Hours studied scenario (15 constructed students) is ŷ = 49.404 + 6.5542x, where x is hours studied and ŷ is predicted quiz score in points. A made-up student studied 4 hours and scored 78. Another made-up student studied 4 hours and scored 70. Find each residual and say what it means.
Teacher's voice: First I find the predicted score at 4 hours, because every residual needs a prediction to subtract. Then I subtract in the order observed minus predicted, and I look at the sign before I say anything.
▶ Prediction. ŷ = 49.404 + 6.5542 × 4 = 49.404 + 26.2168 = 75.6208, about 75.6 points.
▶ Student with 78. residual = 78 − 75.6208 = +2.38. Positive: this student scored about 2.4 points higher than the model predicted for 4 hours.
▶ Student with 70. residual = 70 − 75.6208 = −5.62. Negative: this student scored about 5.6 points lower than the model predicted.
Sense check. 78 is above 75.6, so the point is above the line and the residual is positive. 70 is below 75.6, so the residual is negative. The signs agree with the picture.

Check the line. The means are x̄ = 28 ÷ 7 = 4 and ȳ = 77 ÷ 7 = 11, and 3 + 2(4) = 11, so the line passes through (x̄, ȳ), as a least-squares line must.
⚖️ WHY THE RESIDUALS ADD TO ZERO, AND WHAT SSE IS FOR
The zero in the last example was not luck. It is a fact about every least-squares line (one that has an intercept, like ours): the residuals add to zero, apart from tiny rounding. The line sits so that the misses above it exactly balance the misses below it. You cannot prove it here, but you can check it on any small data set, and you just did.
That fact is also a trap, so notice what it does not say. Residuals adding to zero does not mean the line is good. Even a terrible line through a curved cloud has residuals that add to zero. A zero sum carries no information about quality. For quality we use the second fact from last lesson: the least-squares line makes the sum of the squared residuals, the SSE, as small as possible.
Compare the same seven points with two other lines:
LineResiduals at x = 1 to 7Sum of residualsSSE
ŷ = 3 + 2x (least squares)1, −3, 1, 2, −1, 1, −1018
ŷ = 4 + 2x (shifted up 1)0, −4, 0, 1, −2, 0, −2−725
ŷ = 2 + 2.5x (tilted and shifted)1.5, −3.0, 0.5, 1.0, −2.5, −1.0, −3.5−7.032.00
Shifting the line up by 1 pushes every residual down by 1, so seven residuals drop by 7 in total: the sum goes from 0 to −7, and the SSE rises from 18 to 25. The tilted line does worse still (sum −7.0, SSE 32). One more caution: other lines can also have residuals that add to 0 (any line through the point (x̄, ȳ) does), so a zero sum cannot pick the best line. The SSE can: the least-squares line has the smallest one.
📈 READING A RESIDUAL PLOT
A residual plot graphs each residual against x. The horizontal line at 0 is the fitted line itself: points above it are above the line, points below it are below. You are looking at the line's misses, magnified and with the slope removed. Ask one question: is there a pattern?
The three plots above use constructed residuals to show the shapes.
▶ Random scatter (left). Positives and negatives mixed with no shape, about the same spread everywhere. The line is adequate. This is what you hope for.
▶ A curve (middle). The residuals run in streaks: positive, then negative, then positive again, like a smile. The straight line is too straight; the real relationship bends. The line is not adequate, even if it has a small SSE. A curved model would do better.
▶ A fan (right). The residuals stay close to 0 at one end and spread wider and wider toward the other. The misses are not equally large everywhere, so predictions are far less trustworthy on one side. The line is not adequate as a description of the spread.
Why does this beat looking at the scatter plot alone? Because the eye is good at spotting a departure from a flat line at zero, and poor at spotting a slight bend in a tilted cloud.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 3: READING A LIST OF RESIDUALS
Problem. A constructed least-squares fit to 10 points (x = 1 to 10) leaves these residuals, in order of x: 6.0, 2.0, −1.0, −3.0, −4.0, −4.0, −3.0, −1.0, 2.0, 6.0. Is a straight line adequate?
Teacher's voice: I do not need a graph to start. I read the signs in order of x and look for streaks.
▶ Signs, in order: + + − − − − − − + +.
▶ Streaks: positive at the left end, a long negative run in the middle, positive again at the right end. The sign changes just twice, and the pattern is a smile.
▶ Sanity check: the residuals add to 0 (to rounding), as they must for a least-squares line, so nothing is wrong with the arithmetic. The sum being 0 says nothing about the shape.
Conclusion. Streaks like this mean a straight line is not adequate; the data curve. Random signs, flipping back and forth with no run, would have said the opposite.
🔍 THE BIGGEST MISS, AND A POINT THAT PULLS THE LINE
Two different questions come up about unusual points, and they have different answers.
▶ The biggest miss is the point with the largest residual in size: the point the line describes worst. A point far from the line, with a large residual, is called an outlier in the fit.
▶ A point that pulls the line hard (statisticians say it has high leverage, and is influential if it actually changes the line a lot) is a point far from the others in the x direction. A point out at the edge of the x values acts like the end of a lever: the line swings toward it.
Here is the surprise: they are not the same thing. Use six constructed points: (1, 3), (2, 5), (3, 4), (4, 7), (5, 6), (6, 8). Their least-squares line is ŷ = 2.400 + 0.886x. Now add one more point two ways.
DataLeast-squares lineWhat happened
The six points aloneŷ = 2.400 + 0.886xThe baseline.
Add (3.5, 12): a big miss in the middle of the x valuesŷ = 3.329 + 0.886xResidual at the new point is 5.57: a huge miss. But the slope did not move at all (0.886); only the intercept shifted. An outlier, but it pulls little.
Add (14, 3): far to the right of the othersŷ = 5.455 + −0.0625xThe slope flips from 0.886 to −0.0625: the line is nearly flat. One point changed the story. Yet its own residual is only −1.58, smaller than the residual 2.92 at (6, 8). The line bent toward it, so it hides its own influence.
Lesson learned. A small residual does not clear a point of suspicion. A point far out in x can drag the line toward itself and look innocent in the residual plot. When you see such a point, refit the line with and without it and report both. If the answer changes a lot, say so; do not quietly delete the point to get a result you like. Honesty about a data point you cannot explain is part of responsibility.

⚖️ RESPONSIBILITY: A GOOD-LOOKING LINE IS STILL ONLY AN ASSOCIATION
Suppose your residual plot is a perfect, patternless cloud and the SSE is small. You have shown that a straight line is a fair summary of how these two variables move together. You have not shown that changing x would change y. A good-looking line is still only an association: correlation is not causation. Something else, such as how much a student already knew or how much sleep they got, could be moving both. Lesson 28.3 takes this up fully.
A residual plot checks the shape of the fit. It cannot check the cause. In practice, analysts in many fields look at residual plots before trusting a fitted line for prediction, but they still do not claim a cause from a line fitted to observed data alone.
💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · ONE RESIDUAL IN DESMOS, A RESIDUAL COLUMN IN EXCEL
Desmos Scientific does plain arithmetic, so a single residual is just a prediction and a subtraction. For the student who studied 4 hours and scored 78, type 78-(49.404+6.5542*4) and you get about 2.38. Keep the whole prediction inside its own parentheses so the subtraction takes the entire predicted value. For the student who scored 70, type 70-(49.404+6.5542*4) and you get about −5.62. The order is always observed minus predicted; reversing it flips the sign.

Excel does many residuals at once: put the observed values in one column and the predicted values in the next, type =B2-C2 (observed minus predicted) in the first residual cell, and fill down. =SUM(D2:D8) adds the residuals and should show 0 or a number extremely close to 0 (such as 1E-15), for a least-squares line. =SUMSQ(D2:D8) gives the sum of the squared residuals. To see the shape, select the x column and the residual column and insert a Scatter chart. Lesson 28.2 teaches the functions that compute the slope and intercept directly.
🤖 AI TRAINING · PROMPT → PREDICT → VERIFY
Prompt. Ask an AI tool: “A made-up practice study has these (x, y) points: (1, 6), (2, 4), (3, 10), (4, 13), (5, 12), (6, 16), (7, 16). The fitted line is predicted y = 3 + 2x. List the residual at each point (observed minus predicted), say whether each is above or below the line, and say what the sum of the residuals should be.”
Predict. Before you read its answer, work out the seven residuals yourself and the sum. (You should have 1, −3, 1, 2, −1, 1, −1 and a sum of 0.)
Verify. Check every residual the AI gives, one at a time, against yours, and check the signs yourself: is each point really above or below the line? Common errors to look for: subtracting in the wrong order (predicted minus observed, which flips every sign), an arithmetic slip in one row, saying the residuals sum to a number other than 0, or describing a negative residual as “the model underpredicted.” Report which items it got right, which it got wrong, and anything it stated too confidently. Then ask what the sum of squared residuals is and verify it yourself (it is 18).

1
Residual or Not? Sign and Size
12 min
1
A constructed model predicts the number of cups of tea a cafe sells from the number of rainy hours: ŷ = 20 + 6x. On one day with 3 rainy hours the cafe sold 41 cups. Compute the residual and say what its sign means in context.
2
On another day with 5 rainy hours the cafe sold 46 cups. Compute the residual. Which of the two days was the bigger miss, and which was an underprediction?
3
Narrate: say aloud why the order is observed minus predicted, and what a residual of 0 tells you.
▶  Check
1. ŷ = 20 + 6(3) = 38. Residual = 41 − 38 = +3. The cafe sold about 3 cups more than the model predicted (the model underpredicted). 2. ŷ = 20 + 6(5) = 50. Residual = 46 − 50 = −4. The cafe sold about 4 cups fewer than predicted (the model overpredicted). The 5-hour day was the bigger miss (4 is more than 3 in size); the 3-hour day was the underprediction. 3. The order is fixed so that everyone gets the same sign: positive means the point is above the line. A residual of 0 means the point lies exactly on the line.
2
Check the Sum, Then Read the Shape
15 min
1
A constructed least-squares line is ŷ = 2 + 3x and five points are (1, 4), (2, 9), (3, 10), (4, 15), (5, 17). Compute the five residuals. Add them. Square them and add those too.
2
A different line fitted to a different data set leaves these residuals, in order of x from 1 to 8: +4, +2, +1, −1, −3, −4, −2, +3. Read the signs. Is a straight line adequate? Say what you would try next.
3
A third residual plot is close to 0 for small x and then fans out wider and wider. State what that means for predictions at large x.
▶  Check
1. Predictions 5, 8, 11, 14, 17. Residuals 4 − 5 = −1; 9 − 8 = +1; 10 − 11 = −1; 15 − 14 = +1; 17 − 17 = 0. Sum = (−1) + 1 + (−1) + 1 + 0 = 0. Squares 1, 1, 1, 1, 0, so SSE = 4. (The means are x̄ = 3, ȳ = 11, and 2 + 3(3) = 11, so the line does pass through (x̄, ȳ).) 2. Signs: + + + − − − − +. That is a streak pattern (positive, negative, positive): a curve, so a straight line is not adequate; try a curved model. (These residuals add to 0, so the arithmetic is consistent, but the sum alone would not have told you about the shape.) 3. The misses are small at small x and large at large x, so the line is much less reliable for predictions at large x, and one “typical miss” does not describe the whole range.
3
Outlier or Influential? Make the Call
13 min
1
Using the interactive, in Hours studied mode find the point ringed biggest miss and the point ringed pulls hardest. Write two sentences: one on why a point can have a big residual and still pull the line only a little, and one on why a point can pull the line hard and yet have a small residual.
2
Back to the six points (1, 3), (2, 5), (3, 4), (4, 7), (5, 6), (6, 8) with line ŷ = 2.400 + 0.886x. Adding (14, 3) changed the slope to −0.0625. A classmate says “(14, 3) has a small residual, so it is not a problem.” Explain the mistake and what you would do.
3
In one sentence, explain why a good-looking line with a patternless residual plot still does not show that x causes y.
▶  Check
1. A point in the middle of the x values can be far from the line (a big residual) without tilting it much, because it has little leverage. A point far out in x has a lot of leverage: the line bends toward it, so its own residual can be small even though it moved the whole line. 2. The slope changed from 0.886 to −0.0625, so the point is influential; the small residual (−1.58) is small because the line bent toward it. Refit with and without it, report both lines, and say honestly that one point changed the answer. 3. A residual plot checks only the shape of the fit. Something else could be moving both variables, so the line describes an association and does not show a cause.
