📍 WHERE DOES A VALUE SIT?
The mean and median tell you where the middle of a dataset is. Often we want to know something different: where does one particular value sit among all the others? A student scored 78 — is that high, middling, or low for this class? A single number cannot answer that by itself. What answers it is the value's location in the ordered data, and the tools for location are percentiles and quartiles.
Today you learn to find them by hand, draw a box plot from them, read one, and then check your hand work against Excel — where you will meet a surprise: a spreadsheet can give a slightly different answer, and the difference is not a mistake.
🔢 PERCENTILES AND PERCENTILE RANK
A percentile is a value with about p percent of the data at or below it. The 25th percentile has about a quarter of the data at or below it; the 50th percentile is the median; the 90th percentile has about nine tenths of the data at or below it. The word “about” is honest: with only a handful of values you cannot always cut at exactly p percent, and different books and programs cut slightly differently.
A value's percentile rank goes the other way: you start with a value and ask what percent of the data is at or below it.
percentile rank of a value  =  (number of data values at or below it) ÷ (total number of values) × 100

Worked example 1 — from a value to its rank. What is the percentile rank of the score 78? Count the scores at or below 78. Scanning the ordered list, 78 is the 14th score, so 14 of the 20 scores are 78 or lower. Then \(\dfrac{14}{20} \times 100 = 70\). The score 78 has a percentile rank of 70: about 70 percent of the class scored 78 or lower.
Worked example 2 — from a rank to a value. Which score is at about the 75th percentile? Of 20 scores, 75 percent is \(0.75 \times 20 = 15\) scores, so we want the 15th score: 80. Check: 15 of 20 are at or below 80, and \(\dfrac{15}{20} \times 100 = 75\).
What a percentile rank is not. It is not a percent score. On a quiz out of 100, the score 78 is 78 percent correct, but its percentile rank is 70; these are different numbers answering different questions. Here is a case where they pull far apart: a score of 41 out of 50 is 82 percent correct, and if 18 of 20 classmates scored 41 or lower, its percentile rank is 90. The first number compares the student to the quiz; the second compares the student to the group. Move the same student into a different group and the percentile rank changes while the score does not.
🤝 A COURTESY TO THE DATA AND THE READER
A percentile rank describes where a value sits in this group. It says nothing about the worth of the person who produced it, and it can shift when the group changes. State the group (“among these 20 scores”) every time you quote one. Leaving the group out is a quiet way to make a number sound more final than it is.
✂️ QUARTILES: CUTTING THE ORDERED DATA INTO FOUR PARTS
The three most useful percentiles get their own names. The median (the middle value, defined earlier this week) is the 50th percentile. The first quartile, Q1, is about the 25th percentile, and the third quartile, Q3, is about the 75th. Together the median and the two quartiles cut the ordered data into four parts holding about a quarter of the values each.
The by-hand method: the median of each half.
▶ Order the data from smallest to largest. (Skipping this step is the most common way to get a wrong answer.)
▶ Find the median of the whole list.
▶ Split the ordered list into a lower half and an upper half. If the number of values is even, split it down the middle. If it is odd, the median is a single value in the middle: leave it out of both halves.
▶ Q1 is the median of the lower half. Q3 is the median of the upper half.
Worked example 3 — odd n. Nine students (made up) report the minutes they read yesterday: 15, 9, 30, 5, 12, 19, 8, 22, 14. Order: 5, 8, 9, 12, 14, 15, 19, 22, 30. There are 9 values, so the median is the 5th: 14. Because 9 is odd, 14 is left out of both halves. The lower half is the four values before it, 5, 8, 9, 12, and its median is the average of the middle two: \(\dfrac{8 + 9}{2} = 8.5\), so Q1 = 8.5. The upper half is the four values after it, 15, 19, 22, 30, with median \(\dfrac{19 + 22}{2} = 20.5\), so Q3 = 20.5.

The five-number summary lists the five values that outline a dataset: the minimum, Q1, the median, Q3, and the maximum. For the nine reading times it is 5, 8.5, 14, 20.5, 30; for the ten puzzle times it is 3, 6, 10, 15, 25. (Next lesson uses Q3 − Q1, the interquartile range, to measure spread.)
📐 BUILDING A BOX PLOT BY HAND
A box plot draws the five-number summary on a number line. Do it in this order:
▶ Draw a number line that covers the minimum to the maximum, with equal spacing and a few labeled tick marks.
▶ Above (or beside) the line, draw a box from Q1 to Q3.
▶ Draw a line inside the box at the median.
▶ Draw whiskers: a line from the box out to the minimum on the left and out to the maximum on the right. (For now the whiskers always reach the minimum and the maximum. Lesson 22.3 shows what to do with values that sit very far from the rest.)
▶ Title it, say what is measured, and say it is constructed data.

In the puzzle times the median (10) sits closer to Q1 (6) than to Q3 (15), so the values between the median and Q3 are spread over more room than those between Q1 and the median. The picture carries information that the five numbers hold only implicitly.
👁️ READING A BOX PLOT
Each of the four parts of a box plot — the left whisker, the left half of the box, the right half of the box, and the right whisker — holds about a quarter of the data. Take twelve (made-up) commute minutes: 14, 16, 18, 21, 23, 24, 27, 30, 31, 35, 38, 42. By halves, Q1 = 19.5, the median = 25.5, and Q3 = 33. Count them: three values fall below Q1 (14, 16, 18), three between Q1 and the median (21, 23, 24), three between the median and Q3 (27, 30, 31), and three above Q3 (35, 38, 42).
Twelve Commute Times (constructed data): three values in each quarter
Each quarter of the picture holds 3 of the 12 values, even though the quarters have different widths. Horizontal axis: minutes.
A wide quarter does not hold more data. It holds the same amount, spread over more room. That is how a box plot shows spread: a wide box means the middle half of the data is spread out; a narrow box means it is packed together. Two made-up sets of twelve values share a median of 50:

Set P's middle half is packed into 10 units (45 to 55); Set Q's spreads across 35 units (32.5 to 67.5). Horizontal axis: value.
What a box plot cannot show is the shape inside the box. Last week's widget had two datasets with the same five-number summary and very different histograms; you will see them again below. A box plot is a summary, and a summary always leaves something out. Saying so is part of describing data fairly.
⚖️ WHEN EXCEL DISAGREES: A CONVENTION, NOT A MISTAKE
Excel's formula for quartiles is =QUARTILE.INC(range, quart), where quart is 1 for Q1, 2 for the median, and 3 for Q3. (The “INC” stands for inclusive.) For some datasets it gives the same Q1 and Q3 as your hand method; for many it gives a slightly different answer.
The reason: there is more than one reasonable way to cut ordered data at the 25th and 75th percentiles. Excel's method places Q1 at position \(1 + 0.25(n - 1)\) in the ordered list and Q3 at position \(1 + 0.75(n - 1)\), and when that position falls between two values it goes part of the way from one to the next. Your hand method instead takes the median of each half. Neither is wrong.
Same nine reading times, both methods. With \(n = 9\), Q1 sits at position \(1 + 0.25 \times 8 = 3\), which is the 3rd value, 9. Q3 sits at position \(1 + 0.75 \times 8 = 7\), the 7th value, 19. So Excel reports Q1 = 9 and Q3 = 19, while the by-hand method gave 8.5 and 20.5.

Same ten puzzle times. With \(n = 10\) the positions are \(1 + 0.25 \times 9 = 3.25\) and \(1 + 0.75 \times 9 = 7.75\). Position 3.25 is a quarter of the way from the 3rd value to the 4th: \(6 + 0.25 \times (7 - 6) = 6.25\). Position 7.75 is three quarters of the way from the 7th to the 8th: \(12 + 0.75 \times (15 - 12) = 14.25\). Excel reports Q1 = 6.25 and Q3 = 14.25; the hand method gave 6 and 15.
A fact worth knowing. When the number of values is odd, Excel's answer is the same as you would get by keeping the median in both halves. Some textbooks teach that version. We leave the median out, so our hand answers for odd n can differ from Excel's, as they did above. For large datasets the differences shrink, and sometimes the two agree exactly, as in the 33-value pair in the widget below, where repeated values sit near the quartiles.
Which do we use? In this course, by hand means the median-of-each-half method, and Excel means QUARTILE.INC. A question or assignment says which one it wants; when it does not say, use by hand. When you report quartiles outside this course, say which convention you used, the way you would name the units.
🤝 A COURTESY TO THE READER
Two honest people can compute different quartiles from the same data. The courteous thing is to name the method and the number of values (n) next to the result, so a reader who gets a different answer knows to check the convention before doubting you.

💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · DESMOS FOR THE ARITHMETIC, EXCEL FOR THE LISTS
Desmos Scientific (plain expressions only). The average of the middle pair in a half: (8+9)/2 → 8.5. Going part of the way between two values (the QUARTILE.INC idea): 5+0.25*(7-5) → 5.5. A percentile rank: 14/20*100 → 70. Desmos Scientific does not do list statistics, so the sorting and counting are yours; Excel handles the lists.

Excel. =MIN(range), =MAX(range), =MEDIAN(range), =QUARTILE.INC(range,1) and =QUARTILE.INC(range,3). Excel does not need the data sorted. The full click-by-click steps are in the next section.
💻 EXCEL: THE FIVE-NUMBER SUMMARY IN FIVE FORMULAS
Keep this page open beside Excel. You will enter the nine reading times, ask Excel for the five-number summary, and compare it with your hand answers. Excel work happens outside Canvas.
Part A — enter the data.
▶ Open Excel and start a Blank workbook. Click cell A1, type Minutes, and press Enter.
▶ Click A2 and type the nine values, pressing Enter after each, in this order: 15, 9, 30, 5, 12, 19, 8, 22, 14. They end in A10. (They are not in order; that is fine.)
Part B — the five formulas.
▶ Click C1, type Statistic, press Tab, type Excel, press Tab, type By hand, press Tab, type Difference, press Enter.
▶ Click C2 and type the labels down column C, pressing Enter after each: Minimum, Q1, Median, Q3, Maximum (C2 to C6).
▶ Click D2, type =MIN(A2:A10), and press Enter. You should see 5.
▶ Click D3, type =QUARTILE.INC(A2:A10,1), and press Enter. You should see 9.
▶ Click D4, type =MEDIAN(A2:A10), and press Enter. You should see 14.
▶ Click D5, type =QUARTILE.INC(A2:A10,3), and press Enter. You should see 19.
▶ Click D6, type =MAX(A2:A10), and press Enter. You should see 30.
Part C — compare with your hand work.
▶ Click E2 and type your hand five-number summary down column E, one value per cell, in the same order: 5, 8.5, 14, 20.5, 30.
▶ Click F2, type =D2-E2, and press Enter. Click F2 again, then drag the small square at its lower right corner (the fill handle) down to F6.
▶ Read column F (Excel minus hand). A 0 means the two methods agree. You should see 0, 0.5, 0, −1.5, 0: Excel and the hand method agree on the minimum, median, and maximum and differ on Q1 and Q3.
▶ Click C8 and type one sentence naming both methods and why they differ (for example: “Q1 and Q3 differ because Excel interpolates by position and my hand method takes the median of each half; neither is a mistake.”).
Part D — a percentile rank. Click C10 and type Rank of 14. Click D10 and type =COUNTIF(A2:A10,"<=14")/COUNT(A2:A10)*100, then press Enter. COUNTIF counts the cells at or below 14 (there are 5) and COUNT counts all the numbers (9), so you get \(\dfrac{5}{9} \times 100 \approx 55.6\). Two quick checks: =QUARTILE.INC(A2:A10,0) gives the minimum and =QUARTILE.INC(A2:A10,4) the maximum, so both agree with your MIN and MAX.
🤖 AI TRAINING · PROMPT → PREDICT → VERIFY
Prompt. Give an AI tool this list and ask: “What are Q1, the median, and Q3?” — 7, 20, 4, 13, 2, 16, 10, 5, 15, 8. Do not tell it which method to use.
Predict. Before you read its answer, find the quartiles by hand (order first!) and also by the Excel rule. You should get Q1 = 5, median = 9, Q3 = 15 by hand, and Q1 = 5.5, median = 9, Q3 = 14.5 from QUARTILE.INC. Predict which pair the AI will give.
Verify. Which pair did it give, or did it give something else? If it matches one pair, say which convention it used, and ask it to name the method; does its stated method match its numbers? If it matches neither, find the first step where it went wrong (a common slip is not sorting first). The lesson is that a correct-looking answer can still rest on a convention the AI never told you, and a mismatch means check the method before you say “wrong.”
