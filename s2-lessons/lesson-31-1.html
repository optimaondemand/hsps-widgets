Lesson 31.1
Sampling Variation: Many Samples, Many Estimates
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A fair random sample still misses the truth by a little, and the misses follow a pattern we can describe.
Sampling variation; the distribution of many sample proportions (center, shape, spread); bigger samples swing less; sampling variation versus bias; “typical swing” and “within 5 points of the truth”
Reading & work: Read every section in order, work each example on paper before you read its solution, use the Sample Variation interactive, follow the Excel steps, and complete the three activities at the bottom. Plan on about 80 minutes.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain that different random samples from the same population give different statistics (sampling variation), even when the sampling method is completely fair.
2
I will be able to describe the distribution of many sample proportions: where it is centered, what shape it has, and how spread out it is.
3
I will be able to show that a larger sample gives less spread (about half the swing when n is four times as big), using the typical swing and the share of estimates within 5 points of the truth.
4
I will be able to distinguish sampling variation (chance; it shrinks as n grows) from bias (a lean in the method; it does not shrink as n grows).

✨ A WONDER TO START WITH
Two friends each toss a fair coin 10 times. One gets 3 heads and the other gets 6. Nobody cheated, and the coin did not change. Chance alone made the two results differ.
Sampling works the same way. Last week you learned that a random sample is a fair way to choose people, and that one fair sample can still miss the truth. Today we ask the next question: how far does a fair sample usually miss, and what happens to the misses when we draw many samples, or bigger ones? It turns out the misses are not chaos. They pile up in a shape you can see, measure, and shrink.
Responsibility, this module’s virtue, looks like this: when you report a number from a sample, you owe your reader an honest sense of how much that number could have moved. Reporting a single figure as though it were certain is a small act of overclaiming.
A note on honesty. Every population, percent, and sample in this lesson is constructed (made up) or simulated by a computer to teach the idea. None of it is real survey data.
🎲 ONE POPULATION, MANY SAMPLES
Imagine a made-up school where exactly 40% of the students walk to school. That 40% is a parameter: one fixed number describing the whole population. Now a student council member takes a simple random sample of 25 students and records the sample proportion (the fraction in the sample who walk). That is a statistic.
If she repeats the whole process with a fresh random sample of 25, she will almost surely get a different proportion. A third sample gives a third value. This sample-to-sample difference in a statistic is called sampling variation. It is caused by chance in who landed in the sample, and it happens even though:
▶ the population did not change (it is still 40%),
▶ the method was random, and
▶ nobody made a mistake.
So sampling variation is not an error to be embarrassed about, and it is not bias. It is the normal behavior of a fair method. The honest question is not “will my sample hit the truth exactly?” but “how far is it likely to land?” To answer that, we need to see what happens across many samples.

Center. The 20 counts add to 194, so the mean of the 20 proportions is 194 ÷ (20 × 25) = 194 ÷ 500 = 0.388. That is close to the truth, 0.40, though not equal to it. (Only 20 samples is a small pile, so we expect the mean to be near 0.40 but not on it.)

Within 5 points. Which estimates landed within 5 percentage points of the truth, that is, between 0.35 and 0.45? Only the values 0.36, 0.40, and 0.44 (k = 9, 10, 11). Counting: 2 + 2 + 3 = 7 of 20, or 35%. So 13 of the 20 fair samples missed by more than 5 points.
Answer. In this simulated run: mean 0.388 (near 0.40), typical swing about 0.101, range 0.24 to 0.60, and 7 of 20 estimates within 5 points. None of these 20 fair samples was “wrong.” The swing is what chance does. A second run would give different numbers; the theory below says the typical swing for n = 25 is about 0.098, close to what we saw.
💭 CENTER, SHAPE, AND SPREAD OF THE PILE
When we collect many sample proportions from the same population and the same sample size, the pile of values (a distribution of estimates) has three features to describe, just like any data display.
▶ Center. The pile is centered near the true value. Fair random sampling does not lean high or low, so over many samples the highs and lows balance. (With only 20 samples, the mean is near the truth but not exactly on it, as in Worked Example 1.)
▶ Shape. The pile is roughly symmetric and bell-shaped when the sample is reasonably large and the true proportion is not too close to 0 or 1: a tall middle and thinner tails on both sides. With only 20 dots the picture is lumpy; with hundreds of samples the bell shape becomes clear. You will see this in the interactive.
▶ Spread. The typical swing tells how wide the pile is. It is the number that answers “how far does one fair sample usually land from the truth?”
Notice what the pile does not do. It does not collapse onto one value. Even a perfect method produces a spread, and that spread is a property of the method and the sample size, not of anyone’s carelessness.

📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 2: FOUR TIMES AS BIG, HALF THE SWING
Problem. The interactive ran 20,000 simulated random samples at each of three sample sizes from a made-up population with true proportion p = 0.40. The measured typical swings were 0.0984 (n = 25), 0.0489 (n = 100), and 0.0246 (n = 400). Compare them with the formula √(p(1 − p) ÷ n), and describe what happens each time the sample size is multiplied by 4.
Teacher’s voice: Here p(1 − p) = 0.4 × 0.6 = 0.24. For n = 25 the formula gives √(0.24 ÷ 25) = √0.0096 = 0.0980. For n = 100 it gives √(0.24 ÷ 100) = √0.0024 = 0.0490. For n = 400 it gives √(0.24 ÷ 400) = √0.0006 = 0.0245. These match the simulated values to about three decimals, so the formula and the simulation agree.

Each time n was multiplied by 4, the typical swing was cut to about half. That is no accident: n sits under a square root, and √4 = 2. To halve the swing you need four times as many people. To cut it to a third you would need nine times as many.
Answer. Larger samples swing less, but with diminishing returns: going from 25 to 100 people (75 more) lowers the typical swing by about 0.049, while going from 100 to 400 (300 more) lowers it by only about 0.025. The formula is a tool for reading the pattern; the evidence is the simulation, which you can run yourself.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 3: READING “TYPICAL SWING” AND “WITHIN 5 POINTS”
Problem. Using the same made-up population (p = 0.40), the interactive reports that about 46% of estimates at n = 25, about 74% at n = 100, and about 96% at n = 400 land within 5 percentage points of the truth. (a) Say in a sentence what “typical swing 0.049” means for n = 100. (b) A student reads “46% within 5 points at n = 25” and says “so a sample of 25 is correct 46% of the time.” Is that a fair reading? (c) A researcher wants her sample proportion to land within 5 points of the truth about 95% of the time in this population. Which of the three sample sizes comes closest?
Teacher’s voice: (a) A typical swing of 0.049 means that a single random sample of 100 usually lands within roughly 5 percentage points of the truth, give or take: sometimes closer, sometimes farther. It is a size for the usual miss, not a limit that can never be broken.
(b) Not quite. “Within 5 points” is a window around the truth, not a claim of being exactly right. A better sentence is: “If we drew many random samples of 25, about 46% of them would land within 5 points of the true percent, and the other 54% would miss by more.” At n = 25 a miss of more than 5 points is actually more likely than not. The 46% belongs to the whole pile, not to any one sample.
(c) 96% at n = 400 is the one that reaches about 95%. At n = 100 the share is only about 74%, and at n = 25 it is about 46%. Bigger samples buy more confidence that the estimate sits in a narrow window.
Answer. (a) The usual miss is about 0.049, or about 5 points. (b) Not fair as stated; the 46% describes many samples, not the correctness of one. (c) n = 400. Preview. Reporting a window around an estimate, and saying how often such windows catch the truth, is what Module 12 builds into the margin of error. Today you are only seeing where it comes from: the spread of estimates across many samples.
⚠️ SAMPLING VARIATION OR BIAS?
These two ideas both make a sample statistic miss the truth, and they are easy to mix up. Keep them apart.
Sampling variationBias
Where it comes fromChance in who happens to be in the sampleA lean built into the method (who can be chosen, who answers, how the question is worded)
Which way does it push?Both ways, balancing out over many samplesMostly one way, every time
Where is the pile centered?Near the truthAway from the truth
What happens as n grows?The pile gets narrowerThe pile gets narrower but stays off-center
Can a bigger sample fix it?Yes, it shrinksNo
A constructed illustration. Suppose the true proportion is 0.40, but a made-up poll collects answers only from people at a gym, where (we imagine) the chance of a “yes” is 0.60 for everyone asked. A computer simulation of 20,000 polls at each size gives a pile centered near 0.60 at n = 25, 100, and 400, with typical swings of about 0.099, 0.049, and 0.024. The swings shrink exactly as before, yet the center stays about 0.20 above the truth every time. More people, same lean.
This is why last week’s lesson said a larger sample cures chance error but not bias. Today’s interactive shows only fair random sampling, so it shows sampling variation by itself. When you read a claim, ask two questions: how was the sample chosen (bias), and how large was it (sampling variation).
⚠️ TWO COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS
“A good sample would give exactly the true value.” No. A perfectly good random sample of 25 from a 40% population hits exactly 0.40 only about 16% of the time (the exact chance of 10 walkers out of 25 is about 0.16), so most fair samples miss. In Worked Example 1, only 2 of 20 samples landed exactly on 0.40. A miss is a sign of chance, not of a bad sample.
“A bigger population needs a bigger sample.” Mostly not, with a caution. The typical swing depends mainly on the sample size n, not on how large the population is, as long as the sample is a small part of the population. A more exact calculation for p = 0.40 and n = 100 gives a typical swing of about 0.0485 when the population has 5,000 people and about 0.0490 when it has 500,000, nearly the same. (If you sample a large share of a small population, say a third of it, the swing does get smaller; that is not the situation most polls face.) So a poll of one town and a poll of a whole country can use similar sample sizes.
🌎 WHERE YOU MIGHT SEE THIS: THE “PLUS OR MINUS” IN A POLL
News stories about polls often add a “plus or minus” of some percentage points to their results; in general, that figure is there because a different random sample would have given a somewhat different number, which is the sampling variation you just measured. In Module 12 you will learn how that plus-or-minus is built and how to read it honestly.
💻 EXCEL: SIMULATE MANY SAMPLES, CLICK BY CLICK
You will simulate sampling in a spreadsheet. Everything here is made up: a population in which 40% say “yes.” This is a practice build; you do not upload it.
▶ Open Excel and start a Blank workbook. Save it as Wk31_L1_YourName. Rename the first sheet Draws (right-click the sheet tab at the bottom, choose Rename, type the name, press Enter).
▶ In A1 type Sample. In B1 type 25 answers (1 = yes, 0 = no). In AA1 type Proportion. In A2 type 1 and in A3 type 2. Select A2:A3, then drag the small square at the bottom right corner of the selection down to A21. The sample numbers should run 1 to 20.
▶ One person. Click B2 and type =IF(RANDBETWEEN(1,100)<=40,1,0), then press Enter. RANDBETWEEN(1,100) picks a random whole number from 1 to 100. Exactly 40 of those 100 numbers are 40 or less, so the IF gives 1 (a “yes”) with a 40% chance and 0 otherwise. Press F9 (on some laptops Fn + F9) a few times and watch B2 flip between 1 and 0.
▶ One sample of 25. Click B2 and press Ctrl + C. Click the Name Box (the small box left of the formula bar, showing the cell name), type B2:Z2, and press Enter to select those 25 cells. Press Ctrl + V. You now have 25 random answers. Click AA2, type =AVERAGE(B2:Z2), and press Enter. The average of 1s and 0s is the share of 1s, so this is the sample proportion. (=SUM(B2:Z2) would give the count of yeses instead.)
▶ Twenty samples. Click B2, press Ctrl + C, click the Name Box, type B2:Z21, press Enter, and press Ctrl + V. Then click AA2, press Ctrl + C, select AA3:AA21 in the Name Box, and press Ctrl + V. Column AA now holds 20 sample proportions, one per row. Press F9 to see all of them redrawn at once.
▶ Freeze the estimates. Select AA2:AA21 and press Ctrl + C. Click the + beside the sheet tabs to add a sheet and rename it Results. In A1 type Estimate, then right-click A2 and, under Paste Options, choose Values (the clipboard icon marked 123); or use Home > Paste > Paste Special > Values. Click a cell in A and look at the formula bar: it should show a plain number. The Results column no longer changes when you press F9.
▶ Summaries. On the Results sheet, in C1 type Mean and in D1 type =AVERAGE(A2:A21). In C2 type Typical swing and in D2 type =STDEV.S(A2:A21). In C3 type Theory and in D3 type =SQRT(0.4*0.6/25) (this should show 0.0979796…).
▶ Tally with COUNTIFS. In F1 type From, in G1 type Up to (not including), and in H1 type Count. Type the lower edges 0.04, 0.12, 0.20, 0.28, 0.36, 0.44, 0.52, 0.60, 0.68, 0.76 down F2:F11, and the upper edges 0.12, 0.20, 0.28, 0.36, 0.44, 0.52, 0.60, 0.68, 0.76, 0.84 down G2:G11. Click H2 and type =COUNTIFS($A$2:$A$21,">="&F2,$A$2:$A$21,"<"&G2), press Enter, and drag the fill square down to H11. In H12 type =SUM(H2:H11): it must show 20. If it does not, an estimate fell outside the bins or a number was mistyped.
▶ Chart the tallies. Select H1:H11 (the heading and the ten counts). On the Insert tab, in the Charts group, choose Insert Column or Bar Chart > Clustered Column. Right-click the chart, choose Select Data, under Horizontal (Category) Axis Labels click Edit, drag across F2:F11, and click OK twice. Each column is now labeled by the lower edge of its bin. Give the chart a title and axis titles (select the chart, then Chart Design > Add Chart Element > Axis Titles, or use the + button beside the chart).
Check yourself. Your numbers will not match anyone else’s, because the draws are random. But the mean should be near 0.40 (usually between about 0.35 and 0.45), the typical swing should be near 0.098 (usually between about 0.065 and 0.13), and the chart should look like a rough bell around 0.40. Press F9 on the Draws sheet, then freeze again, and watch the numbers change: that is sampling variation in your own workbook.
💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · SIMULATE, TALLY, AND CHART IN EXCEL; THE SWING FORMULA IN DESMOS
Excel. =IF(RANDBETWEEN(1,100)<=40,1,0) simulates one person from a 40% population. Copy it across 25 cells for one sample, and =AVERAGE(...) (or =SUM(...) for the count) gives the sample proportion. Copy the block down for 20 samples. =COUNTIFS(range,">="&low,range,"<"&high) tallies how many estimates fall in a bin, and a Clustered Column chart of the tallies is your histogram. =STDEV.S(range) gives the typical swing of your estimates.
Volatility warning. RANDBETWEEN recalculates whenever anything in the workbook changes. Before you analyze or submit anything, copy the estimates and use Paste Special > Values to freeze them.
Desmos Scientific is plain arithmetic here. For a true proportion of 0.4 and a sample of 25, type sqrt(0.4*0.6/25) and you should get about 0.098. Change 25 to 100 and you should get about 0.049; change it to 400 for about 0.0245. Try sqrt(0.5*0.5/400) for 0.025.
🤖 AI TRAINING · ASK AN AI TO PREDICT THE SWING; CHECK IT, AND FLAG BIAS-VERSUS-VARIATION MIX-UPS
Prompt. Ask an AI tool: “A population has a true proportion p = 0.4. If I take many simple random samples, how much sample-to-sample variation should I expect in the sample proportion at n = 25 and at n = 100? Give numbers and explain.”
Predict. Before you read its answer, decide what a careful answer must say: the pile of sample proportions is centered near 0.40, the typical swing is about 0.098 at n = 25 and about 0.049 at n = 100 (half), and a larger sample shrinks the swing but does nothing about a biased method.
Verify. Open the Sample Variation interactive, set n = 25, press Draw 1000, and read Typical swing (SD); then press Clear, set n = 100, and press Draw 1000 again. Compare with the AI’s numbers. Did the AI get the swing right to within about 0.01? Did it say the swing halves when n is four times as big? Did it confuse sampling variation with bias (for example, calling the spread an “error in the method,” or saying a big sample removes every kind of error)? Rewrite its worst sentence so that it is true. Verdict. A confident tone is not evidence; the simulation is.

Chance or Lean?

1
Decide for each constructed situation whether the miss is mainly sampling variation or bias, and say whether making the sample four times as big would help. (a) Two simple random samples of 50 students from the same made-up school give 38% and 46% who like the new lunch menu. (b) A made-up website asks visitors “Do you love video games?” and 10,000 visitors answer. (c) A reporter surveys only the first 30 people to arrive at a made-up concert about whether they like the band. (d) A random sample of 200 residents says 54% favor a new park; a random sample of 800 residents from the same town says 51%.
2
Your classmate says: “If I make my biased sample 10 times as large, the bias will shrink to one-tenth.” Use the gym illustration above to explain what really happens to the center and the spread.
▶  Check
1. (a) Sampling variation; different random samples differ by chance; more students would shrink the swing. (b) Bias (self-selection; people who love video games are more likely to answer); 10,000 answers do not fix the lean, and four times as many would not help. (c) Bias (convenience; early arrivals may be the biggest fans); a larger group of early arrivals keeps the same lean. (d) Sampling variation; both samples are random, they differ by chance, and the larger one (800) typically sits closer to the truth. 2. Making the sample bigger narrows the spread (the swing shrinks, about half for four times the size), but the center stays where the bias put it. In the gym illustration the swing shrank from about 0.099 to 0.024 while the center stayed near 0.60, about 0.20 above the truth. The bias does not shrink with size.
