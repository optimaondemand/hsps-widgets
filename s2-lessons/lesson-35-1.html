Lesson 35.1
The Logic of Hypothesis Testing: Two Schools Revisited
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A hypothesis test is a decision rule you write down before you see the data, and every rule can fail in exactly two ways.
The null and alternative hypotheses, the significance level, the p-value, the language of “reject” and “fail to reject,” Type I and Type II errors, power, and how the frequentist and Bayesian schools ask different questions
Reading & work: Read every section in order, work each example on paper before you read its solution, follow the Excel steps, and complete the three activities at the bottom. The reading and Excel steps take about 40 minutes and the activities about 35 more, so plan on about 75 minutes in all.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to state a null and an alternative hypothesis, fix the significance level before looking at data, and report a decision in the correct language: reject or fail to reject the null, never “accept” and never “prove.”
2
I will be able to define a Type I error (a false alarm) and a Type II error (a miss), and compute the Type I rate and the power of a coin-flip decision rule with exact binomial arithmetic or Excel.
3
I will be able to explain how sample size, the size of the real effect, and the significance level change the power of a test.
4
I will be able to say what a frequentist test and a Bayesian analysis each answer, and why the two can disagree, as in Lindley’s paradox.

✨ A WONDER TO START WITH
Before the season starts, a made-up school referee, Ms. Okoro, must decide whether to keep using the old school coin. She cannot flip forever, so she writes a rule on an index card before she flips: “Flip 100 times. If I see 61 or more heads, or 39 or fewer, I will replace the coin. Otherwise I keep it.” Then she flips and gets 60 heads. Keep the coin or replace it? By her own card, she keeps it, even though 60 heads looks suspicious. Why did she write the rule first? And how often will this rule lead her to throw away a perfectly good coin, or to keep a bad one?
Those two questions are the heart of today. In Week 34 you asked, how surprising is this result if nothing is going on? You did it by shuffling and by simulating fair coins. Today we put that habit into its formal skeleton, with names for each part, a rule fixed in advance, and an honest count of the ways the rule can fail. Then we step back and ask what the test does not tell us, and how that connects to the two schools of probability you met in Semester 1.
Service, this module’s virtue, shows up in the rule on the card. A rule written in advance protects the people who depend on your decision (a team, a council, a clinic) from a decision bent by what you hoped to find. And knowing the two ways a rule can fail lets you tell them honestly what a “keep” or a “replace” is worth.
A note on honesty. Every coin, spinner, group, and study in this lesson is constructed (made up) to teach the idea. Nothing here is real data.
📐 THE SKELETON OF A HYPOTHESIS TEST
A hypothesis test is the same set of moves you used in Week 34, with each part given a name and the rule fixed ahead of time. Here is the whole skeleton.
▶ 1. State the claims. The null hypothesis (written H0) is the boring world: no difference, no effect, a fair coin. The alternative hypothesis (written Ha) is what you suspect instead: the coin is not fair, the treatment does change something.
▶ 2. Fix the rule before the data. Choose the significance level (the usual 5%), and whether you will count extremes on one side or both, before you look. The significance level is the false-alarm rate you are willing to accept.
▶ 3. Collect the data and compute the statistic. A count of heads, a difference of means. (The data must come from a random sample or a random assignment, or the test has nothing to stand on.)
▶ 4. Find the p-value. By simulation, as you did in Week 34, or by exact arithmetic with the binomial. It is the share of boring worlds at least as extreme as your data.
▶ 5. Decide. If the p-value is below the significance level, reject the null. If not, fail to reject the null.
▶ 6. Say it in words, with the size of the effect, not only the verdict.
SettingNull hypothesis (boring world)Alternative hypothesisStatistic
A coin (the referee)The coin is fair: heads has chance 0.5The coin is not fair: heads has some other chanceNumber of heads in 100 flips
The made-up plant experiment from Week 34The fertilizer does nothing, so the group labels are arbitraryThe fertilizers lead to different mean growthObserved difference in means (B minus A)
For the plant data you found a shuffle share of about 0.001. With the level fixed at 5% in advance, that is below the level, so the decision is reject the null. In words: if the fertilizer did nothing, shuffling alone would almost never produce a gap this big, so chance alone is a poor explanation for the 1.39 cm gap.
The p-value rule and the cutoff rule are the same rule. For 100 flips of a fair coin, 60 heads (or 40) has a two-sided p-value of 0.0569, which is not below 0.05. But 61 heads (or 39) has a two-sided p-value of 0.0352, which is below it. So the 5% test for 100 flips is exactly the card the referee wrote: reject if 61 or more heads, or 39 or fewer. A cutoff rule is just a p-value rule written as a count you can check in advance. The referee’s 60 heads fails to reach it, which matches the p-value of 0.057 you saw in Week 34.
⚖️ THE LANGUAGE OF A DECISION
The two possible verdicts are worded with care, and the care matters.
▶ Reject the null means: the data would be very surprising in the boring world, so we have good reason to doubt it. It does not mean the null is proved false.
▶ Fail to reject the null means: the data are not surprising enough in the boring world to rule it out. It does not mean the null is true. A small or noisy study often fails to reject a null that is really false.
▶ Never say “accept the null” and never say “prove.” A test of one dataset can only be strong or weak evidence, never a proof in either direction.
Think of a courtroom verdict of “not guilty.” It says the evidence did not clear the bar. It does not say the person is innocent. “Fail to reject” is the same kind of statement.
What the p-value is, in one more line. You met this in Week 17 and again in Week 34: it is how often boring randomness alone would produce a result at least this extreme. It is not the probability that the null is true, not the probability that you are wrong, and not the size of the effect. Hold on to that sentence; it comes back when we compare the two schools.
❌ TWO KINDS OF MISTAKES
Any rule that decides from a random sample can fail in exactly two ways. Look at the two things that might be true about the world and the two things you might decide.
Decision: reject the nullDecision: fail to reject the null
The null is really true (the coin is fair)Type I error: a false alarm. You reject a true null.Correct: you keep a good coin.
The null is really false (the coin is biased)Correct: you catch the biased coin. The chance of this is the power.Type II error: a miss. You fail to reject a false null.
▶ The long-run rate of Type I errors, when the null is true, is the chance that the rule rejects. If you fix the significance level at 5%, this rate is at most 5%. (For coins it is often a little under, because heads come in whole numbers.) This is why the level is called the false-alarm rate.
▶ The Type II rate is the chance the rule misses a real effect. The power of a test is 1 minus the Type II rate: the chance it rejects when the null is false.
▶ Power is not one number for a test. It depends on how the null is false. A coin that favors heads a lot is easy to catch; a coin that favors heads a little is hard. So we compute power for a particular true value.
You cannot see the Type I or Type II rate from your data. They describe how the rule would behave over many possible samples, which is why we compute them from the model, before collecting anything.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 1: THE REFEREE’S RULE AND ITS FALSE-ALARM RATE
Problem. The referee’s rule: flip 100 times; reject the fair-coin null if there are 61 or more heads or 39 or fewer. Find the Type I error rate, and decide what to do with 60 heads and with 62 heads.
Teacher’s voice: The Type I rate is the chance the rule rejects when the coin is fair. So we use p = 0.5. The rule rejects on two tails: 61 or more heads, or 39 or fewer. By exact binomial arithmetic, the chance of 61 or more heads is 0.0176, and by symmetry the chance of 39 or fewer is also 0.0176. Add them: the Type I rate is 0.0352, about 3.5 in 100. In Excel: =1-BINOM.DIST(60,100,0.5,TRUE)+BINOM.DIST(39,100,0.5,TRUE). Read it in two pieces: 1-BINOM.DIST(60,100,0.5,TRUE) is the chance of 61 or more, and BINOM.DIST(39,100,0.5,TRUE) is the chance of 39 or fewer.
For the two observed results: 60 heads is not in the rejection region, so fail to reject: this evidence is not strong enough to call the coin unfair. 62 heads is in the region (the two-sided p-value is 0.0210), so reject the null: a fair coin would do this only about 2 times in 100.
Notice. The rule is allowed under a 5% level because its Type I rate, 0.0352, is below 0.05. Widening the region to 60 or more or 40 or fewer would give a Type I rate of 0.0569, which is above 5%, so that card would break the referee’s own limit.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 2: HOW GOOD IS THE RULE AT CATCHING A BAD COIN?
Problem. Use the same rule (61 or more heads or 39 or fewer in 100 flips). Find the chance it rejects if the coin’s true chance of heads is 0.55, 0.6, and 0.7. Give the power and the Type II rate for 0.6 and 0.7.
Teacher’s voice: Same rule, same two tails, but now we compute the rejection chance with the true heads chance in place of 0.5. Excel pattern: =1-BINOM.DIST(60,100,0.6,TRUE)+BINOM.DIST(39,100,0.6,TRUE) for a true chance of 0.6. Here are the exact results.

0.50 (fair coin)0.0352Type I rate (the null is true)

Read the table honestly. If the coin really lands heads 70% of the time, the rule almost always catches it (power 0.979). If the coin lands heads 60% of the time, the rule misses it more than half the time (a Type II rate of 0.5379). A 60% coin is clearly biased, yet 100 flips and this rule fail to detect it more often than not. Power depends on the size of the real effect: the bigger the real gap from the null, the easier it is to catch.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 3: MORE FLIPS, MORE POWER
Problem. Compare three rules, each built to keep the Type I rate near 4%: 25 flips (reject at 18 or more heads or 7 or fewer), 100 flips (61 or more or 39 or fewer), and 400 flips (221 or more or 179 or fewer). How does the number of flips change the power against a coin with heads chance 0.6 and against one with 0.7?

Teacher’s voice: The false-alarm rate stays near 4% in every row, because we built the rules that way. Only the power moves. With 25 flips, a 60% coin is caught only about 15 times in 100. With 100 flips, about 46 in 100. With 400 flips, about 98 in 100. More flips means less chance scatter in the percent of heads, so a real gap stands out. This is the same shrinking-spread pattern you saw with standard error. It is why planning a study means choosing n large enough to have decent power for an effect worth finding, before collecting data.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 4: THE TRADE BETWEEN THE TWO ERRORS
Problem. Keep 100 flips and the true chance of heads at 0.6. What happens to power if the referee demands fewer false alarms, or accepts more?

Teacher’s voice: Read down the table. A stricter rule makes false alarms rarer (0.0066) but misses more real bias (power drops to 0.2386). A looser rule catches more bias (0.5433) but raises false alarms to 0.0569, above the 5% limit. You cannot lower both errors at once with the same data. To lower both, you need more data. So the level is a judgment about costs: if a false alarm is very costly (replacing something expensive, or approving a bad treatment), choose a stricter level; if a miss is very costly, accept a looser one and plan a bigger sample. That is a decision for the people the test serves, and it has to be made before the flipping starts.
🎲 TWO SCHOOLS, REVISITED
In Semester 1 you met two ways of understanding probability. The frequentist school treats probability as a long-run frequency; a hypothesis is either true or false, so it does not get a probability. The Bayesian school treats probability as a degree of belief; a hypothesis can get a probability, but only after you choose a prior to start from. A hypothesis test as we have built it today is a frequentist tool. Put the two side by side and the difference is plain.
The frequentist testThe Bayesian analysis
The question it answersHow surprising are these data if the boring world were true?How probable is the hypothesis, given the data and what I believed before?
What it needsA null model and a rule fixed in advanceA prior, and a model for the alternatives too
What it reportsA p-value, a decision, and the error rates of the ruleA probability for the hypothesis (a posterior)
What it cannot sayThe probability that the null is trueAnything that does not depend on the prior you chose
So when a p-value is 0.03, the frequentist can say only: if nothing were going on, results this extreme would occur about 3 times in 100. The sentence “there is a 3% chance that nothing is going on” is a Bayesian-style claim, and it needs a prior to make. A p-value cannot supply one.
Where they can disagree: Lindley’s paradox. Recall the florist and her tulip bulbs from Week 17. The shipment is either the 25%-red kind or the 50%-red kind, equally likely before looking. She plants 48 bulbs and 36 come up red. Test the 25% hypothesis: the expected count is 12 with standard deviation 3, and 36 is 8 standard deviations above, so a significance test rejects it. Test the 50% hypothesis: the expected count is 24 with standard deviation about 3.46, and 36 is about 3.5 standard deviations above, so a significance test rejects that one too. A faithful use of the test at the 1% level rejects both possible shipments. The Bayesian compares them instead: the data are about 5 hundred million times (5 × 108) more probable under the 50% hypothesis, and with equal priors that puts the probability of the 50% shipment above 0.999999998. The test asked, “is this surprising under this one hypothesis?” The Bayesian asked, “which of the possibilities explains the data better?” Those are different questions, and they can give different answers.
The honest conclusion is not that one school wins. They ask different questions. A test gives you a disciplined rule with known error rates and needs no prior, which makes it easy to share and check. A Bayesian analysis answers the question people usually want answered (how likely is the hypothesis?) but its answer depends on a prior that reasonable people may set differently. Both are honest when used honestly: when you say which question you asked, what you assumed, and what the answer does not tell you.
📜 A MOMENT IN THE HISTORY OF MATHEMATICS · TESTING AS A DECISION RULE: NEYMAN AND PEARSON
In the 1930s, Jerzy Neyman and Egon Pearson wrote about testing as a decision rule. In their way of thinking, you fix the rule and the two kinds of error before collecting data, and you judge the rule by how often it goes wrong in the long run. That is the Type I and Type II language you used today. It differs from the view of Ronald Fisher, whom you met with the lady tasting tea in Week 17, who treated the p-value as a measure of the strength of evidence against the null rather than as part of a decision rule. Historians describe the three men as having disagreed about how testing should be understood. Today the two approaches are often mixed: a p-value is reported (a Fisher-style idea) and compared with a fixed cutoff such as 5%, while talk of false alarms and power comes from the decision-rule side. Many courses present the blend as one method, which can hide that it began as different ideas answering different questions. This is a short sketch only; historians would add many names and details.

▶ Open Excel and start a Blank workbook. Save it as Wk35_L1_YourName. Rename the first sheet RulePower (right-click the sheet tab at the bottom, choose Rename, type the name, press Enter).
▶ The rule. In A1 type Flips n and in B1 type 100. In A2 type Upper cutoff and in B2 type 61. In A3 type Lower cutoff and in B3 type =B1-B2 (it should show 39; a symmetric rule keeps the two tails balanced).
▶ The table. In A5 type True chance of heads, in B5 type Chance the rule rejects, and in C5 type Type II rate. In A6:A9 type the four numbers 0.5, 0.55, 0.6, 0.7 (one per cell).
▶ The exact tail formula. Click B6 and type =1-BINOM.DIST($B$2-1,$B$1,A6,TRUE)+BINOM.DIST($B$3,$B$1,A6,TRUE), then press Enter. The first piece is the chance of at least the upper cutoff (one less than the cutoff goes inside, because BINOM.DIST with TRUE gives “that many or fewer”). The second piece is the chance of at most the lower cutoff. The dollar signs keep the rule cells fixed when you copy down.
▶ Click B6, then drag its small square handle (bottom right corner) down to B9 to copy the formula. Click C7 and type =1-B7; copy it down to C9. (Row 6 is the null world, so it has a Type I rate, not a Type II rate; leave C6 empty.)
▶ Check yourself. No random numbers are involved, so everyone’s results should match. B6 ≈ 0.0352 (the Type I rate); B7 ≈ 0.1352; B8 ≈ 0.4621 (C8 ≈ 0.5379); B9 ≈ 0.9790 (C9 ≈ 0.0210). If B6 is far off, the usual culprits are putting 61 instead of 60 inside the first BINOM.DIST, or leaving out TRUE.
▶ Change the rule. Set B1 to 400 and B2 to 221. B3 becomes 179. You should get B6 ≈ 0.0402 and B8 ≈ 0.9762. Then set B1 to 25 and B2 to 18 (B3 becomes 7): B6 ≈ 0.0433 and B8 ≈ 0.1548. Last, return to 100 and try B2 = 64 (B6 ≈ 0.0066, B8 ≈ 0.2386) and B2 = 60 (B6 ≈ 0.0569, B8 ≈ 0.5433). These are the rows of Worked Examples 3 and 4.
💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · EXACT TAIL PROBABILITIES IN EXCEL; HAND-LOGIC ARITHMETIC IN DESMOS
Excel. =BINOM.DIST(k, n, p, TRUE) gives the chance of k or fewer successes. So the chance of at least k is =1-BINOM.DIST(k-1, n, p, TRUE) (one less than k inside). For a two-tail rejection rule with upper cutoff u and lower cutoff l: =1-BINOM.DIST(u-1,n,p,TRUE)+BINOM.DIST(l,n,p,TRUE). With p = 0.5 this is the Type I rate; with another true p it is the power.
Desmos Scientific handles plain arithmetic only here. For a tiny hand-countable rule, the Type I rate is a count divided by a power of 2: type 22 ÷ 1024 to get 0.0215 (Activity 2). A Type II rate is one minus the power: type 1 − 0.4621 to get 0.5379. Desmos Scientific does not give binomial tail areas in this course, so we use Excel for those.
🤖 AI TRAINING · ASK FOR TWO EXPLANATIONS OF ONE TEST, THEN GRADE BOTH
Prompt. Ask an AI tool: “A coin landed heads 62 times in 100 flips. Explain what a hypothesis test says about whether the coin is fair, first the way a frequentist would, and then the way a Bayesian would. Keep each explanation to four sentences.”
Predict. Before you read its answer, write your own two explanations in two sentences each. For the frequentist one, use a boring world and the words “if the coin were fair.” For the Bayesian one, say what the analysis needs before it can give a probability (a prior). Recall that the exact two-sided p-value for 62 of 100 is 0.0210.
Verify. Grade each of the AI’s two explanations from 0 to 3, one point for each of these checks. Frequentist: (1) it describes the p-value as how surprising the data are if the coin is fair, (2) it does not call the p-value the probability that the coin is fair, (3) it uses the language “reject” or “fail to reject,” never “prove” or “accept.” Bayesian: (1) it names a prior, (2) it says the answer is a probability for the hypothesis (not a p-value), (3) it admits that the answer changes with the prior. Notice whether the AI blends the two schools into one muddled paragraph, or quotes a single number such as “98% chance the coin is biased” without naming a prior; ask it what prior it assumed. Rewrite the weakest sentence so that it is true. Verdict. Two fluent explanations are not two correct ones; your checklist is the referee.

Type I and Power from a Rule
15 min
1
By hand logic. A made-up game tosses a fair coin 10 times and rejects “the coin is fair” if it sees 9 or 10 heads, or 0 or 1 heads. There are 1,024 equally likely sequences of 10 tosses. Using the counts 1 sequence with 10 heads and 10 sequences with exactly 9 heads (and the mirror images for 0 and 1 heads), find the Type I rate. Give it as a fraction and a decimal.
2
Power by hand. If the coin truly lands heads with chance 0.9, the chance of 10 heads is 0.910 = 0.3487 and of exactly 9 heads is 10 × 0.99 × 0.1 = 0.3874. The chance of 0 or 1 heads is under 0.0000001, so ignore it. Estimate the power and the Type II rate.
3
With Excel. A made-up spinner is supposed to land on red half the time. The rule for 40 spins is: reject if 27 or more reds, or 13 or fewer. Use your RulePower sheet (B1 = 40, B2 = 27) to find the Type I rate and the power if the true chance of red is 0.7. State the Type II rate. Then try B2 = 26 and say why that rule would break a 5% limit.
▶  Check
1. The sequences with 9 or 10 heads number 10 + 1 = 11, and the sequences with 0 or 1 heads number 1 + 10 = 11, so 22 of 1,024 sequences reject: 22/1024 = 0.0215. 2. Power ≈ 0.3487 + 0.3874 = 0.7361 (exact 0.7361), so the Type II rate is 1 − 0.7361 = 0.2639: the rule misses a 90% coin about 26 times in 100. 3. Type I rate = 0.0385 (about 4 in 100); power at 0.7 = 0.7033; Type II rate = 1 − 0.7033 = 0.2967, so about 3 in 10 real 70% spinners are missed. With B2 = 26 (B3 becomes 14) the Type I rate is 0.0807, which is above 0.05, so that rule would break a 5% limit even though it catches more (power 0.8075 at 0.7).

Two Schools, One Result
10 min
1
A made-up coin gives 62 heads in 100 flips. The exact two-sided p-value for a fair coin is 0.0210. Write one sentence a frequentist would stand behind about what 0.0210 means.
2
A Bayesian wants to say how probable it is that the coin is biased. Name two things the Bayesian needs to decide before giving a number.
3
Error analysis. A student writes: “The p-value of 0.0210 means a 2.1% chance the coin is fair, so both schools agree the coin is 97.9% likely to be biased.” Find two mistakes and say why a test and a Bayesian analysis can answer differently.
▶  Check
1. If the coin were fair, 100 flips would give 62 or more heads, or 38 or fewer, only about 2 times in 100 (0.0210). 2. A prior (how plausible a biased coin was before flipping) and what “biased” means (which heads chances count, and how likely each one was). 3. Mistake one: the p-value is not the probability that the coin is fair; it is computed assuming the coin is fair.
