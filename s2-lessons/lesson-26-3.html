✨ ORDER, TESTED
Over this module you have watched a curve with two numbers, a mean and a standard deviation, stand in for a whole population. Order seemed to rise out of randomness: heights, fill weights, and even the counts from many coin flips gathered into the same bell. That is a real wonder, and it is worth something only if we use it carefully.
Today is a lab. You will take the toolbox from Week 25 (z-scores, the Empirical Rule, NORM.DIST, NORM.INV, NORM.S.DIST, STANDARDIZE) and the two habits from this week (checking whether a normal model fits, as in Lesson 26.1, and the binomial-normal approximation with its continuity correction and its np and n(1 − p) check, as in Lesson 26.2) and put them to work in three short investigations: a measurement in a factory, a pair of test scores, and a survey count. 📐 THE LAB ROUTINE: FOUR MOVES EVERY TIME
Every part of today follows the same four moves. Say them aloud until they are a habit.
▶ 1. Name the model. What is being measured or counted, and what are the mean and SD (or n and p)?
▶ 2. Check the conditions. Is a normal curve plausible for this measurement (Lesson 26.1)? For a count, are np and n(1 − p) both at least 10 (Lesson 26.2)?
▶ 3. Compute. Use a z-score, NORM.DIST, NORM.INV, or the continuity correction, and sense-check the size of the answer against the Empirical Rule.
▶ 4. State it as an estimate, and name what could break it. One sentence: fit, independence, or random sampling.
The workbook you build has one sheet for each part (Tolerance, Scores, Survey) and a fourth, Notes, for the “what would make this wrong” sentences. Rounding in this lesson: areas to four decimals, percents to one decimal, cutoffs to two decimals, counts to whole numbers.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE, PART 1 — A MANUFACTURING TOLERANCE
Constructed scenario, not real data. A made-up shop cuts bolts whose lengths are modeled by a normal curve with mean 25.00 mm and SD 0.20 mm. A bolt is in specification if its length is between 24.70 mm and 25.30 mm. A batch has N = 2500 bolts.
▶ Name the model and check. Lengths of cut bolts are a continuous measurement, and the problem tells us to treat them as normal. (In a real shop, Lesson 26.1’s checks, a histogram, mean versus median, and the Empirical Rule, would come first.)
▶ z-scores. z for 24.70 = (24.70 − 25.00) ÷ 0.20 = −1.5. z for 25.30 = +0.30 ÷ 0.20 = +1.5. In Desmos: (24.7-25)/0.2.
▶ Percent too short. =NORM.DIST(24.7,25,0.2,TRUE) = 0.0668.
▶ Percent too long. =1-NORM.DIST(25.3,25,0.2,TRUE) = 0.0668, the same by symmetry.
▶ Percent in specification. 1 − 0.0668 − 0.0668 = 0.8664, about 86.6%. Sense check: ±1 SD holds about 68% and ±2 SD about 95%; ±1.5 SD should sit between them, and 86.6% does.
▶ Percent out of specification. 1 − 0.8664 = 0.1336, about 13.4%.
▶ Count in the batch. Expected rejects = 0.1336 × 2500 = 334.0, so about 334 bolts, and about 2166 are in specification (334 + 2166 = 2500).
▶ Work backward (a 2% reject rate). The shop wants an upper limit that rejects only 2% of bolts for being too long. “2% above” means 98% below, so =NORM.INV(0.98,25,0.2) = 25.41 mm. (By hand: z ≈ 2.0537, so 25 + 2.0537 × 0.2 = 25.41.) Sense check: a limit of 25.30 rejects 6.7% on the long side, so a limit that rejects only 2% must be looser, and 25.41 is above 25.30 as it should be.
▶ Say it. The model estimates that about 13.4% of bolts, about 334 of 2500, are out of specification, and that an upper limit of about 25.41 mm would reject only the longest 2%.
What would make this estimate wrong? The numbers come from a normal curve, so they hold only if the lengths really are roughly normal (a skewed or two-humped batch, or a machine that drifts during the day, breaks that) and only if the mean and SD were estimated from a random, representative set of bolts.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE, PART 2 — TEST SCORES
Constructed scenario. Scores on a made-up Test A are modeled by a normal curve with mean 74 and SD 10. Scores on a made-up Test B (taken by a different class) have mean 68 and SD 5.
(a) The percentile of a score. Maya scored 88 on Test A. A percentile is the percent of scores below hers.
▶ z = (88 − 74) ÷ 10 = 1.4.
▶ =NORM.DIST(88,74,10,TRUE) = 0.9192. About the 92nd percentile: the model estimates that about 91.9% of Test A scores are below 88.
(b) The score needed for the top 15%. “Top 15%” means 85% below the cutoff.
▶ =NORM.INV(0.85,74,10) = 84.36. (By hand: z ≈ 1.0364, so 74 + 1.0364 × 10 = 84.36.) A score of about 84.4 or higher is in the top 15%.
(c) Who did better? Maya scored 88 on Test A. Theo scored 76 on Test B. The raw scores say Maya, but the tests have different centers and spreads, so compare z-scores.
▶ Maya: z = (88 − 74) ÷ 10 = 1.4, percentile 0.9192.
▶ Theo: z = (76 − 68) ÷ 5 = 1.6, percentile =NORM.DIST(76,68,5,TRUE) = 0.9452.
▶ Say it. Relative to their own classes, Theo’s score is higher (1.6 SDs above his mean, about the 94.5th percentile, against Maya’s 1.4 SDs, about the 91.9th), even though Maya’s raw score is 12 points higher. To match Maya’s z of 1.4, Theo would have needed 68 + 1.4 × 5 = 75.
What would make this estimate wrong? Both tests must be roughly normal for these percentiles to be trustworthy. Scores are often bunched up near the top of the scale (a ceiling) and skewed, and a z-comparison treats the two classes as comparable groups, which may not be true.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE, PART 3A — A SURVEY COUNT WHERE THE APPROXIMATION IS FINE
Constructed scenario. A made-up claim says that 40% of the students in a school play an instrument. You take a random sample of n = 100 students. If the claim is true, what is the probability that at most 35 of them play an instrument? The count X is binomial with n = 100 and p = 0.4.
▶ Audit first. np = 100 × 0.4 = 40 and n(1 − p) = 100 × 0.6 = 60. Both are at least 10, so the normal approximation is allowed.
▶ Mean and SD. Mean = np = 40. SD = √(np(1 − p)) = √24 = 4.899. In Desmos: sqrt(100*0.4*0.6).
▶ Continuity correction. “At most 35” includes the whole bar at 35, which stretches to 35.5, so we use 35.5. z = (35.5 − 40) ÷ 4.899 = −0.919.
▶ Normal estimate. =NORM.DIST(35.5,40,4.899,TRUE) = 0.1792.
▶ Exact value. =BINOM.DIST(35,100,0.4,TRUE) = 0.1795. The estimate is within 0.0003 of the exact value.
▶ What the correction buys. Without it, =NORM.DIST(35,40,4.899,TRUE) = 0.1537, off by about 2.6 percentage points. Always use the correction for a count.
▶ Say it. If 40% of students really play an instrument, the chance that a random sample of 100 has 35 or fewer players is about 18% (the normal estimate is 0.1792, the exact value 0.1795).
What would make this estimate wrong? The sample must be random and the students independent of each other. A sample drawn from one friend group, one band room, or one class would break both, and no formula repairs that. The claim of 40% is also only a claim; the probability is computed assuming it.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE, PART 3B — WHEN THE AUDIT FAILS
Constructed scenario. A made-up machine produces a defective part with probability p = 0.05, independently each time. In a sample of n = 20 parts, find the probability of at most 1 defective part.
▶ Audit. np = 20 × 0.05 = 1, which is less than 10. (n(1 − p) = 19 passes, but both numbers must be at least 10.) The audit fails: do not use the normal approximation.
▶ Use the exact method. =BINOM.DIST(1,20,0.05,TRUE) = 0.7358.
▶ See why the approximation fails. Here the mean is 1 and the SD is √(20 × 0.05 × 0.95) = 0.9747. With the continuity correction, =NORM.DIST(1.5,1,0.9747,TRUE) = 0.6960, about 4 points too low. Worse, the normal curve puts =NORM.DIST(0,1,0.9747,TRUE) = 0.1525 of its area below 0, where a count of defective parts can never go. The bell is trying to describe a lopsided pile of bars hugging zero, and it cannot.
▶ Say it. The exact probability of at most 1 defective part is 0.7358, about 73.6%. The approximation would have given 69.6%, and the audit told us in advance not to trust it.
What would make this estimate wrong? Even the exact answer assumes the parts are made independently with the same fixed chance of being defective; if defects come in clumps (a bad batch of metal, a worn tool), the binomial model itself is wrong.

💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · EXCEL FOR THE LAB, DESMOS FOR THE ARITHMETIC
Excel is the star. Percent below: =NORM.DIST(x,mean,SD,TRUE). Cutoff for a percent below: =NORM.INV(probability,mean,SD). z-score: =STANDARDIZE(x,mean,SD) or =(x-mean)/SD. Exact binomial: =BINOM.DIST(k,n,p,TRUE). Approximation with the continuity correction: =NORM.DIST(k+0.5,n*p,SQRT(n*p*(1-p)),TRUE). Audit gate: =IF(AND(n*p>=10,n*(1-p)>=10),"PASS","FAIL"). Use cell references so one formula fills across or down, and add dollar signs (F4) when a reference must not move.

Desmos Scientific is for plain arithmetic only. Type sqrt(100*0.4*0.6) and read 4.899. Type (35.5-40)/4.899 and read about −0.919. Type (88-74)/10 for a z-score, and 74+1.0364*10 to turn a z-score back into a score. The areas and the exact binomial values come from Excel.
🏭 WHERE PEOPLE USE THIS
Normal approximations show up wherever a decision needs a quick estimate: quality-control teams estimating how many parts fall outside a tolerance, test-scoring groups placing a score on a scale or setting a cutoff for a chosen percent, and pollsters estimating how likely a sample count is under a stated claim. Their appeal is speed and a single picture that anyone can read. Their limit is the one you practiced today: the answer is only as good as the conditions behind it, and when a count is small or the data are skewed, careful practitioners switch to an exact method or collect more data. (This is a general description; every number in this lesson is constructed.)
🤖 AI TRAINING · PROMPT → PREDICT → VERIFY
Prompt. Ask an AI tool exactly this: “A made-up claim says 30% of the students at a school play an instrument. In a random sample of 150 students, use a normal approximation to find the probability that at most 40 play an instrument. First check that the approximation is appropriate, and show every step including any continuity correction.”
Predict. Before you read its answer, work it out yourself. What are np and n(1 − p)? Is the approximation allowed? Is 40 below, near, or above the mean, and so is your answer under, near, or over one half? Write your prediction down.
Verify. The values to verify: np = 150 × 0.3 = 45 and n(1 − p) = 150 × 0.7 = 105, both at least 10, so the approximation is allowed. SD = √(150 × 0.3 × 0.7) = √31.5 = 5.612. With the continuity correction, z = (40.5 − 45) ÷ 5.612 = −0.80 and the estimate is 0.2113. The exact value, =BINOM.DIST(40,150,0.3,TRUE), is 0.2126. Then audit the AI. Did it actually compute np and n(1 − p) and compare them with 10, or just announce “conditions met”? Did it use 40.5 (the correct continuity correction for “at most 40”), or 40 (giving about 0.1865), or 39.5? Did it use the SD 5.612, or mistakenly use the variance 31.5 as the SD (which would give z = −0.14 and about 0.44)? Did it call the result an estimate and say what it assumes (a random sample, independent students)? State your own verified answer and list each problem you found.
