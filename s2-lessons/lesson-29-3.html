Lesson 29.3
Conditional Relative Frequencies, Segmented Bars, and Association
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
One table can give you two different percents. The denominator tells you which question you asked.
Learn to turn counts into conditional percents, to see them as segmented bars, to build a whole table from group sizes and percents, and to say what a difference between groups does and does not show.
Reading & work: Read the worked examples, try the table explorer, build a table in Excel, and write one carefully worded conclusion. Plan on about 90 minutes. Every dataset and scenario on this page is constructed (made up), and the screening test is hypothetical.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to compute row and column conditional relative frequencies from a two-way table and say which denominator I used, in words that start “of those who…”.
2
I will be able to draw and read a segmented (100%) bar graph, say what bars that look alike would mean, and decide whether two categorical variables look associated by comparing conditional relative frequencies, with a hedged sense of how big a difference matters.
3
I will be able to build a complete two-way table from group sizes plus conditional percents, by table and by tree diagram, and show that the tree and the table agree.
4
I will be able to state that an association in observational data is not evidence of cause, and write one careful sentence that says only what the data show.

✨ RESPONSIBILITY: NAME THE GROUP YOU ARE TALKING ABOUT
A percent without its group is a half-sentence. “65% were alert” is silent about 65% of whom. The two most common errors in reading a table are dividing by the wrong total and then speaking as if you had divided by the right one. Both are quiet mistakes, and both change what a reader will believe about real people.
This lesson makes one habit automatic: before you write a percent, finish the sentence “of those who…”. The group after those words is your denominator. If you cannot name it, you do not yet know what you computed. Being responsible with a table means making the denominator visible, and making your conclusion no bigger than the data.
📊 THREE DENOMINATORS, THREE DIFFERENT QUESTIONS
In a two-way table of counts, every cell can be divided by three different totals. They answer three different questions.
▶ Joint relative frequency. Divide a cell by the grand total. “Of everyone surveyed, what share are in both categories?”
▶ Marginal relative frequency. Divide a row total (or column total) by the grand total. “Of everyone surveyed, what share are in this category, ignoring the other variable?”
▶ Conditional relative frequency. Divide a cell by its row total or by its column total. The group you are conditioning on becomes the whole world.
A row conditional relative frequency answers “of those in this row, what share fall in this column?” Each row’s conditionals add to 100%. A column conditional answers “of those in this column, what share fall in this row?” Each column’s conditionals add to 100%. The two are not the same, and mixing them up is the error this lesson is built to prevent. (You met the same warning in Semester 1: the chance of A given B is not the chance of B given A.)
🍳 WORKED EXAMPLE 1 — ROW AND COLUMN CONDITIONALS, AND THE BARS
Constructed data, not real. A made-up survey asked 300 students two questions: did you eat breakfast this morning, and did you feel alert in first period?

▶ Step 1, audit the totals. 117 + 63 = 180, 54 + 66 = 120, 117 + 54 = 171, 63 + 66 = 129, and 171 + 129 = 300 = 180 + 120. The table is consistent.
▶ Step 2, row conditionals (the denominator is the row total). Of those who ate breakfast, 117 / 180 = 0.65 = 65% felt alert, and 63 / 180 = 0.35 = 35% did not. Of those who did not eat breakfast, 54 / 120 = 0.45 = 45% felt alert, and 66 / 120 = 0.55 = 55% did not. Each row adds to 100%.
▶ Step 3, a column conditional (the denominator is the column total). Of those who felt alert, 117 / 171 ≈ 68.4% ate breakfast. Of those who did not feel alert, 63 / 129 ≈ 48.8% ate breakfast. Notice that 68.4% is not the alert rate for breakfast eaters. It answers a different question, about a different group (the 171 who felt alert).
▶ Step 4, the marginal for comparison. Overall, 180 / 300 = 60% of the students ate breakfast. Among the alert students the share is higher (68.4%), among the not-alert students it is lower (48.8%). The overall 60% sits between them.

▶ Step 5, read the bars. Each bar is one row, scaled to the same full length (100%). The blue segment is the row conditional for “felt alert”: 65% for breakfast eaters, 45% for the others. The bars differ by 20 percentage points (65 − 45).
▶ Step 6, say it carefully. “In this made-up survey of 300 students, 65% of those who ate breakfast felt alert, compared with 45% of those who did not. In this sample, breakfast and alertness look associated.” That sentence names both groups, gives both percents, and says “in this sample.”
📈 SEGMENTED BARS, AND WHAT ALIKE BARS WOULD MEAN
A segmented bar graph (also called a 100% stacked bar graph) draws one bar per row. Every bar has the same total length, 100%, and is cut into pieces that show that row’s conditional relative frequencies. Because every bar is scaled to 100%, the groups can be compared fairly even when they have very different sizes. (A plain bar graph of counts would make the bigger group look bigger on every segment.)
Now the key idea. Suppose the two bars in Worked Example 1 had come out looking exactly alike, say 55% alert for both. That would mean the share who felt alert was the same whether or not a student ate breakfast. Knowing the row would tell you nothing about the column. In this sample, the two variables would show no association. If the bars differ, the variables are associated in this sample: knowing the row changes the share you expect in each column.
Words to avoid: “independent” is a claim about a population or a model, and a single sample will almost never produce bars that match perfectly. Say “no association in this sample,” or “the bars are nearly the same.”
📐 HOW BIG A DIFFERENCE MATTERS (HONESTLY, THERE IS NO MAGIC NUMBER)
Real bars are never exactly alike, so you need a sense of when a gap is worth reporting. There is no single cutoff, but three things help you weigh it.
▶ The size of the gap. Measure it in percentage points. A 20-point gap (65% versus 45%) is large. A 1-point gap is small.
▶ The size of the groups. The same gap is more believable with more people behind it. Constructed illustration: 20 of 40 students versus 22 of 40 is 50% versus 55%, a 5-point gap that disappears if just two students in the second group had answered the other way. But 2,000 of 4,000 versus 2,200 of 4,000 is also 50% versus 55%, and a gap that size is much harder to blame on the luck of who was asked.
▶ Sampling variation. A different sample of students would give somewhat different percents, even if nothing about the population changed. Later in this course you will learn how to judge how much a percent can wander. For now, the responsible move is to report the difference you see, say it is for this sample, and avoid announcing a big discovery from a small gap in a small group.
A rough working habit, hedged: a gap of a few points in small groups is a reason to be cautious. A gap of 15 to 20 points or more, with groups of a hundred or more, is worth reporting as a real difference in the sample, and it is the kind of difference you would want to investigate further.
🌳 WORKED EXAMPLE 2 — BUILD THE WHOLE TABLE FROM SIZES AND PERCENTS
Constructed data, not real. A made-up school has 500 students. Its records say that 60% of students ride the bus. Of the bus riders, 30% arrive early (before the first bell). Of the students who do not ride the bus, 55% arrive early. You are given only group sizes and percents, not a table. Can you build the table?
Yes, but notice what you need: the group sizes (the marginal shares) and the conditional percents. The percents alone are not enough, because “30% of the bus riders” means nothing until you know how many bus riders there are.
▶ Step 1, group sizes. Bus riders: 0.60 × 500 = 300. Non-riders: 500 − 300 = 200.
▶ Step 2, each cell is (group size) × (conditional percent). Bus and early: 0.30 × 300 = 90. Bus and not early: 300 − 90 = 210. Non-bus and early: 0.55 × 200 = 110. Non-bus and not early: 200 − 110 = 90.
▶ Step 3, add the totals. Early: 90 + 110 = 200. Not early: 210 + 90 = 300. Grand total: 200 + 300 = 500.

▶ Step 4, the tree route. Draw the first branches as the group shares (0.60 and 0.40). From each, draw the second branches as the conditional percents. Multiply along each path to get the leaf, which is a joint relative frequency.
Tree diagram for the made-up school (drawn with inline SVG)
Constructed (made-up) data.
▶ Step 5, the tree and the table agree. Leaf × 500 gives the table cell: 0.18 × 500 = 90, 0.42 × 500 = 210, 0.22 × 500 = 110, 0.18 × 500 = 90. The leaves add to 0.18 + 0.42 + 0.22 + 0.18 = 1, as they must. The tree is the table written as percents, and the table is the tree written as counts.
▶ Step 6, now use the table. The row conditionals are the second branches you were given: 30% of bus riders arrive early, 55% of non-riders do. A column question, by contrast, needs the table: of the students who arrive early, what share ride the bus? It is 90 / 200 = 45%, not 30%. The 30% is of bus riders. The 45% is of early arrivers.
▶ Step 7, say what it shows, and what it does not. “In this made-up school, 30% of bus riders arrived early, compared with 55% of non-riders, a 25-point difference in this sample.” That does not show that riding the bus makes students late. Bus routes, distance from school, and when families leave home could all matter, and nobody assigned students to ride or not.
⚠️ RESPONSIBILITY CHECKPOINT: ASSOCIATION IS NOT CAUSATION
Return to the breakfast survey. Students chose whether to eat breakfast, and nobody assigned them. That makes it an observational study. The table shows an association: breakfast eaters were more likely to report feeling alert. It does not show that breakfast caused the alertness. Maybe students who eat breakfast also tend to get more sleep, or have calmer mornings at home, and sleep and calm mornings are what help. The table cannot tell these stories apart.
The careful claim is the one in Worked Example 1: “In this survey, students who ate breakfast were more likely to report feeling alert.” A causal claim needs more, such as a randomized experiment, which you will study in Module 11. You also saw this idea earlier this week when a third variable explained away a strong correlation. Two-way tables are subject to the same rule: a difference in conditional percents is an association, and an association is not a cause.

🏭 WHERE PEOPLE USE THIS · FALSE POSITIVES REVISITED
A hypothetical screening test (made up, not a real test). In Semester 1 (Lesson 8.3) you met the base rate fallacy: reading a test’s accuracy as if it were the answer, and so forgetting how rare the condition is. You solved that problem with a 1,000-person count, a tree, and the long theorem. Here is the same idea, and you will see that the 1,000-person count was a two-way table all along (Lesson 9.3 said so). Suppose 1,000 people are screened for a made-up condition. 2% of them have it. The test catches 90% of those who have it, and wrongly flags 5% of those who do not.
Out of 1,000 peopleTests positiveTests negativeTotal
Has the condition (2% of 1,000)18220
Does not have it (98%)49931980
Total679331,000
Building it. 2% of 1,000 = 20 have the condition, and 980 do not. The test catches 0.90 × 20 = 18 and misses 2. It wrongly flags 0.05 × 980 = 49 and correctly clears 931. Every cell is a whole number, which is why these made-up numbers were chosen.

Row conditional (of those who have the condition): 18 / 20 = 90% test positive. This is the “the test catches 90%” fact, a statement about the test.
Column conditional (of those who test positive): 18 / 67 ≈ 26.9% actually have the condition. About 73% of the positives (49 of 67) are false positives. This is the number a person who just tested positive needs. It is a different question, about a different group, so it has a different denominator.
The same screening problem as a tree (drawn with inline SVG)
Hypothetical (made-up) test.
The tree agrees. The two positive leaves are 0.018 and 0.049, which add to 0.067, and 0.018 / 0.067 = 18/67. Each leaf times 1,000 gives the table cell (18, 2, 49, 931).

The Semester 1 route agrees too. What Semester 1 called Bayes’ theorem (the “long theorem” of Lesson 8.2) gives (0.90 × 0.02) / (0.90 × 0.02 + 0.05 × 0.98) = 0.018 / 0.067 = 18/67 ≈ 26.9%. It is the same fraction, because the table’s column conditional is that computation, done with counts instead of with formulas. Compare Semester 1’s numbers (1% have it, 90% caught, 9% false alarms), which gave about 9 in 98, roughly 9%: a different base rate, a different answer, the same method.

Why it matters, hedged. When a condition is rare in the group screened, a good test can still produce many more false positives than true positives, and that is why a positive screening result is commonly followed by further testing rather than treated as the final answer. These numbers are made up to show the arithmetic. Real tests and real populations have their own numbers, so look them up before quoting any.
💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · EXCEL: CONDITIONAL FREQUENCIES AND A 100% BAR CHART
Excel is the star. Put the counts in a block with the row totals in column D and the column totals in row 4. A row conditional divides each cell by its row total: =B2/$D2. The dollar sign locks column D, so when you fill the formula right or down, the denominator always comes from column D while the row number is free to change. A column conditional divides each cell by its column total: =B2/B$4. The dollar sign locks row 4. Format the results as percents with Home → Percent Style (%). For the chart, select the counts, then choose Insert → Insert Column or Bar Chart → 100% Stacked Column. Full click-by-click steps are in Activity 3.

Desmos Scientific is for plain arithmetic only. Type each fraction as two counts so you can trace it: 117/180 gives 0.65, 117/171 gives 0.6842…, 18/67 gives 0.2686…, and 0.6*500 gives 300.
🤖 AI TRAINING · FALSE-POSITIVE PROBLEM VIA THE TABLE; COMPARE WITH THE BAYES ROUTE (PROMPT → PREDICT → VERIFY)
Predict first (build the table yourself). A new hypothetical screening: 1,000 people, 4% have a made-up condition, the test catches 80% of those who have it, and it wrongly flags 10% of those who do not. Before you open any AI tool, build the two-way table by counts (condition in rows, test result in columns) and write your answer to: of those who test positive, what share actually have the condition?
Prompt. Now give an AI tool exactly this: “1,000 people are screened. 4% have a condition. The test catches 80% of those who have it and wrongly flags 10% of those who do not. Build the two-way table of counts, then tell me, of those who test positive, what share have the condition. Then solve it again with Bayes’ theorem and tell me whether the two answers agree.”
Verify. Check its table cell by cell against yours, and check its row and column totals. Did it divide by the number who test positive (the column total) or by the number who have the condition (the row total)? Did the Bayes route and the table route give the same fraction? Did it say “80% chance you have it” anywhere, mixing up the test’s catch rate with the answer? Report what it got right, any place it was wrong or vague, and what you did about it. ▶  Check
Totals: print 90 + 68 = 158, e-book 60 + 102 = 162, grand total 320. Row conditionals: of the teens under 15, 90 / 150 = 0.60 = 60% prefer print; of the teens 15 or older, 68 / 170 = 0.40 = 40% prefer print. Column conditional: of the teens who prefer print, 90 / 158 ≈ 57.0% are under 15. That is a different group (the 158 print fans) and a different number from 60%. Build the Table Two Ways

1
A made-up gym has 600 members. 40% are morning members and 60% are evening members. 70% of morning members attend at least three days a week, and 45% of evening members do.
2
Build the two-way table of counts (membership time in rows, attends at least three days in columns). Show each cell as (group size) × (percent), and add the totals.
3
Draw the tree: first branches are the group shares, second branches are the conditional percents, leaves are the products. Multiply each leaf by 600 and check that it equals your table cell.
4
Compute, with the denominator in words: of all members, what share attend at least three days? Of those who attend at least three days, what share are morning members?
5
Draw the two segmented bars. In one hedged sentence, say whether membership time and attendance look associated, and add the sentence that blocks a causal reading.
▶  Check
Group sizes: 0.40 × 600 = 240 morning, 360 evening. Cells: morning attend 0.70 × 240 = 168, morning not 72; evening attend 0.45 × 360 = 162, evening not 198. Totals: attend 330, not 270, grand 600. Tree leaves: 0.28, 0.12, 0.27, 0.33, and times 600 they are 168, 72, 162, 198, which match the table. Of all members, 330 / 600 = 55% attend at least three days. Of those who attend at least three days, 168 / 330 ≈ 50.9% are morning members. Bars: 70% versus 45%, a 25-point gap, so in this sample they look associated. A careful sentence: “In this made-up gym, 70% of morning members and 45% of evening members attended at least three days a week.” Not causation: members chose their time slot. Work schedules or age could affect both.

Answer key for the AI Training task: 1,000 people, 4% = 40 have it; 80% of 40 = 32 positive, 8 negative; 960 do not; 10% of 960 = 96 positive, 864 negative; positives 32 + 96 = 128; 32 / 128 = 25%. Bayes route: (0.80 × 0.04) / (0.80 × 0.04 + 0.10 × 0.96) = 0.032 / 0.128 = 25%.
