Lesson 34.2
Comparing Two Treatments in a Randomized Experiment
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
When chance alone deals the groups, how big a gap is too big to blame on luck?
Random assignment and what it allows you to say; shuffling the group labels to build a world where the treatment does nothing; counting the shuffles at least as extreme as the gap we saw; a rule of thumb of 5 in 100; the same gap with different spread; what a verdict does not tell you
Reading & work: Read every section in order, work each example on paper before you read its solution, use the Two-Sample Compare interactive, follow the Excel steps, and complete the three activities at the bottom. Plan on about 75 minutes: roughly 40 minutes for reading, examples, the interactive, and the Excel steps, and about 35 minutes for the activities.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain why random assignment lets a gap that chance alone would rarely produce be credited to the treatment, and what it still does not let you say.
2
I will be able to describe a shuffle of the group labels as a world where the treatment does nothing, and use a pile of shuffled gaps to find how often chance alone makes a gap at least as big as the observed one, in either direction.
3
I will be able to apply the rule of thumb “unusual if fewer than about 5 in every 100 shuffles” and explain why the same gap can be convincing with tightly packed data and unconvincing with scattered data.
4
I will be able to write an honest conclusion that separates what chance can explain, what random assignment lets you say about cause, and what an unremarkable result does not show.

✨ A WONDER TO START WITH
A made-up gardening club plants 24 seedlings. By a random draw, 12 get fertilizer A and 12 get fertilizer B. After the same growing time, the B seedlings average 10.30 cm and the A seedlings 8.91 cm. B is ahead by about 1.4 cm. Is fertilizer B really better, or did B simply land the luckier seedlings?
In Lesson 34.1 you compared two populations by taking a random sample from each, and you built a plausible range for the true difference. Today the setting is different. We have one set of subjects, and we split them into two groups by chance. The question is no longer “how big is the difference between two populations?” but “could chance alone, the luck of who landed in which group, produce a gap this big?” We will answer it by simulation: we will build the world where the fertilizer does nothing and see how big a gap luck alone makes.
Service, this module’s virtue, appears here as honesty toward the people who will use your result. A gardener, a school board, and a clinic all want to know whether a treatment works. Telling them “this gap is bigger than luck explains” when it is, and “the data cannot tell us yet” when it is not, is a real service, even when the second answer is less exciting.
A note on honesty. Every dataset in this lesson is made up (constructed) or simulated by a computer. None of it is real research data.
🎲 RANDOM ASSIGNMENT: WHY IT MATTERS, AND WHAT IT LEAVES OPEN
In an experiment, the researcher decides who gets which treatment. In a randomized experiment, chance makes that decision, with a coin flip, a random draw, or a computer shuffle. Seedlings differ in many ways: how sturdy they were, where they sat, things nobody thought to measure. Random assignment tends to spread all of those differences across the two groups, so that, on average, neither group has the edge before the treatment starts. That is why, in Module 11, randomization was the step that makes a claim about cause possible.
Notice the words tends and on average. Random assignment does not make the two groups identical. By luck of the draw, one group may get a few more sturdy seedlings. So even if the two fertilizers were exactly the same, we would still expect the two group means to differ a little. Only gaps that are bigger than that luck-sized wobble point toward the treatment.
That gives the whole plan of the lesson in three steps: (1) measure the observed gap, B minus A; (2) find out how large a gap chance alone makes when the groups are formed this way; (3) compare. The interactive has a box titled Which tab fits?. It says: if you have two random samples of two populations, you want to estimate the difference, and you use the first tab (Lesson 34.1). If you have one set of subjects split by random assignment into two treatments, you want to know whether chance alone could explain the gap, and you use the second tab, Randomized experiment. Today lives on the second tab.
🔀 SHUFFLE THE LABELS: BUILD THE BORING WORLD
Suppose, just for a moment, that the fertilizer makes no difference at all. In that world each seedling would have grown to the same height whichever fertilizer it received, and the labels A and B are just names we tossed on at random. Then the heights we measured would be the same, and only the label-sorting would be left to chance. Call this the boring world.
We can act it out. Pool all 24 measured heights into one list. Shuffle the pool, deal the first 12 heights into a pretend group A and the other 12 into a pretend group B, and compute the gap, B minus A. Record it. Then shuffle again, and again, thousands of times. Every shuffle is one way the luck of the draw could have fallen if the treatment did nothing, so the pile of shuffled gaps shows what chance alone produces.
Now look where our real gap sits in that pile. If it lands in the middle, chance makes gaps like ours all the time. If it lands far out in a thin tail, chance alone rarely makes a gap that big. We count the shuffles whose gap is at least as big as ours, in either direction (our gap or bigger, and the mirror image, our gap in the other direction or bigger), and divide by the number of shuffles. We count both directions because a gap of that size the other way would have impressed us just as much, so luck gets credit for both.
How small must that share be before we call the gap unusual? A common convention, which the interactive lets you adjust, is: fewer than about 5 of every 100 shuffles. It is a convention, a place people agreed to draw a line, not a law of nature. Lesson 34.3 looks at where it comes from and what it does and does not tell you.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 1: DO THE SEEDLINGS PASS THE SHUFFLE TEST?
Problem. In the made-up seedling experiment, 12 seedlings were randomly assigned to each fertilizer. Heights in cm: A: 8.1, 9.4, 7.8, 10.2, 8.9, 9.0, 7.5, 9.7, 8.4, 10.0, 8.8, 9.1. B: 10.3, 9.8, 11.2, 9.5, 10.9, 8.7, 11.6, 10.1, 9.9, 12.0, 10.4, 9.2. Find the observed gap, then use shuffles to decide whether chance alone could plausibly explain it.
Teacher’s voice: First the gap. The A heights add to 106.9, so the A mean is 106.9 ÷ 12 = 8.908. The B heights add to 123.6, so the B mean is 123.6 ÷ 12 = 10.300. The observed gap, B minus A, is 10.300 − 8.908 = +1.392 cm. The spreads are modest: the standard deviations are about 0.85 cm (A) and 0.98 cm (B), so most seedlings in each group are within about a centimeter of their group’s mean.
Now the boring world. A computer script (fixed starting seed, so the run can be repeated) pooled the 24 heights and shuffled them 10,000 times, each time computing B minus A. Here is the pile.
Simulated data: 10,000 shuffles of the 24 made-up seedling heights. Each bar counts the shuffles in a 0.2 cm bin. Gold lines: the gap we saw (+1.39, solid) and its mirror image (dashed).
The pile is a bell shape centered at 0, as it should be: in the boring world there is no reason for B to beat A or the reverse. A typical shuffled gap is about 0.5 cm in either direction (the spread of the 10,000 gaps is 0.46 cm), and the biggest in this run was +1.61 and the smallest −1.53. Our gap of 1.39 sits right out at the edge. Only 8 of the 10,000 shuffles gave a gap of +1.39 or more, and 5 gave −1.39 or less, so 13 shuffles in all were at least as big in either direction. That is 13 ÷ 10,000 = 0.0013, about 1 shuffle in 1,000. (A count of every one of the 2,704,156 ways to split the 24 seedlings into two groups of 12 gives 0.0014, so the simulation is close, and your own interactive run will wobble a little, which is why we say “about.”)
Answer. The observed gap is about +1.39 cm. Chance alone would make a gap this big only about 1 time in 1,000, far below the 5-in-100 rule of thumb, so the gap is unusual. Because the seedlings were randomly assigned, we may say fertilizer B caused the taller growth in these seedlings. Whether the 1.4 cm matters to a gardener is a separate question that the shuffles do not answer.

📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 2: SEEDS THAT SPROUT, A YES-OR-NO OUTCOME
Problem. A made-up gardening class sows 40 seeds. By random assignment, 20 go into soil mix A and 20 into soil mix B. After the same time, 9 of the 20 seeds in mix A sprouted and 14 of the 20 in mix B. Is that gap more than chance alone would produce?
Teacher’s voice: The outcome is Yes or No, so the statistic is a difference of proportions. Mix A: 9 ÷ 20 = 0.45. Mix B: 14 ÷ 20 = 0.70. The observed gap, B minus A, is 0.70 − 0.45 = +0.25, a 25-point difference. That looks big. But there are only 20 seeds per group, so each seed moves a proportion by 0.05, and small groups wobble a lot.
The boring world says the soil mix does nothing, so the 40 seeds were going to give 23 sprouts and 17 non-sprouts whichever mix they went into. Shuffle: take 23 “sprouted” and 17 “did not” labels, deal them at random into two groups of 20, compute the gap in sprout proportions, record it, and repeat. A count of every possible deal gives a share at least as big in either direction of 0.200, and a simulation of thousands of shuffles lands close to that. Said in words: about 20 of every 100 shuffles produce a gap of 0.25 or more in one direction or the other.
Answer. About 0.20 is far above the 5-in-100 rule of thumb, so the gap is not unusual: chance alone makes a 25-point gap about one time in five here. The data do not show that mix B is better. They also do not show that the mixes are the same. With only 20 seeds in each group the experiment simply could not tell, and a bigger experiment would be needed to find out.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 3: THE SAME GAP, DIFFERENT SPREAD
Problem. Two made-up experiments each randomly assigned 16 people to two groups of 8, and in both the B mean beat the A mean by exactly 6 points. Does the same gap mean the same strength of evidence?
Teacher’s voice: With 8 people per group there are only 12,870 ways to split the 16 scores into two groups of 8, so a computer can count every one instead of sampling shuffles. For each experiment I count the splits whose gap is 6 or more in either direction, then divide by 12,870.

In the tightly packed experiment, every A score sits within a couple of points of 50 and every B score within a couple of points of 56. Mixing the scores and dealing them out again almost never recreates a clean 6-point split: only 2 of the 12,870 ways do, so chance alone would make this gap about 2 times in 10,000. In the scattered experiment, scores run from the thirties to the seventies in both groups, so shuffling the labels easily makes gaps of 6 points or more in either direction: about 36 shuffles in every 100.
Answer. The same gap of 6 is very unusual when scores are tightly packed (about 0.0002) and not unusual at all when they are scattered (about 0.36). The lesson: judge a gap relative to the spread of the data. A big gap against a small spread is convincing; the same gap against a large spread may be nothing but luck.

⚠️ WHAT A VERDICT DOES AND DOES NOT TELL YOU
It can support a cause, in these subjects. When subjects were randomly assigned and the gap is unusual, we have a good reason to say the treatment caused the difference for the subjects in the study, because random assignment has taken away the usual rival explanations.
It does not by itself reach beyond the subjects. Random assignment answers “did the treatment cause the gap?” Random sampling from a larger population answers “do the results apply to that population?” You met these as two separate questions in Module 11. A made-up study of 40 volunteers at one school, randomly assigned, can support a cause claim about those volunteers, and a claim about every student needs more than shuffles can give.
A not-unusual gap does not show “no effect.” In Worked Example 2, the data did not rule out chance, but 20 seeds per group is a small, noisy test. A real effect can hide in a small or scattered experiment. The honest sentence is “this experiment could not tell,” not “the treatment does nothing.”
It does not repair a flawed design, and it does not measure importance. Shuffling assumes the groups were formed by random assignment. If people chose their own groups, the shuffle logic does not apply and other differences may explain the gap. And a small share says the gap is probably not luck, not that it is large or worth acting on. Real studies often use a formula-based method for the same job, one you may meet later; the shuffle shows the idea behind it.
⚠️ THREE COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS
“The share was 0.38, so the treatment has no effect.” No. It means chance alone often makes a gap this big, so this experiment does not show an effect. It does not show there is none.
“The share was 0.001, so there is only a 0.1% chance the fertilizer does nothing.” No. The share is how often chance alone (the boring world) would make a gap this big. It is not the probability that the fertilizer works or fails.
“It was a randomized experiment, so the result applies to everyone.” Not necessarily. Random assignment supports a cause claim for the subjects studied; applying it to a wider population depends on how the subjects were chosen.
💻 EXCEL: THE GAP, AND A COUNT OF SHUFFLES AT LEAST AS BIG, CLICK BY CLICK
You will build two small things: a summary of the seedling gap, and a count of how many listed shuffles are at least as big as a gap. Everything is made up. This is a practice build; you do not upload it.
▶ Open Excel and start a Blank workbook. Save it as Wk34_L2_YourName. Rename the first sheet Seedlings (right-click the sheet tab at the bottom, choose Rename, type the name, press Enter).
▶ Type the headings. In A1 type A and in B1 type B. Type the 12 A heights into A2:A13 (8.1, 9.4, 7.8, 10.2, 8.9, 9.0, 7.5, 9.7, 8.4, 10.0, 8.8, 9.1) and the 12 B heights into B2:B13 (10.3, 9.8, 11.2, 9.5, 10.9, 8.7, 11.6, 10.1, 9.9, 12.0, 10.4, 9.2).
▶ Build the summary table. In D2 type n, in D3 type Mean, in D4 type SD, and in D5 type Gap (B minus A). In E1 type A and in F1 type B.
▶ In E2 type =COUNT(A2:A13) and in F2 type =COUNT(B2:B13). In E3 type =AVERAGE(A2:A13) and in F3 type =AVERAGE(B2:B13). In E4 type =STDEV.S(A2:A13) and in F4 type =STDEV.S(B2:B13). You should see 12 and 12, then about 8.908 and 10.300, then about 0.848 and 0.983.
▶ In E5 type =F3-E3 and press Enter. It should show about 1.392. (To show three decimals, select E3:F5 and on the Home tab, Number group, click Increase Decimal as needed.)
▶ Now the counting practice. Click the + at the bottom to add a new sheet and rename it Shuffles. In A1 type Shuffled gap. In A2:A21 type these 20 shuffled gaps (they are made-up shuffles of the seeds-sprouting data: 9 of 20 against 14 of 20), one per cell, top to bottom: 0.05, -0.25, -0.15, 0.35, -0.05, -0.15, -0.15, -0.05, -0.05, 0.15, -0.15, 0.15, 0.15, 0.15, -0.05, 0.35, 0.05, 0.15, 0.05, 0.15
▶ In C1 type Observed gap and in C2 type =ROUND(14/20-9/20,2). This is the observed gap, 0.70 − 0.45 = 0.25. The ROUND keeps the computer from storing 0.25 as a hair under 0.25, which could make the count skip a value that is exactly equal.
▶ In E1 type Count at least as big and in E2 type =COUNTIF(A2:A21,">="&C2)+COUNTIF(A2:A21,"<="&-C2), then press Enter. The first part counts shuffled gaps at or above +0.25 and the second counts those at or below −0.25. You should see 3. In E3 type Share and in F3 type =E2/COUNT(A2:A21). You should see 0.15.
Check yourself. Change C2 to 0.35 (a bigger observed gap) and watch the count drop to 2. Only 20 shuffles is far too few to decide anything, because a share built on 20 shuffles is coarse and jumpy; the interactive uses thousands, and for these data it settles near 0.20. The point here is the counting rule: add the tail on each side, and ties count as “at least as big.”
💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · GAPS AND SHUFFLE SHARES IN EXCEL AND DESMOS
Excel. Group summaries: =AVERAGE(range), =STDEV.S(range), and =COUNT(range). The observed gap is one mean minus the other (B minus A), for example =F3-E3. To count shuffled gaps at least as big as the observed gap in either direction, with the observed gap in C2 and the shuffled gaps in A2:A21, type =COUNTIF(A2:A21,">="&C2)+COUNTIF(A2:A21,"<="&-C2) and divide by the number of shuffles for the share. Wrap a computed gap in ROUND(...,2) before comparing it to typed values.
Desmos Scientific is plain arithmetic here. A group mean is a sum divided by a count: enter 123.6 ÷ 12 to get 10.3, and 106.9 ÷ 12 to get 8.9083…. Subtract to get the gap: 10.3 − 8.9083 is about 1.3917. For proportions, enter 14 ÷ 20 − 9 ÷ 20 to get 0.25. A shuffle share is a count divided by the number of shuffles: 13 ÷ 10000 gives 0.0013, and 3 ÷ 20 gives 0.15.
🤖 AI TRAINING · ASK AN AI WHETHER A TREATMENT WORKS; CHECK ITS CAUSAL LANGUAGE (PROMPT → PREDICT → VERIFY)
Prompt. Give an AI tool these made-up facts: “A gardening club randomly assigned 24 seedlings, 12 to fertilizer A and 12 to fertilizer B. Mean heights were 8.91 cm (A) and 10.30 cm (B), with standard deviations of about 0.85 and 0.98. In 10,000 shuffles of the group labels, only about 13 gave a gap at least as big as 1.39 cm in either direction. Does fertilizer B work?”
Predict. Before you read its answer, write what a careful answer should say: the gap is about 1.4 cm; about 13 in 10,000 (about 1 in 1,000) is far below the 5-in-100 rule of thumb, so chance alone is an unlikely explanation; because assignment was random, it is fair to say B caused the extra growth in these seedlings; the answer should not claim more than that.
Verify. Check the AI’s number against your own, then grade its language. Did it mention random assignment as the reason a cause claim is allowed? Did it mention spread (or the shuffle share) rather than only the two means? Did it overreach, such as “fertilizer B works on all plants,” or “proves”? Now ask it a follow-up, “What if the shuffle share had been 0.40?” and check that it does not say “so the fertilizer does nothing.” Rewrite its weakest sentence so that it is true. Verdict. A confident paragraph is not a checked paragraph; the shuffle count and the design are.
