📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A spread measure that uses every value — and an honest account of what it is.
Deviations from the mean; squaring; standard deviation by hand; sample versus population; STDEV.S and STDEV.P; SD and outliers
Reading & work: Read every section in order, work the table by hand alongside the text, test the n−1 claim, play with the widget, follow the Excel steps, and complete the four activities at the bottom. Plan on about 65 minutes.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to compute the standard deviation of a small dataset by hand from the deviations, for both a population (divide by n) and a sample (divide by n−1), and explain why the deviations are squared.
2
I will be able to interpret a standard deviation as a typical distance from the mean in the data's units, and compare the spread of two datasets with the same mean.

🎯 WHY WE NEED ONE MORE MEASURE OF SPREAD
Last week you measured spread two ways. The range (largest minus smallest) uses only two values. The IQR (the width of the middle half, Q3 minus Q1) ignores the lowest quarter and the highest quarter completely. Neither one looks at every value. Here are two sets of nine values (constructed) with the same mean and, in fact, the same five-number summary:
Set ASet B
Values0, 10, 10, 10, 20, 30, 30, 30, 400, 10, 10, 19, 20, 21, 30, 30, 40
Min, Q1, median, Q3, max (median of each half)0, 10, 20, 30, 400, 10, 20, 30, 40
Range / IQR40 / 2040 / 20
Mean2020
Set A puts several values out at 10 and 30; Set B packs its middle values close to 20. Anyone who sees only the range and the IQR would call the two sets identical. They are not. A measure that uses every value can tell them apart, and that measure is the standard deviation, usually written SD. (Preview: the sample SD of Set A is about 13.23 and of Set B about 12.26. You will learn to compute numbers like these in a moment.)
A note on courtesy, this module's virtue. Giving only the range and the IQR can hide differences like this one. Giving the SD too is a way of telling the reader a little more of the truth about how the data are scattered. All datasets in this lesson are constructed (made up) to teach the ideas.
⚖️ FROM DEVIATIONS TO SQUARES
Start where Lesson 22.1 ended. The mean is the balance point, and the distance from each value to the mean is called its deviation: deviation = value − mean. A natural idea is to average the deviations to see how far values typically sit from the mean. Try it on six practice times (constructed), in minutes: 4, 5, 7, 8, 11, 13. The mean is (4+5+7+8+11+13)/6 = 8.

Dots are the values; gold segments show each deviation from the mean.
The deviations are −4, −3, −1, 0, +3, +5. They add up to 0. That is no accident: the deviations from the mean always add to zero, because the mean is the balance point. So the plain average of the deviations is always 0 and tells you nothing.
The fix is to square each deviation. Squares are never negative, so nothing cancels, and a deviation of 5 counts for more than a deviation of 1 (25 against 1). Then you average the squares, and finally take a square root to come back to the original units. That is the whole idea of the standard deviation.

▶ Step 1: find the mean. 48 ÷ 6 = 8.
▶ Step 2: find each deviation (value minus mean). Check that they add to 0; if they do not, you made an arithmetic slip.
▶ Step 3: square each deviation and add the squares. The sum of squares is 60.
▶ Step 4: divide. If these six values are the whole group you care about (a population), divide by n = 6: 60 ÷ 6 = 10. If they are a sample from a bigger group, divide by n − 1 = 5: 60 ÷ 5 = 12. The result is called the variance.
▶ Step 5: take the square root. Population SD = √10, about 3.16 minutes. Sample SD = √12, about 3.46 minutes.
population SD = √( sum of squared deviations ÷ n )     sample SD = √( sum of squared deviations ÷ ( n − 1 ) )
See inside the formula in Desmos. You can do the last two steps on Desmos Scientific. Type sqrt((16+9+1+0+9+25)/6) and you get a decimal beginning 3.162 (population). Type sqrt((16+9+1+0+9+25)/5) and you get a decimal beginning 3.464 (sample). Notice you typed the squared deviations yourself: Desmos is checking your arithmetic, not doing the statistics for you.
📐 WHAT THE NUMBER MEANS
The SD is a kind of typical distance from the mean, measured in the same units as the data. For the practice times, the sample SD is about 3.46 minutes: a typical practice time sits something like three and a half minutes away from the mean of 8.
“Something like” is honest wording. The plain average of the distances (ignoring signs) is (4+3+1+0+3+5)/6 ≈ 2.67, a bit smaller than the SD. Squaring gives far-away values extra weight, so the SD sits a little above the plain average distance. Think of it as a typical distance that listens harder to the values that are farthest out.
Units. The squared deviations and the variance are in squared units (square minutes: odd and hard to picture). The square root brings you back to minutes. That is the main reason to report the SD rather than the variance. A small SD means the values cluster near the mean; a large SD means they are scattered; an SD of 0 means every value is identical.
🔀 SAMPLE OR POPULATION? STDEV.S OR STDEV.P
Which divisor you use depends on what the data are:
▶ Population: the data are every member of the group you care about (all five trips in your own log, if the log is the whole question). Divide by n. Excel: STDEV.P.
▶ Sample: the data are a sample from a bigger group and you want to say something about that bigger group (20 customers from a store with thousands). Divide by n − 1. Excel: STDEV.S.
Course default. In statistics we nearly always have a sample, not the whole population. So unless a problem says the data are the whole group, use the sample SD (n − 1, STDEV.S). The two answers get closer as n grows: with n = 6 the divisors 6 and 5 differ a lot; with n = 600 they barely differ.
Why n − 1? An honest intuition (not a proof). Imagine a big population and a small sample from it. You measure distances from the sample's own mean, because you do not know the population's true center. But the sample mean is built from those very values, so the values sit closer to their own mean than they would to the true center. (In fact, no other number has a smaller sum of squared distances to a set of values than their own mean.) So the squared distances come out a little small on average, and dividing by n would underestimate the spread. Dividing by n − 1 instead of n enlarges the result just enough to correct for that, on average. One extreme case shows the problem: if you draw a sample of two values and both happen to be 2, the sample shows no spread at all (SD 0), though the population has some. A sample can only show the spread it happens to contain.
🔎 TEST THE CLAIM: ALL 16 SAMPLES OF SIZE 2
Let us check the n − 1 idea with a tiny made-up population of four values: 2, 4, 6, 8. Its mean is 5, and its squared deviations (9, 1, 1, 9) add to 20, so its population variance is 20 ÷ 4 = 5.
Now draw samples of size 2, with replacement (so a value can be drawn twice), keeping the order. There are 4 × 4 = 16 possible samples. For a pair of values, the squared deviations from their own mean add to (gap)² ÷ 2. So the variance with divisor n = 2 is (gap)² ÷ 4, and with divisor n − 1 = 1 it is (gap)² ÷ 2. Group the 16 samples by the gap between the two values:
Gap between the two valuesNumber of samples (of 16)ExampleVariance, divide by nVariance, divide by n − 1
04(2, 2)00
26(2, 4) or (6, 4)12
44(2, 6)48

Average over all 162.55
Check the averages: with divisor n, (4×0 + 6×1 + 4×4 + 2×9) ÷ 16 = 40 ÷ 16 = 2.5. With divisor n − 1, (4×0 + 6×2 + 4×8 + 2×18) ÷ 16 = 80 ÷ 16 = 5. The population variance is 5. So dividing by n averages to half the true value here (a big shortfall, because n = 2 is tiny), and dividing by n − 1 averages to exactly the true value. This is a check of the claim on one small population, not a general proof.
One honest wrinkle. This works for the variance. After taking square roots, the sample SD (n − 1) still tends to come out a little low on average (for these 16 samples the average sample SD is about 1.77, against a population SD of about 2.24). Dividing by n − 1 is the standard correction, not a perfect one.

Does it claim n−1 makes every sample's SD more accurate, or only that it is right on average? Does it say the variance averages out exactly while the SD does not? If it gives a numerical example, redo its arithmetic: you know the true answers (2.5 and 5 in the 16-sample table). Flag anything wrong, vague, or too confident, and say what you would ask next.
💪 SD IS NOT RESISTANT
The SD uses every value, and squaring makes big deviations count even more. So one extreme value moves the SD a lot. Change the 13 minutes to 40 in the practice times (4, 5, 7, 8, 11, 40) and compare, using the median-of-each-half quartiles:
Original (13)Changed to 40
Mean812.5
Sample SD≈ 3.46≈ 13.69
Q1, Q3, IQR5, 11, 65, 11, 6
The IQR did not move at all; the SD nearly quadrupled. In the language of Lesson 22.1, the IQR is resistant and the SD is not resistant. When data are strongly skewed or have a far outlier, report the median and IQR (and say why), or report the mean and SD and say outright that an extreme value is inflating the SD.
⚖️ SAME MEAN, DIFFERENT SPREAD
A mean alone is only half a description. Two delivery services each deliver five orders, with these times in minutes (constructed): Service X: 48, 49, 50, 51, 52. Service Y: 30, 40, 50, 60, 70. Both have a mean of 50 minutes.

Same mean, very different experience: Service X is almost always within a couple of minutes of 50; Service Y can be 20 minutes early or late. Whichever divisor you use, Y's SD is about ten times X's. Compare spreads only when the data are in the same units; an SD of 3 seconds and an SD of 3 miles cannot be ranked against each other.

📜 A MOMENT IN THE HISTORY OF MATHEMATICS · WILLIAM SEALY GOSSET, WRITING AS “STUDENT”
In the early 1900s William Sealy Gosset was a chemist at the Guinness brewery in Dublin. Brewing runs on measurements: the quality of a batch of barley, the strength of a brew, the temperature of a vat. A brewery cannot test everything, so each question had to be answered from a small handful of measurements. Gosset worked on a problem that sits right behind this lesson: when all you have is a small sample, how far can you trust the spread you computed from it, and what can you honestly say about the larger group? The usual account is that the company did not want its staff publishing under their own names, so Gosset wrote under the pen name “Student.” The key paper appeared in the journal Biometrika in 1908. His work is the origin of the name “Student’s t,” which you may meet in a later statistics course; you do not need it here. The point of the story is modest and useful: some of the most practical ideas about small samples came from someone with ordinary measuring problems and a clear sense of how little a few numbers can tell you. This is a short sketch, and historians add many details beyond it.

Honest note. Menu names and the look of the window differ a little between desktop Excel and Excel for the web, but typing a formula in a cell works the same in both. Older files and some other programs use STDEV (the same as STDEV.S) and STDEVP (the same as STDEV.P); this course uses the names with the dot. If a cell shows #NAME?, check the spelling and the dot.
💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · STANDARD DEVIATION IN DESMOS AND EXCEL
Desmos Scientific does plain arithmetic, so you do the deviations yourself and type the rest. Population SD of 2, 4, 6, 8, 10: the mean is 6, the squared deviations are 16, 4, 0, 4, 16, so type sqrt((16+4+0+4+16)/5) → a decimal beginning 2.828. For the sample SD, change only the divisor: sqrt((16+4+0+4+16)/4) → a decimal beginning 3.162. Keep the sum in its own parentheses so the division applies to the whole sum.

Excel does the list work: =STDEV.S(A2:A7) for a sample, =STDEV.P(A2:A7) for a whole population, and =VAR.S(...) or =VAR.P(...) for the variances. Use Desmos to check Excel on a small list; use Excel when the list is long. When in doubt, use STDEV.S.
