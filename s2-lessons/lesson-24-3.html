🧭 THE LAST LESSON OF THE MODULE: A WHOLE STORY, START TO FINISH
For four weeks you have been collecting tools: dot plots and histograms, the mean and the median, quartiles and the IQR, fences for outliers, the standard deviation, and a table that sets two groups side by side. Today you use all of them on one dataset, from the first question to the final paragraph, and you decide at each step what is fair to the data and to the reader.
A summary number is a courtesy or a discourtesy. It is a courtesy when it tells the reader what the data are really like. It is a discourtesy when it is true but leaves out the part that would change the reader's mind. Every number in this lesson is true. The difference between the two stories we write is what each one chooses to say and what it leaves out.
🚌 THE DATASET — CONSTRUCTED, NOT REAL
A made-up school district wants to know whether to move riders from Route A to Route B. A made-up dispatcher recorded how many minutes late each bus arrived on 25 school days for each route. These numbers were constructed for this lesson; they are not from any real bus system. Here they are, in day order (Day 1 first):
RouteMinutes late on each of 25 school days
Route A6, 8, 5, 7, 6, 9, 4, 6, 7, 5, 8, 6, 7, 5, 6, 10, 6, 7, 4, 8, 5, 6, 7, 6, 5
Route B3, 4, 2, 5, 3, 5, 1, 3, 24, 4, 2, 5, 3, 4, 3, 31, 2, 4, 3, 5, 21, 5, 2, 3, 4
We will treat each route's 25 days as a sample of the days that route runs, so the standard deviation is the sample one (STDEV.S).
❶ STEP 1 — ASK A QUESTION BEFORE YOU TOUCH THE NUMBERS
A data story answers a question. Ours is: “Which route is the better one to ride if you care about arriving on time?” Notice that “better” hides two different questions: How late is a typical day? and How bad can a bad day get? A rider who must never miss first period cares about the second. A rider who wants the shortest wait on an ordinary morning cares about the first. We will report on both and let the reader weigh them.
❷ STEP 2 — LOOK FIRST
Before computing anything, draw the data. Both routes below use the same number line, from 0 to 32 minutes. Each dot is one day; red dots are the days the 1.5 × IQR rule will flag in Step 5.
Route A: minutes late (25 days)
Route B: minutes late (25 days)
Route A box plot
Route B box plot (whiskers stop at the last unflagged value, 5)
What the picture says before any arithmetic. Route A is a tight pile centered near 6. Route B is a tight pile centered near 3 or 4 with three days far off to the right: a shape that is right-skewed with outliers. If we had gone straight to one average, we would never have seen those three dots.
❸ STEP 3 — CHOOSE SUMMARIES THAT FIT THE SHAPE
Route A is roughly symmetric: its mean (6.36) and median (6) are close, and the gap (0.36) is smaller than about a quarter of its IQR (\(0.25 \times 2 = 0.5\)). Mean and standard deviation would be fine for Route A. Route B is not: its mean (6.04) sits 2.04 above its median (4), and three flagged days pull it. For Route B the median and IQR describe the typical day, and the standard deviation (7.50) mostly measures those three days.
When we compare two groups, we use the same pair for both, or the comparison is unfair. So the main comparison uses the median and IQR, and the standard deviation is reported as a second view because it carries the news about the long tail.
❹ STEP 4 — COMPARE THE GROUPS
Here is the side-by-side summary. (Quartiles by the median-of-each-half method; on this dataset Excel's QUARTILE.INC gives exactly the same quartiles, so the two methods agree and you can check the table with either.)

❺ STEP 5 — CHECK THE OUTLIERS, THEN DECIDE: WITH AND WITHOUT
Route B's fences are \(Q_1 - 1.5 \times IQR = 3 - 3 = 0\) and \(Q_3 + 1.5 \times IQR = 5 + 3 = 8\). The days 21, 24, and 31 are above 8, so all three are flagged. Route A's fences are 2 and 10, and nothing falls outside.
A flag is a question, not a verdict. Suppose we check the dispatcher's log and find that these three late runs really happened: they are not typing errors. Then we may not delete them, because deleting true data to make a story tidier is a discourtesy to the data. Instead we report both views:
Route BAll 25 daysWithout 21, 24, 31 (22 days)
Mean6.043.41
Median43
SD (sample)7.501.18
Maximum315
The three days make up 76 of Route B's 151 late minutes: about half the total from 12 percent of the days. That is exactly what a reader needs to know. (Be careful with the trimmed set: once the three days are gone the quartiles tighten, and the rule would start flagging a different day. Trimming and re-flagging can go on and on, so we flag once, on the full data, and stop.)
❻ STEP 6 — WRITE THE STORY, AND MAKE EVERY NUMBER TRACEABLE
Here are two stories built from exactly the same data. Every number in both is correct. Read them both before reading on.
THE DISHONEST VERSION (true numbers, unfair story)
Route B is the better route. Its median delay is only 4 minutes, a third lower than Route A's 6 minutes, and its mean delay (6.04 minutes) is even lower than Route A's (6.36 minutes). The middle half of its days are as steady as Route A's (an IQR of 2 minutes for both). Route B's drivers are clearly doing a better job, so every rider should switch to Route B.
THE HONEST VERSION (same data, fair story)
We compared how many minutes late two school bus routes ran on each of 25 school days (constructed data). On a typical day Route B ran 2 minutes less late than Route A: the median delay was 4 minutes for Route B and 6 minutes for Route A. The middle half of the days was equally steady on both routes (IQR 2 minutes each). But Route B had three days of 21, 24, and 31 minutes late (3 of 25 days, or 12 percent), which the 1.5 × IQR rule flags as outliers, while Route A's worst day was 10 minutes. Those three days pull Route B's mean up to 6.04 minutes, just under Route A's 6.36, and make its standard deviation 7.50 minutes against 1.47 for Route A. Without those three days, Route B's median is 3 minutes and its mean is 3.41 minutes. So Route B is better on a typical day and far worse on a bad day. With only 25 days per route, these data cannot tell us why the late days happened or whether they will keep happening.

The honest version is about 180 words. It is longer than the dishonest one, and every sentence earns its place.
❼ STEP 7 — THE HONESTY CHECKLIST FOR A DATA STORY
Before any data story goes out, hold it up against these seven questions. They are the whole module, in a form you can use on any dataset for the rest of your life.
▶ 1. Did I state n and the units? (25 days per route; minutes late.)
▶ 2. Did I say what my summary hides? (A median hides the tail; a mean hides the typical day; any one number hides the spread.)
▶ 3. Did I avoid claiming a cause? The data show that Route B had three bad days, not why.
▶ 4. Did I keep the axis honest? A bar chart that starts at 6 instead of 0 makes a 0.36-minute gap look like nine times a 0.04 gap, so start bars at zero (dot plots and box plots may use any axis, but say what it is).
▶ 5. Did I report both views when they differ? (With and without the outliers; median and mean.)
▶ 6. Did I describe the spread as well as the center? (IQR, SD, and the largest value.)
▶ 7. Did I say what the data cannot tell? (Why the days were late; whether it will happen again; anything about days not recorded.)
🏆 WHERE ANALYSTS DO THIS
People who work with public records, such as sports results, weather readings, and wage figures, face the same choices you just made: which summary to report, whether to show the spread, and what to do with the extreme values. The habits are the same ones in your checklist: state how many observations there are, say what a single number hides, and be careful about claiming a cause. (This is a general description; no real data are used in this lesson.)
💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · SIDE-BY-SIDE TABLE IN EXCEL, ARITHMETIC IN DESMOS
Excel builds the table (Activity 1): =COUNT(range), =AVERAGE(range), =MEDIAN(range), =STDEV.S(range), =MIN(range), =QUARTILE.INC(range,1), =QUARTILE.INC(range,3), =MAX(range). Excel's quartiles can differ slightly from the by-hand ones; on this dataset they agree.

Desmos Scientific checks the plain arithmetic in a story, one expression at a time: 3/25 → 0.12 (the 12 percent); (6-4)/6 → 0.3333 (“a third lower”); 5+1.5*(5-3) → 8 (Route B's upper fence); 151/25 → 6.04; (151-76)/22 → 3.4091. Desmos does not take a list and return a median or standard deviation; Excel does.
💻 THE EXCEL TABLE, FROM LESSON 24.1, IN ONE PARAGRAPH
In Lesson 24.1 you built a side-by-side summary table: a row for each statistic, a column for each group, and a B minus A column that does the subtraction for you. Today's table is the same idea. This time the table is the workbook that your story comes from: each number you write in a sentence should be a cell you can point to.

AI DRAFT (CONSTRUCTED FOR THIS EXERCISE)
Over 20 days each (constructed practice logs), violin players practiced a median of 30 minutes per day, while trumpet players practiced a median of 24.5 minutes. Mark What Changed
10 min
1
Read the dishonest and honest stories side by side. On paper, list every difference you can find: things the honest story added, removed, or changed. Aim for at least six.
2
For each difference, name the checklist item (1 to 7) it belongs to.
▶  Check
Added: (1) the sample size and units, 25 days and minutes late; (2) the three flagged days, 21, 24, and 31, and the fact that they are 3 of 25 days, 12 percent; (3) Route A's worst day, 10 minutes; (4) the standard deviations, 7.50 and 1.47; (5) the with-and-without view (median 3, mean 3.41) next to the all-days view; (6) a closing sentence on what the data cannot tell. Removed: (7) the claim that Route B's drivers are doing a better job (a cause the data cannot show); (8) “every rider should switch” (a recommendation the data do not support on their own); (9) the unqualified “Route B is the better route”, replaced by “better on a typical day and far worse on a bad day”. Changed: the line about equal IQRs is kept but is no longer alone: it now sits beside the extremes it was hiding. Checklist items: 1 (n and units), 2 and 6 (what the median hides, spread), 3 (cause), 5 (both views), 7 (what the data cannot tell). The dishonest version uses only true numbers, so it passes a number check and fails an honesty check.
