Design-a-Study Workshop: From a Vague Question to a Plan
Lesson · Workshop
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A good question is only the start. A good design is a promise about what you will, and will not, claim.
Use everything from this module to turn a vague question into a plan someone else could carry out and trust.
Reading & work: Learn the eight-question design checklist, study three worked designs that end in three different answers, repair a flawed design, and then build your own. Plan on about two hours. Every school, greenhouse, and town on this page is constructed (made up), and no result here is a real finding.

✨ RESPONSIBILITY: A DESIGN IS A PROMISE
When you design a study you make two promises. The first is to the people (or plants, or animals) in it: you will not ask more of them than the question deserves, and you will not harm them to find an answer. The second is to everyone who later reads your result: you will claim no more than your design earned.
Most bad statistics does not come from bad arithmetic. It comes from a design that could not answer the question, followed by a claim that sounded as if it had. Today you practice the unglamorous work that comes before any data exist: deciding, in writing, how you will be fair and what you will honestly be able to say.
✅ THE DESIGN CHECKLIST: EIGHT QUESTIONS
Every design you write today answers the same eight questions, in this order. Keep this table beside you for the whole lesson.
QuestionA good answer includes
1. What is the question, and what is the aim?One sentence. Is the aim to describe a population, to compare groups as they already are, or to find out whether one thing causes another?
2. Who is the population?Exactly who or what you want to speak about, named so a stranger could tell who is in and who is out.
3. What are the variables?List each one with its units or categories. Mark the explanatory variable (what may explain) and the response variable (what we measure as the outcome). Name other variables that might matter.
4. Which design: survey, observational study, or experiment?Pick one and say why. If an experiment is the natural tool but cannot be done ethically or practically, say so and choose observational.
5. How will we choose who is in the study?The list you will draw from, the method (simple random, stratified, and so on), and the sample size, with the arithmetic.
6. If the aim is cause, how will we assign treatments?Random assignment, a comparison (control) group, blinding where possible, enough subjects per group (replication), and blocks if a known difference could swamp the effect. If the study is not an experiment, write instead why we cannot assign and what we will measure to account for other factors.
7. What could go wrong?Name the risks (bias, confounding, nonresponse, measurement error) and, for each, one thing the design does about it.
8. What can we conclude, and what can we not?One honest paragraph that matches the design: who it applies to, whether it supports cause, and what it leaves open.
🔍 CHOOSING THE DESIGN: THREE TOOLS, THREE KINDS OF CLAIM
The aim in question 1 usually points to the design in question 4. Read across each row.
DesignBest when…What you doWhat you may claim
Sample surveyYou want to describe a population: a share, an average, an opinion.Choose a random sample, ask everyone chosen the same fair questions, follow up with those who do not answer.An estimate for the population you sampled, with an honest allowance for chance swing. Not why people answer as they do.
Observational studyYou want to compare groups or look for a link and you cannot, or should not, assign the groups.Choose a sample, measure the variables as they naturally are, and also measure other factors that might explain the pattern.An association (a link) in the population sampled. Not cause, because confounding variables may be at work.
ExperimentYou want to know whether a treatment causes a change, and you can ethically assign it.Assign treatments at random, include a comparison group, blind where possible, replicate, block if needed.Cause and effect, for the kind of subjects in the study. Generalizing farther depends on how the subjects were chosen.
Two ideas that get tangled. Random sampling (how the people are chosen) protects your claim about the population. Random assignment (who gets which treatment) protects your claim about cause. A design can have one, both, or neither, and each earns a different sentence in question 8.
📈 HOW BIG SHOULD THE SAMPLE BE? A WORKSHOP ANSWER
Notice that the checklist asks for a sample size but gives no formula. Earlier in the module you watched the same population produce a different estimate every time a new sample was drawn. The lesson from that: more samples, less swing. A larger random sample gives estimates that stay closer together from one sample to the next, so the answer is steadier.
The other half of the lesson is just as important: more does not fix bias. If the way you chose people leaves some of them out, or the question nudges the answer, a bigger sample repeats the same mistake more times. So think of size and fairness as two separate dials. Fairness comes from how you choose and how you ask. Size only buys steadiness.
In practice, choose a size you can actually carry out, including the follow-up work with people who do not answer, and then say what size you chose and why. In an experiment, aim for enough subjects in each group that one unusual plant, patient, or student cannot decide the result. A small study is not shameful. A small study that claims too much is.
🍴 WORKED MINI-DESIGN 1 — THE LUNCH-MENU SURVEY (A SAMPLE SURVEY)
Constructed scenario, not real. The cafeteria manager at the made-up Fairview High wants student input before choosing a new lunch option. Watch how each answer on the checklist follows from the one before it.
Checklist questionOur answer
1. Question and aimWhich of three proposed new lunch options (pasta bar, build-your-own bowl, or wraps) do Fairview students prefer? Aim: describe.
2. PopulationAll 1,260 students enrolled at Fairview High this term.
3. VariablesPreferred option (categorical: pasta bar, bowl, wraps) and grade level (categorical: 9 to 12). We are describing, not explaining, so there is no explanatory or response variable. We report the proportion choosing each option. Grade level lets us check whether the grades agree.
4. DesignSample survey. The aim is to estimate a preference, not to test what a change causes. Asking every student (a census) would work but would take far longer than asking a well-chosen few.
5. Sampling planStratified by grade, in proportion, n = 90, drawn at random from each grade’s enrollment list. Grade sizes are 378, 336, 294, and 252, so each grade’s share is 90 × (grade size ÷ 1,260): 27, 24, 21, and 18, which add to 90. We stratify because tastes may differ by grade, and this way no grade can be missed by bad luck.
6. AssignmentNone. We give no treatment. Nothing is assigned, so we will make no cause-and-effect claim.
7. Risks and what we doBias from the question: neutral wording, and the three options appear in a different random order on different forms, so the first option does not get a boost. Nonresponse: forms are filled out in homeroom, with one follow-up for anyone absent. If only 54 of 90 answer (60%), we compare who answered with the plan (27, 24, 21, 18) and report the gap. Honesty of answers: forms are anonymous.
WHAT WE CAN AND CANNOT CONCLUDE
Because chance chose the students within every grade, we can estimate which option Fairview students prefer, and we should state that the estimate carries a chance swing. We cannot say why they prefer it, cannot speak for staff or next year’s students, and if many chosen students did not answer, our estimate may lean toward the ones who did. A survey describes; it does not explain.
Why this answer? The aim was to describe, we could list everyone, and the groups (grades) plausibly differ. That points to a survey with stratification.
🍅 WORKED MINI-DESIGN 2 — DOES A NEW FERTILIZER HELP TOMATOES? (A RANDOMIZED EXPERIMENT WITH BLOCKS)
Constructed scenario, not real. The made-up Mill Creek Greenhouse wants to know whether a new fertilizer is worth buying. This is a cause question about something we may freely assign, so the experiment is the right tool.
Checklist questionOur answer
1. Question and aimDoes the new fertilizer, called Grow-Plus, increase tomato yield compared with the usual fertilizer? Aim: cause.
2. PopulationTomato plants of this one variety grown in this made-up Mill Creek Greenhouse. We will be careful not to speak beyond that.
3. VariablesExplanatory: fertilizer type (categorical: Grow-Plus or usual). Response: total mass of ripe tomatoes per plant over a 10-week harvest, in kilograms. Other variables that could matter: bench position (light and temperature), water, pot size, seedling size at the start.
4. DesignRandomized experiment, with blocks. We care about cause, and we can assign fertilizer to plants with no ethical problem.
5. Sampling planThe greenhouse has 24 seedlings of the same variety, age, and size, spread over 3 benches of 8. We use all 24. They are not a random sample of all tomato plants, which limits what we may generalize to (see the conclusion).
6. AssignmentBlock by bench, because the benches get different light and heat. Within each bench, shuffle the 8 plant ID numbers with a random number beside each and sort. The first 4 get Grow-Plus and the other 4 get the usual fertilizer. That is 12 plants per treatment (replication). The usual-fertilizer plants are the comparison group: same pots, same water, same schedule, so the only planned difference is the fertilizer. Bags are labeled with codes, and the person who harvests and weighs does not know which code is which (blinding).
7. Risks and what we doConfounding from bench position: blocks put 4 and 4 on every bench, so bench cannot favor one fertilizer. Hidden differences between plants: random assignment spreads them across both groups. Expectation bias at harvest: blinding. One odd plant deciding the result: 12 per group. Nonresponse: not an issue for plants, but we record any plant that dies and report it.
WHAT WE CAN AND CANNOT CONCLUDE
If the Grow-Plus plants out-yield the usual-fertilizer plants by more than the random assignment alone would likely produce, we can conclude that Grow-Plus caused the extra yield for plants like these, in this greenhouse, over this 10-week harvest. We cannot say it works for other tomato varieties, outdoors, or in another climate, because these 24 plants were not a random sample of all tomato plants, and 24 plants is a small study.
Why this answer? Random assignment, a fair comparison, blinding, and blocks are exactly the tools that let an experiment support cause. Notice that nothing about the sampling is random here, and the conclusion is narrower for that reason.
😴 WORKED MINI-DESIGN 3 — DO TEENS WHO SLEEP LESS HAVE LOWER GRADES? (AN OBSERVATIONAL STUDY WITH CAREFUL CLAIMS)
Constructed scenario, not real. This one tempts us to jump to cause. Look at how the design, and the ethics, push the answer toward a careful, limited claim.
Checklist questionOur answer
1. Question and aimAmong Lakeview students, are shorter school-night sleep times linked to lower grades? Aim: find a link (an association). Cause is what we would love to know, but we will see that the design cannot give it.
2. PopulationAll 1,000 students enrolled at the made-up Lakeview High (grades 9 to 12).
3. VariablesExplanatory: average school-night sleep, in hours, from a 7-night log. Response: semester GPA, in grade points (0 to 4.0). Possible confounders to measure: hours of after-school work, daily screen time, and a simple stress rating.
4. DesignObservational study. We measure students as they already are. An experiment would have to assign some teens to sleep much less than others for weeks, which is unethical (it could harm health and safety, and grades) and impractical (we cannot control how long teens sleep at home).
5. Sampling planStratified by grade, in proportion, n = 120, drawn at random from each grade’s list. Grade sizes are 280, 270, 250, and 200, so 120 × (grade size ÷ 1,000) gives 33.6, 32.4, 30, and 24, which round to 34, 32, 30, and 24, adding to exactly 120. Students log their sleep for 7 nights; GPA comes from school records with permission.
6. AssignmentNothing is assigned, and that is the whole point: students keep their own habits. Because we cannot randomize sleep, we measure work hours, screen time, and stress and compare students who are alike on those, to see whether the link survives.
7. Risks and what we doConfounding: a student with a heavy work schedule may both sleep less and have less study time; we measure work and compare like with like, but we cannot measure everything. Reverse direction: low grades may cause worry and then lost sleep. Measurement error: self-reported sleep is imprecise; a daily log is better than memory. Nonresponse: reminders in homeroom, and we report how the responders compare with the plan (34, 32, 30, 24).
WHAT WE CAN AND CANNOT CONCLUDE
If the sample shows that students who sleep fewer hours tend to have lower GPAs, we can say that, for Lakeview students, shorter sleep is associated with lower grades, even after comparing students with similar work hours, screen time, and stress. We cannot say that sleeping less causes lower grades, because we did not assign the sleep, other factors we did not measure may explain the link, and the cause may even run the other way. We also cannot speak for other schools.
Why this answer? The aim was a link, the only way to test cause would be unethical, so the responsible move is an observational study with a claim that stops where the design stops. Correlation is not causation; here the design is honest about that.

🏭 WHERE PEOPLE USE THIS · RANDOMIZED AND OBSERVATIONAL STUDIES TOGETHER
Medical researchers commonly test a new treatment in a randomized, blinded trial, while questions about harms that cannot ethically be assigned are often studied by observing groups as they already are, and the details of each study’s design decide how strong a claim it can support.
💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · EXCEL: A PLANNING TABLE FOR STRATIFIED ALLOCATION, AND A RANDOM SHUFFLE
Planning table. List each group and its size, add the sizes for N with =SUM(B2:B5), put your sample size n in its own cell, and compute each group’s share with =ROUND($F$1*B2/$B$6,0). Add the allocations with =SUM(C2:C5) and test the total with =IF(C6=F1,"OK","adjust"). Always check that the allocations add to n before you trust the table.

Random shuffle. As you did earlier this week, put the list in one column, put =RAND() beside each item, freeze the numbers with Copy and Paste Special → Values, then sort both columns by the random numbers. The top half of the shuffled list is one group and the bottom half is the other. Full click-by-click steps are in Activity 2.

Desmos Scientific is for plain arithmetic only. Type 90*378/1260 to get 27, and 120*280/1000 to get 33.6.
🤖 AI TRAINING · HAVE AN AI CRITIQUE YOUR OWN DESIGN, THEN JUDGE THE CRITIQUE (PROMPT → PREDICT → VERIFY)
Predict first. Do this after you finish Activity 4. Before you open any AI tool, reread your design and write down the two weakest points you can find yourself. (This is your prediction.)
Prompt. Give the AI your full design, with this request: “Here is a study design written by a high-school statistics student. Critique it. Name the three biggest weaknesses, say which step of the design each one is in, and say what claims in the final paragraph would be too strong. [paste your design]”
Verify. Judge each critique with these four questions.
▶ 1. Is it specific? Does the critique point to a particular step in your design (“your sampling plan draws only from volunteers”), or could it be pasted under anyone’s design (“consider a larger sample”)? Generic critiques do not stick.
▶ 2. Is it true? Check the claim against your own text. Did the AI misread your design type (calling an observational study an experiment), invent a step you never wrote, or praise something you left out?
▶ 3. Is the fix allowed? Would the fix be ethical and possible? An AI may suggest randomly assigning people to a harmful condition, or doing something you have no way to carry out.
▶ 4. Does it fix the right kind of error? Bias and confounding are not cured by a bigger sample. Random assignment protects a cause claim, and random sampling protects a claim about a population. Watch for a critique that mixes these up.
Then decide which critiques stick (keep and use), which need revision, and which to reject. In your notebook, write: one critique you kept and what you changed in your design because of it; one critique you rejected and which of the four questions it failed; and whether the AI caught either of the two weak points you predicted. Do not paste in the AI’s fix without checking it against the checklist.

2
(a) What share of the households in the made-up Brook County have a working smoke detector? (b) Does a new brand of cough drop shorten how long a sore throat lasts? (c) Do people who live near busy roads have more asthma? (d) Do students who play a musical instrument have higher math scores?
3
For one of (a) to (d), write one sentence on what you could not claim from the design you chose.
▶  Check
(a) Sample survey: the aim is to describe a share, so take a random sample of households. (b) Experiment: we can ethically assign cough drops; use random assignment and a comparison group (such as an identical-looking plain lozenge) and blind the people who judge recovery. (c) Observational: we cannot assign where people live, and people near busy roads may differ in other ways (income, housing age), so only a link can be claimed. (d) Observational: students choose to play, and instrument-playing may go with other advantages (time, family resources, interest in school), so we cannot claim music causes higher scores. (An experiment assigning students to lessons is possible in principle, but would be a different, larger study.) Sample limits: from (a) you could not say why households do or do not have detectors; from (b) you could not speak beyond the kind of people in the study; from (c) or (d) you could not claim cause.

Critique It: Repair a Flawed Design
30 min
1
Read Ms. Okafor’s design (constructed, not real).
Ms. Okafor (a made-up teacher) wants to know whether playing quiet classical music while studying raises quiz scores for high-school students. She asks for volunteers in her 3rd-period class, and 14 students sign up. They study with the music on for one week. Her 6th-period class, 26 students, studies in silence. Both classes take the same vocabulary quiz on Friday. The music group averages 4 points higher (a made-up result). She announces: “Classical music raises quiz scores for high-school students.”
2
Error analysis. List at least four problems with the design or the conclusion. For each, name the checklist question it breaks (1 to 8) and say which way it could mislead.
3
Write a repaired design in the checklist order (questions 1 to 8), using what she has available: the two classes, a Friday quiz, and willing students. Keep it practical. Finish with a one-paragraph what we can and cannot conclude.
▶  Check and model repair
Problems (accept any four, or others you can defend): (1) Volunteers chose the treatment, and volunteers may be more motivated (breaks Q6, confounding). (2) The groups differ in other ways: different class period, time of day, classmates, and 14 versus 26 students (Q6, Q7, confounding). (3) No random assignment (Q6). (4) One teacher and one pair of classes cannot speak for all high-school students (Q2, Q5, overreach in Q8). (5) The quiz is scored by someone who knows who had music; a blind scorer is better (Q6, blinding). (6) One week and one quiz is thin, and 14 students is a small group (replication). Model repair: Q1: Does studying with classical music raise vocabulary-quiz scores for students in these two classes? Aim: cause. Q2: students in Ms. Okafor’s 3rd- and 6th-period classes who agree to take part. Q3: explanatory: music or silence while studying (categorical); response: Friday vocabulary quiz score, in points out of the quiz total. Q4: randomized experiment with blocks. Q5: all willing students from both classes (say how many; they are volunteers, not a random sample). Q6: block by class period; within each, give every student a random number with =RAND() and sort, the top half studying with music and the bottom half in silence (same room, same time, earbuds for both groups, only the music differs); a colleague scores quizzes labeled by code only. Q7: class period (blocks), volunteer motivation (random assignment balances it within the volunteers), scorer expectation (blind scoring), chance (repeat the week, or compare several quizzes). Q8: “If the music group scores higher by more than chance assignment would likely produce, music caused the difference for these volunteers. Because they volunteered and came from two classes, we cannot say it holds for all students.”

▶  What strong designs tend to look like
Prompt A: a survey, stratified by grade (for example, 1,000 students split proportionally), with the two options shown in random order and a plan for nonresponse; conclusion limited to this school’s students and to preference, not reasons. Prompt B: an experiment, probably blocked by homeroom (8 blocks), randomly choosing 4 of 8 homerooms, or better, randomly choosing half of each homeroom’s students; the comparison group gets no reminder; note that blinding the students is not possible, but the person recording on-time homework can be kept unaware; conclusion about 7th graders at this school with permission. Prompt C: either is defensible. A strong observational answer measures other factors (sleep, hunger, time of day) and claims only a link. A strong experimental answer explains that withholding breakfast from teens is an ethical concern and might propose a safe variation, such as providing a free breakfast option to randomly chosen homerooms. Both must limit the claim. Prompt D: an experiment, blocked by experience level (beginners and experienced), random assignment within each block, a comparison group using the usual shoe, and awareness that runners will know which shoe they wear (blinding is limited), so use a standard soreness scale and a person who scores without knowing the shoe.
