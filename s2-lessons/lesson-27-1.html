⚖️ RESPONSIBILITY: DESCRIBING A RELATIONSHIP HONESTLY
Two numbers can be measured on the same people, the same days, the same towns. Put one on the horizontal axis, the other on the vertical axis, and a picture appears: one dot for each person, day, or town. That picture is a scatter plot, and it is one of the most useful pictures in statistics because it lets us see whether two things move together.
In Week 21 you met scatter plots informally: you looked for direction, form, strength, and unusual points, and you learned to tell the explanatory variable from the response variable. This week we make that language exact, put a number on it, and then use the number to draw a line through the data. Along the way there is a discipline to practice, and that discipline is responsibility. Whoever describes a relationship is answerable for the description. A careless sentence (“this causes that,” “these are unrelated,” “the numbers prove it”) can steer a decision, and the person who wrote it owns the result.
So today you will practice two habits side by side. First, say what the picture really shows, in four precise parts. Second, claim no more than the picture supports. A responsible description is specific enough to be useful and modest enough to be true. Every dataset in this lesson is constructed (made up) to teach the ideas; none of it is real data.
🔍 FROM DOTS TO A DESCRIPTION: EXPLANATORY AND RESPONSE
Each point on a scatter plot is one case (one student, one day, one town) measured on two numerical variables. The horizontal axis carries the explanatory variable, the one we use to explain or predict. The vertical axis carries the response variable, the one we expect to depend on it. If you ask “does studying more go with a higher score?” then hours studied is explanatory and score is the response.
Choosing which is which is a statement about how you plan to use the data. It is not a claim that one causes the other. Labeled axes with units are part of an honest plot: a scatter plot with no axis titles is a riddle.
📝 THE FOUR-PART DESCRIPTION
To describe a scatter plot, answer four questions, in this order. Write them as a sentence or two, always in the context of the variables, never as bare words.
▶ 1. Direction. Do the points tend to rise from left to right (positive association: larger x goes with larger y), fall (negative association: larger x goes with smaller y), or show no tilt?
▶ 2. Form. Do the points follow a straight line (linear), a curve (rising then falling, or bending), or no pattern at all? Say which.
▶ 3. Strength. How tightly do the points hug the pattern? Tight is strong, a loose band is moderate, a wide cloud is weak. Below, a number (r) will make this exact for straight-line patterns.
▶ 4. Unusual features. Is any point far from the rest (an outlier)? Do the points form separate groups (clusters)? Is there a gap?
A model sentence: “Among these ten students, more hours of study tended to go with higher quiz scores: a positive, roughly linear, fairly strong association, with no outliers.” Notice the hedge: tended to. It describes a tendency, not a law.

🔢 THE CORRELATION COEFFICIENT, r
Words like “strong” and “moderate” are judgments, and two people can disagree. Statisticians therefore use a number, the correlation coefficient, written r. You do not need to compute it by hand; in Week 28 you will have Excel do it. For now, learn what it means.
▶ r always lies between −1 and +1.
▶ The sign is the direction: positive r for a rising pattern, negative r for a falling one.
▶ The size (how far from 0) is the strength of the straight-line pattern. r = +1 or −1 means every point lies exactly on a line; r near 0 means no straight-line tilt.
▶ r has no units, and it does not change if you swap which variable is on which axis.
Here is a rough guide, not a rule. Different fields draw the lines in different places, and the same r can look different depending on how many points there are.
Size of r (ignore the sign)A rough description
about 0.8 to 1strong straight-line pattern
about 0.5 to 0.8moderate
about 0.2 to 0.5weak
below about 0.2little or no straight-line pattern
Two cautions already. First, r measures only straight-line association. A strong curved pattern can have r near 0, as Example 3 will show. Second, r is one number summarizing a whole picture, so it can be pulled around by a single unusual point (Example 4). Always look at the plot as well as the number, and never quote r to more decimal places than the data deserve.

Constructed (made-up) data.
▶ Direction. There is no single direction: the points rise and then fall.
▶ Form. A clear curve (an arch).
▶ Strength. The points hug the arch very tightly. As a curved relationship it is strong.
▶ Unusual features. None.
Now the computed correlation: r = −0.01, essentially 0. The left half of the arch pushes r up, the right half pushes it down, and the two cancel. This is the most important warning about r: it answers “how close to a straight line?” and nothing else. A value near 0 does not mean the variables are unrelated. Here height is almost completely determined by time. Someone who reported only “r is about 0, so time and height are unrelated” would be reporting something false. Draw the plot first.

▶ Direction and form. Nine points rise in a tight straight band: positive, linear.
▶ Strength. For those nine, very strong.
▶ Unusual features. One outlier: week 10 at 8 cm sits far below where the band would put it.
With all ten points, r = +0.39: weak to moderate. With the outlier set aside, the other nine points give r = +0.99. One point moved r from about +0.99 to about +0.39.
Does that mean we delete the outlier? No, not automatically. The responsible move is to ask why. Here we know the plant was bent, so reporting both numbers and the reason is honest. If we did not know why, deleting it to make r look better would be hiding evidence. Report the plot, report r both ways, and say what you know.
⚠️ ASSOCIATION IS NOT CAUSATION
Everything in this lesson describes how two variables move together in a set of cases. None of it shows that one variable makes the other change. Even a very strong association (an r near +1) is compatible with several stories: x might cause y, y might cause x, a third variable might drive both, or the pattern might be chance in a small dataset.
The study hours example is a good test. Students who study more may score higher because studying works. They may also be students who find the subject easier, who sleep more, or who care more, and each of those could lift both numbers. The scatter plot cannot tell these stories apart. Only a well-designed study (Module 11 takes up study design) can address cause, and even then with care. In Week 28 you will give this idea a full lesson.
What you may say: “is associated with,” “tends to go with,” “is related to.” What you may not say from a scatter plot alone: “causes,” “leads to,” “is responsible for,” “proves.” Choosing the right verb is the responsibility part of this lesson.
💡 WHERE THIS LEADS: FITTING A LINE
If a scatter plot shows a roughly linear pattern, we can summarize it with a line and use the line to make predictions. Which line is “best,” and how do we decide? That is Lesson 27.2. Today's job is to decide whether a line is a sensible summary at all: is the form linear, and is the strength worth summarizing? A straight line fitted to the ball toss in Example 3 would be a poor description, even though the points follow a clean curve.

💡 WHAT THE FOUR SETS SHOW (READ AFTER YOU HAVE WRITTEN YOUR OWN NOTES)
The widget's four sets in When r misleads each teach one lesson about the number r:
▶ A. A curve. r = −0.003, essentially zero, yet the points follow a clear curve. Same lesson as Example 3.
▶ B. An outlier. r = +0.377 with the far point and +0.975 without it. One point can hide a strong pattern.
▶ C. Two clusters. Taken together r = +0.956, which looks like a very strong line, but each cluster by itself has r near zero. The high number comes from the two groups sitting far apart, not from a pattern inside either group.
▶ D. A straight line. r = +0.972: here r does what we hope, because the pattern really is a straight line.
The widget also warns that r measures straight-line association only, and that a high r does not show that x causes y. That is the whole lesson in one line.

Excel draws the picture. Select the two columns of numbers, including the headings, then Insert > Scatter (X, Y) > the dots-only option; add axis titles and a chart title with the + (Chart Elements) button. Put the explanatory variable in the left column so that it lands on the horizontal axis. (Excel's correlation formula arrives in Week 28; this week you estimate r by eye.)

Desmos Scientific handles plain arithmetic. To see how steeply a pattern rises, find the slope between two points by typing the rise over the run. Using the first and last study points from Example 1, (0.5, 58) and (4.5, 77), type (77-58)/(4.5-0.5) and read 4.75: between those two points the score rose 4.75 points per extra hour. That is not the best line for the whole dataset (two points never are), and Lesson 27.2 shows how to find a better one.
🌐 WHERE THIS MATTERS
Scatter plots and correlation are used across many fields to look for patterns between two measurements: in health research, in weather and climate records, and in sports analytics, for example. In each, the first question is the one you practiced today: what does the picture actually show? And in each, a careful reader holds a firm caution: an association in observational data is a reason to investigate further, not a conclusion about cause. Responsible work reports the pattern, its strength, any unusual points, and what the data cannot say.
🤖 AI TRAINING · PROMPT → PREDICT → VERIFY
Prompt. Give an AI tool this constructed dataset and ask for r: “Twelve students did practice problems. x is the number of practice problems done and y is the quiz score. The pairs (x, y) are: (1, 40); (2, 55); (3, 48); (4, 62); (5, 58); (6, 70); (7, 64); (8, 75); (9, 68); (10, 82); (11, 72); (12, 78). Describe the scatter plot and estimate the correlation coefficient r. Explain how you decided.”
Predict. Before you read its answer, sketch the twelve points yourself. They rise from left to right along a fairly tight, straight band, with a few points a little off it: positive, linear, strong. Write down your own estimate of r as a number.
Verify. The Pearson correlation of these twelve pairs, computed by a program, is r = 0.8942, about 0.89. For a sense of what a pattern of this size looks like, play a few rounds of Guess r in the game. Compare your estimate, the AI's estimate, and 0.89.
Flag the errors. A weak answer gives r to many decimal places as if it had computed it exactly (false precision: an eyeball estimate is good to roughly one decimal place), says “this shows practice causes higher scores,” gets the direction wrong, or never mentions form or unusual points. Ask a follow-up: “Did you compute r or estimate it?” Write three sentences grading the AI: which of the four parts it covered, how close its r was, and anything it overclaimed.
