Lesson 34.3
Is the Difference Real? Significance by Simulation
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A result is called “real” only after you have asked how often plain chance, in a world where nothing is going on, would produce something just as striking.
The boring world (null model), the simulated p-value, the 5% convention, what a p-value is and is not, statistical significance versus practical importance, and why honest reporting includes the results that were not significant
Reading & work: Read every section in order, work each example on paper before you read its solution, use the Significance Simulator, follow the Excel steps, and complete the three activities at the bottom. The reading, interactive, and Excel steps take about 35 minutes and the activities about 40 more, so plan on about 75 minutes in all.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to describe the four moves of a simulation test: name the boring world (the model where nothing interesting is going on), choose a statistic from the data, simulate many boring worlds, and report the share at least as extreme as the data, the p-value.
2
I will be able to estimate a p-value with the Significance Simulator and apply a rule fixed in advance (the 5% convention) to call a result statistically significant or not.
3
I will be able to say what a p-value is and what it is not (not the probability the claim is true, not the probability of being wrong, not the size or importance of an effect), and tell statistically significant from practically important.
4
I will be able to explain why running many tests produces false alarms by chance, and why honest reporting includes the non-significant results.

✨ A WONDER TO START WITH
A made-up student council settles disputes by flipping one school coin. A member grumbles that the coin favors heads. To test it, the council flips the coin 100 times and gets 60 heads. “There it is, the coin is rigged!” says one member. “Sixty is only ten away from fifty,” says another. “Anyone could get that.” Which member is right? Staring at the number will not settle it. What settles it is a question you can answer by simulation: if the coin were perfectly fair, how often would 100 flips look this lopsided?
You have already asked that question twice this week. In Comparing Two Populations from Two Random Samples you asked how far apart two sample means can be. In Comparing Two Treatments in a Randomized Experiment you shuffled group labels to see whether chance alone could produce the gap you saw. Today we name the idea behind both, make it general, and use it on the coin so that you can see the whole machine in one picture.
Service, this module’s virtue, comes with a responsibility here. People who lean on your numbers (a council, a clinic, a classroom teacher) will often hear only the word “significant.” Serving them well means telling them what the word does and does not mean, and reporting the results that did not turn out exciting along with the ones that did.
A note on honesty. Every coin result, group, and study in this lesson is constructed (made up) or simulated by a computer to teach the idea. Nothing here is real data.
🎲 THE BORING WORLD AND THE FOUR MOVES
Every simulation test has the same four moves. Learn them once and you can run them on any claim.
▶ Move 1: Name the boring world. This is the model in which nothing interesting is going on: the coin is fair, the treatment does nothing, the two populations are alike. Statisticians call it the null model. We pretend it is true so that we can see what plain chance does on its own.
▶ Move 2: Choose a statistic from the data. A number that captures what caught your eye: the number of heads, the difference of two means, the difference of two proportions.
▶ Move 3: Simulate many boring worlds. Generate the data again and again as the boring world would, and record the statistic each time. A computer can do a thousand worlds in a moment.
▶ Move 4: Count the share at least as extreme. What fraction of the simulated boring worlds produced a statistic as far out as yours, or farther? That share is the p-value. When it comes from a simulation, the simulator calls it the empirical p-value, because it was observed from simulated worlds instead of computed from a formula.
Here are two settings you already know, written in the same four-move language:
SettingThe boring worldThe statistic“At least as extreme” means
A coin (today)The coin is fair: every flip is heads with chance 0.5Number of heads in 100 flips60 or more heads or 40 or fewer (in either direction)
A randomized experiment (the plant data from the last lesson)The fertilizer does nothing, so the group labels are arbitrary and shuffling them is harmlessObserved difference in means (B minus A)A shuffled gap at least as big as 1.39 cm, in either direction
“Extreme” can mean one direction or both. A one-sided count looks at only one tail (at or beyond your result, in the direction you care about). A two-sided count looks at both tails: results as far from what the boring world expects, in either direction. In the simulator these are the toggle One-sided (at or beyond) and Two-sided. Unless there is a strong reason to care about only one direction, and that reason was settled before the data came in, use two-sided. It is the honest default, and it is what you did with the shuffle histogram.
One more honest note. Real studies often use a formula-based method that gives nearly the same answer as the simulation without running thousands of worlds. You may meet those formulas later. Simulation comes first here because it shows what the answer is an answer to.
📑 THE 5% CONVENTION, AND WHY IT IS A CONVENTION
Once you have a p-value, you need a way to talk about it. By long-standing convention, a result is called statistically significant when fewer than 5% of boring worlds are at least this extreme: a p-value below 0.05. The Significance Simulator shows this in a panel called The 5% convention.
Three cautions keep that convention honest.
▶ It is a convention, not a law of nature. Nothing special happens at 0.05. A p-value of 0.049 and a p-value of 0.051 describe almost the same amount of surprise. Some fields use stricter lines such as 1%.
▶ Decide the rule before you look. Choose the line (5%) and the sides (two-sided) first. If you pick the rule after seeing the answer, you can always find one that makes your result look good, and then the p-value stops meaning anything.
▶ Borderline results are borderline. A simulation is itself random. Try 1,000 boring worlds again and again for the same data and the empirical p-value moves a little each run. For a result whose true two-sided p-value is about 0.057 (you will meet one in a moment), repeated runs of 1,000 worlds typically land between about 0.047 and 0.069, so some runs fall just under 0.05 and some just over. Do not let a coin toss of a simulation decide the verdict; report the number and say it is near the edge.
💭 WHAT A P-VALUE IS, AND WHAT IT IS NOT
The p-value answers exactly one question: if nothing interesting were going on, how often would boring randomness alone produce a result like mine, or more extreme? That is all. People read much more into it, so learn the four most common overreadings now.
▶ It is not the probability that the claim is true. A p-value of 0.03 does not mean “a 3% chance that the treatment does nothing” or “a 97% chance that it works.” The p-value is computed assuming the boring world; it cannot also tell you how likely that world is.
▶ It is not the probability that you are wrong. It describes how surprising the data would be in the boring world, not how likely a particular conclusion is to be a mistake.
▶ It is not the size of the effect. A tiny p-value can come with a tiny effect (when the sample is huge) and a large effect can come with a modest p-value (when the sample is small).
▶ It is not a verdict that something is important. “Significant” here is a technical word about chance. It does not mean “large,” “useful,” or “worth acting on.”
And one more, in the opposite direction: a large p-value does not show there is no effect. It shows only that boring chance could easily have produced what you saw. A small or noisy study often has a large p-value even when something real is going on. Not significant means “not enough evidence here,” not “nothing is there.”

📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 1: THE SCHOOL COIN, 60 HEADS IN 100
Problem. The made-up student council’s coin gave 60 heads in 100 flips. Decide, using the 5% convention (two-sided, chosen in advance), whether the result is statistically significant.
Teacher’s voice: Move 1. The boring world is a fair coin: each flip is heads with chance 0.5. Move 2. The statistic is the number of heads, which we saw to be 60. Move 3. Simulate many 100-flip fair-coin worlds. A fair coin averages 50 heads. Move 4. “At least as extreme, in either direction” means 60 or more heads or 40 or fewer, because 40 is just as far from 50 as 60 is. The share of boring worlds that land in those tails is about 0.057 (exact arithmetic gives 0.0569; one side alone is 0.0284). So about 6 boring worlds in 100 are this lopsided.
Verdict. 0.057 is not below 0.05, so by our rule the result is not statistically significant. In words: a fair coin produces a result this lopsided about 6 times in 100, which is uncommon but not rare enough to rule out plain chance. Note carefully what we did not prove. We did not show the coin is fair; we showed only that this evidence is not strong enough to call it unfair. Sixty heads sits near the edge of what a fair coin does, so a sensible next step is to flip the coin more, not to announce a verdict.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 2: AN ORDINARY RESULT AND A VERY UNUSUAL ONE
Problem. Use the same boring world (a fair coin). Find the two-sided p-value for (a) 55 heads in 100 flips, and (b) 36 heads in 50 flips.
Teacher’s voice: (a) Extreme means 55 or more heads or 45 or fewer. The share is about 0.37 (exact: 0.3682). More than a third of boring worlds do at least this well, so 55 heads tells us nothing unusual. (b) With 50 flips a fair coin averages 25 heads. Extreme means 36 or more or 14 or fewer. The share is about 0.003 (exact: 0.0026), roughly 1 boring world in 400. That is far below 0.05, so the result is statistically significant: a fair coin would almost never do this.
Notice the pattern. At 100 flips, 60% heads (60 of 100) sits near the edge, and 55% heads is ordinary. At 50 flips, 72% heads (36 of 50) is a big surprise. The p-value depends on how far you are from what the boring world expects measured against how much chance scatter that world has, and the scatter depends on how many flips there are.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 3: THE SAME PERCENT, MORE FLIPS
Problem. What happens to the p-value when the percent of heads stays the same but the number of flips grows?
Teacher’s voice: Here are exact two-sided p-values for a fair coin.

The same 60% that was merely suggestive at 100 flips is overwhelming at 1,000. Why? More flips means less chance scatter in the percent of heads (this is the same pattern as the shrinking standard error you saw earlier in the module). A 10-point gap is easy for 100 flips to produce by luck and nearly impossible for 1,000. One honest limit: a simulation of 1,000 worlds cannot resolve a p-value like 0.0000000003. It would show 0 red-tail worlds, and all you could say is “fewer than about 1 in 1,000.” (In the comparison tool, the made-up “clear difference” data hit the same limit: 0 of 20,000 shuffles.)
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 4: SIGNIFICANT BUT NOT IMPORTANT
Problem. A made-up school flips its kickoff coin 10,000 times in a computer test and gets 5,100 heads (51%). Is that statistically significant? Does it matter?
Teacher’s voice: The boring world is a fair coin and the average is 5,000 heads. Extreme means 5,100 or more or 4,900 or fewer. The share is about 0.047 (exact: 0.0466), which is below 0.05, so by the 5% convention the result is statistically significant. But look at the size of the effect: the coin lands heads 51% of the time instead of 50%. For deciding who kicks off, that is a trivial difference; nobody would replace the coin. So the result is statistically significant but not practically important. With enough data, even a tiny effect can clear the 5% line. Significance tells you chance is an unlikely explanation. It does not tell you the effect is big.
The reverse also happens. In the last lesson, the made-up “barely a difference” data had a shuffle share of about 0.76, so chance easily explains the gap. But a small study can also miss a real, meaningful effect because there are too few observations to separate it from chance. A large p-value with a small sample is a reason to gather more data, not to declare the effect absent.
🔗 CONNECTING THE PIECES FROM THIS WEEK
You have now seen three tools that answer related questions. In the plant experiment, the bootstrap range for the true difference was about 0.68 to 2.10 cm, which stays well away from zero, and the shuffle share was about 0.001. In the “barely a difference” data, the bootstrap range was about −1.8 to 2.6, which includes zero, and the shuffle share was about 0.76. In the seed experiment, the shuffle share was about 0.20. Usually (not always, because the two methods answer slightly different questions) an interval that stays away from zero goes with a small p-value, and an interval that includes zero goes with a large one.
The two tools also say different things. The p-value speaks only to whether chance alone is a reasonable explanation. The interval tells you the size of the difference that is plausible, and that is usually the more useful half of the story.
🎲 MANY TESTS, A FEW FALSE ALARMS
There is a trap hiding in the 5% convention. Suppose a made-up club tests 20 different ideas, and in every one of them the boring world is actually true (nothing is going on). Each test has a 5% chance of looking significant purely by luck, and a 95% chance of not doing so. If the 20 tests are independent (a simplifying assumption), the chance that none of them looks significant is 0.9520 = 0.358. So the chance that at least one does is 1 − 0.358 = 0.642. About 64 times in 100, a club that tests 20 empty ideas will turn up a “significant” one. On average that is about 1 false alarm in 20 tests.
This matters for honesty and for service. If the club tells the council, “Idea 14 is significant!” and says nothing about the 19 others, the council is misled, because the surprising result was selected from many tries. The honest practice is to say how many comparisons were made, to decide which ones to run in advance, and to report the ones that were not significant too. A small, honest negative result is a real contribution; it can save a community from acting on a false alarm.
💻 EXCEL: A SMALL NULL-WORLD SIMULATOR, CLICK BY CLICK
You will build a tiny simulator that does what the Significance Simulator does, using a formula that draws a fair-coin world. Everything here is made up. This is a practice build; you do not upload it (Assignment 34.3 is answered in Canvas). Excel behavior in this section has not been tested against the live program by the course team, so if a step does not work, tell your teacher which step it was.
▶ Open Excel and start a Blank workbook. Save it as Wk34_L3_YourName. Rename the first sheet NullWorlds (right-click the sheet tab at the bottom, choose Rename, type the name, press Enter).
▶ One boring world. In A1 type =BINOM.INV(100,0.5,RAND()) and press Enter. The formula asks Excel for a random number of heads in 100 flips of a fair coin. The three inputs are the number of flips (100), the chance of heads (0.5), and a random number between 0 and 1 supplied by RAND(). Press F9 a few times and watch the number change; it should hover around 50.
▶ One thousand boring worlds. Click the Name Box (the small box just left of the formula bar), type A1:A1000, and press Enter. That selects 1,000 cells. Now type =BINOM.INV(100,0.5,RAND()) (do not press Enter alone) and press Ctrl + Enter, which fills every selected cell with the formula.
▶ Your observed result. In C1 type Observed heads and in D1 type 60. In C2 type Mirror and in D2 type =100-D1 (this is 100 minus 60, the mirror image of 60).
▶ Count the extremes. In C3 type Extreme worlds and in D3 type =COUNTIF(A1:A1000,">="&D1)+COUNTIF(A1:A1000,"<="&D2). This counts the worlds with 60 or more heads plus the worlds with 40 or fewer.
▶ Turn the count into a p-value. In C4 type Empirical p-value and in D4 type =D3/1000. Format D4 with three decimals, or leave it as it is.
▶ Run it again. Press F9. All 1,000 worlds are redrawn and the p-value changes. Press it ten times and write down the p-values. They should usually fall between about 0.04 and 0.08, near the exact value 0.057. That wobble is the simulation error you read about earlier in this lesson.
▶ Try it. Change D1 to 55 (the mirror cell updates by itself) and press F9 a few times. Which p-value do you expect? Then change D1 to 65. What happens to the count?
💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · NULL-WORLD COUNTS IN EXCEL; P-VALUE ARITHMETIC IN DESMOS
Excel. =BINOM.INV(100,0.5,RAND()) draws one fair-coin world of 100 flips. Fill it down a column to get many worlds, then count the extremes with =COUNTIF(range,">="&obs) plus =COUNTIF(range,"<="&mirror), and divide by the number of worlds. For a set of simulated gaps you can use the same idea: =COUNTIF(range,">="&obs)+COUNTIF(range,"<="&-obs) counts the ones at least as big as your observed gap in either direction. Press F9 to redraw all the random numbers.
Desmos Scientific handles plain arithmetic only here. An empirical p-value is a count divided by the number of worlds: type 130 ÷ 2000 to get 0.065. The false-alarm arithmetic is 1 − 0.95 raised to the 20th power: type 1 − 0.95, then use the exponent key for the power of 20 (you should see about 0.642). Desmos has no built-in simulation here; the simulating belongs to the widget and to Excel.
🤖 AI TRAINING · ASK AN AI TO DESIGN A SIMULATION TEST, THEN RUN IT YOURSELF
Prompt. Ask an AI tool: “A coin landed heads 62 times in 100 flips. Design a simulation test to decide whether the coin is fair. Say what the null model is, what statistic you use, how many simulations to run, and how you would read the p-value.”
Predict. Before you read its answer, write your own design in four lines: the boring world (a fair coin), the statistic (the number of heads), the simulation (many 100-flip fair-coin worlds), and what “extreme” means (62 or more, or 38 or fewer, in the two-sided version). Predict the p-value: because 60 heads was near the edge, will 62 be below or above 0.05?
Verify. Run the AI’s design in the Significance Simulator (n = 100, observed 62, two-sided). You should find a p-value near 0.02 (exact arithmetic gives 0.0210). Then read the AI’s explanation sentence by sentence. Look especially for the most common error: saying the p-value is “the probability that the coin is fair” or “the probability the result is due to chance.” Also check whether it chose one-sided or two-sided and whether it said why, and whether it claims the test “proves” anything. Rewrite its worst sentence so that it is true. Verdict. A tidy design is not a correct explanation; your simulation is the referee.

1. 23 ÷ 1000 = 0.023. That is below 0.05, so the result is statistically significant at 5%. If the rule had been 1% (0.01), 0.023 is not below it, so the result would not be significant.
