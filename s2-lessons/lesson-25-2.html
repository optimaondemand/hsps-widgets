🎯 WONDER: THE SAME THREE NUMBERS, OVER AND OVER
In the last lesson you built the normal model and measured how far a value sits from the mean in standard deviations, the z-score. Here is the strange part. Take any normal distribution at all: heights, fill weights, quiz scores, measurement errors. Change the mean, stretch the SD, change the units. Cut the curve at the mean and at one, two, and three SDs on each side, and the share of values in each slice comes out the same. Not nearly the same. The same. A bell curve forgets what it is measuring and remembers only how many SDs you have walked from the center.
That regularity is the Empirical Rule, also called the 68–95–99.7 rule. For a normal distribution:
▶ about 68% of the values lie within 1 SD of the mean (between μ − σ and μ + σ);
▶ about 95% lie within 2 SD of the mean;
▶ about 99.7% lie within 3 SD of the mean.
Here μ is the mean and σ the SD of the distribution. The exact normal-model values are 68.27%, 95.45%, and 99.73%. The rule rounds them so you can work in your head. All scenarios in this lesson are constructed (made up) to teach the ideas.
A note on wonder, this module’s virtue. Nothing about heights or test scores forces them to obey one curve with one set of percents. When a measurement is the sum of many small, independent nudges, order shows up anyway. Look at the numbers, then ask why they should be so tidy; Week 26 returns to that question.
📐 THE SLICES: USE SYMMETRY
The three numbers give the middle of the curve. To get the pieces, remember that the normal curve is symmetric about the mean, so each left slice equals its right twin.
The Normal Curve Cut at Whole Standard Deviations (approximate percents)
Each slice shows about what percent of the values fall in it. The two middle slices together are about 68%.
How the slices come from the rule:
▶ Mean to +1 SD: half of 68% is 34%. The same on the left.
▶ +1 SD to +2 SD: within 2 SD is 95%, within 1 SD is 68%, so the two sides together hold 95 − 68 = 27%, and each side holds 13.5%.
▶ +2 SD to +3 SD: 99.7 − 95 = 4.7%, so each side holds 2.35%.
▶ Beyond +3 SD: 100 − 99.7 = 0.3%, so each tail holds about 0.15%.
Check: 34 + 13.5 + 2.35 + 0.15 = 50, one whole half of the curve. Treat these slice percents as about, because they inherit the rounding of 68, 95, and 99.7.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 1: INTERVALS FROM A MEAN AND SD
Scores on a constructed quiz follow a normal distribution with mean 72 and SD 6. Find the intervals for 1, 2, and 3 SD.
▶ 1 SD: 72 − 6 = 66 and 72 + 6 = 78. About 68% of scores are between 66 and 78.
▶ 2 SD: 72 − 2×6 = 60 and 72 + 2×6 = 84. About 95% are between 60 and 84.
▶ 3 SD: 72 − 3×6 = 54 and 72 + 3×6 = 90. About 99.7% are between 54 and 90.
The pattern is mean ± k × SD, with k = 1, 2, 3. Notice what the rule leaves out: it says nothing about 75 or 81, only about the whole-SD cutoffs. Lesson 25.3 handles cutoffs that fall between them.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 2: PERCENT ABOVE, BELOW, AND BETWEEN
Same quiz: mean 72, SD 6. Always draw the curve, mark the cutoffs, and shade what the question asks.
(a) What percent score above 78? 78 is 1 SD above the mean. Within 1 SD holds 68%, so 100 − 68 = 32% lies outside, split evenly into two tails. The upper tail is 32 ÷ 2 = 16%. Another route: 50% above the mean, minus the 34% between 72 and 78, is 16%. The exact normal value is 15.87%.
(b) What percent score below 60? 60 is 2 SD below the mean. Outside 2 SD there is 100 − 95 = 5%, so each tail is 2.5% (exact 2.28%).
(c) What percent score between 66 and 84? That is from 1 SD below to 2 SD above. The left part, 66 to 72, is 34%. The right part, 72 to 84, is half of 95%, which is 47.5%. Total 34 + 47.5 = 81.5%. Equivalently, 68÷2 + 95÷2 = 34 + 47.5. The exact value is 81.86%, so the rule is off by about a third of a percentage point here.
Speak it: explain aloud why the answer to (a) is not 34%. (The 34% is the slice between the mean and +1 SD; the question asked for everything beyond +1 SD.)
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 3: WORKING BACKWARD
The rule also runs in reverse. A constructed batch of seedlings is measured, and the heights are approximately normal. The middle 95% of heights run from 38 cm to 62 cm. Find the mean and SD.
▶ Mean. The interval is centered on the mean, so the mean is the midpoint: (38 + 62) ÷ 2 = 50 cm.
▶ SD. 95% means 2 SD on each side, so the distance from the mean to either end is 2 SD: 62 − 50 = 12 = 2σ, so σ = 6 cm.
▶ Check. 50 ± 2×6 gives 38 and 62. Then 1 SD gives 44 to 56, which should hold about 68% of the seedlings.
If the middle 68% had run from 38 to 62, one SD would be 12 and the SD 12 cm, a very different spread. Read which percent the problem gives before you divide.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 4: FROM PERCENTS TO COUNTS
A constructed school has 2,000 students, and the scores on a school-wide test are approximately normal with mean 72 and SD 6. About how many scored above 84?
▶ 84 is 2 SD above the mean (72 + 2×6 = 84).
▶ Share above 2 SD: (100 − 95) ÷ 2 = 2.5%.
▶ Count: 2.5% of 2,000 = 0.025 × 2,000 = about 50 students.
The exact normal share is 2.275%, which gives about 45.5, so a count near 46. The rule says 50. Both are honest answers to “about how many?”, but say the word about. How many scored between 66 and 78? 68% of 2,000 = 0.68 × 2,000 = about 1,360.
⚠️ WHEN NOT TO USE THE RULE
The rule is a statement about normal distributions. A dataset only earns it if a bell-shaped, symmetric model is a fair description. Before you use it, look at the data (a histogram or dot plot) and ask three questions: Is it roughly symmetric? Is it single-humped? Does the context allow values on both sides of the mean?
Here are fifteen constructed waiting times, in minutes: 1, 1, 2, 2, 2, 3, 3, 3, 4, 4, 5, 6, 8, 11, 35. The mean is 6 minutes and the sample SD is about 8.47 minutes. The rule would say that 95% of waits fall between 6 − 2×8.47 and 6 + 2×8.47, and the lower end of that interval is about −10.9 minutes, which is impossible. Look at the shape: most waits are short, and one long wait (35) drags the mean and SD upward. The median is 3, far below the mean of 6. And 14 of the 15 values (about 93%) lie within 1 SD of the mean, not 68%.
That is a right-skewed distribution, and the 68–95–99.7 percents do not describe it. For skewed data, report the median and IQR, as in Module 8, and describe the shape in words. In Week 26 you will learn how to check, with a histogram and the rule itself, whether a normal model fits at all.

💻 EXCEL: A SMALL EMPIRICAL-RULE TABLE, CLICK BY CLICK
You will build a three-row table of interval endpoints for the quiz example (mean 72, SD 6) and check each interval against the exact normal share. Keep this page open beside Excel. This workbook is practice; you do not upload it. If you keep it, save it as Wk25_L2_YourName.
▶ Open Excel and start a Blank workbook.
▶ Click A1, type Mean, press Tab, type 72, press Enter. Click A2, type SD, press Tab, type 6, press Enter.
▶ Click A4 and type these headings across row 4, pressing Tab after each: k, Lower, Upper, Exact share inside. Press Enter.
▶ In A5, A6, A7 type 1, 2, 3 (one per cell).
▶ Click B5 and type =$B$1-A5*$B$2, press Enter. You should see 66. (The dollar signs keep the mean and SD cells fixed when you copy the formula down.)
▶ Click C5 and type =$B$1+A5*$B$2, Enter. You should see 78.
▶ Click D5 and type =NORM.DIST(C5,$B$1,$B$2,TRUE)-NORM.DIST(B5,$B$1,$B$2,TRUE), Enter. You should see a decimal beginning 0.6826.
▶ Select B5:D5 (click B5, hold Shift, click D5), then drag the small square at the bottom-right corner of the selection down through row 7 to copy the formulas.
▶ Rows 6 and 7 should show 60, 84, 0.9544… and 54, 90, 0.9973…. To read column D as percents, select D5:D7 and choose the % button in the Number group of the Home tab; you should see about 68.27%, 95.45%, 99.73% (the % button may round to whole numbers; add decimals with the Increase Decimal button beside it).
▶ Compare. Your D column should agree with the Empirical Rule’s 68, 95, 99.7 to within about half a percentage point. That small gap is the rounding in the rule.
What is NORM.DIST? The function NORM.DIST(x, mean, standard_dev, TRUE) returns the share of a normal distribution that lies at or below x. Subtracting two of them gives the share between two values, which is how column D works. Lesson 25.3 uses this function fully, including for cutoffs that are not whole SDs. Menu names and the look of the window differ a little between desktop Excel and Excel for the web, but typing a formula in a cell works the same in both.
💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · INTERVAL ENDPOINTS IN DESMOS AND EXCEL
Desmos Scientific does plain arithmetic, so type the endpoints yourself. For mean 72 and SD 6: type 72-2*6 and read 60; type 72+2*6 and read 84. For mean 500 and SD 4: type 500-3*4 (488) and 500+3*4 (512). Pattern: mean − k×SD and mean + k×SD.

Excel builds the table for you: =$B$1-A5*$B$2 and =$B$1+A5*$B$2 for the endpoints, and =NORM.DIST(C5,$B$1,$B$2,TRUE)-NORM.DIST(B5,$B$1,$B$2,TRUE) for the exact share between them. Use the rule for a quick estimate and NORM.DIST when you need the closer number.
🤖 AI TRAINING · PROMPT → PREDICT → VERIFY
Prompt. Ask an AI tool exactly this: “A made-up test has scores that are normally distributed with mean 500 and standard deviation 20. Using the Empirical Rule, what percent of scores are above 540? Show your reasoning.”
Predict. Before you read its answer, work it yourself. 540 is (540 − 500) ÷ 20 = 2 SD above the mean. Outside 2 SD there is about 5%, split into two tails, so you should expect about 2.5%. The exact normal value is 2.275%.
Verify. Compare the AI’s answer with yours. Common errors to look for: answering 5% (it forgot that the 5% is split between two tails), answering 95% or 97.5% (it reported the share below, not above), or mixing up 1, 2, and 3 SD. Does it say “about” and mention that the rule is approximate? Then ask a second question to test it: “Now a list of waiting times is strongly right-skewed. Can I use the Empirical Rule?” A good answer says no, or only with great care. Flag anything wrong, vague, or too confident, and say what you would ask next.

Intervals and Slices
15 min
1
Cereal boxes (constructed) have fill weights that are approximately normal with mean 500 g and SD 4 g. Write the intervals that hold about 68%, 95%, and 99.7% of boxes.
2
Find the share of boxes (a) above 504 g, (b) below 492 g, (c) between 500 g and 508 g, (d) between 496 g and 512 g. Sketch the curve for each.
▶  Check
Intervals: 496 to 504, 492 to 508, 488 to 512. (a) 504 is +1 SD: about 16%. (b) 492 is −2 SD: about 2.5%. (c) 0 to +2 SD: 34 + 13.5 = 47.5%. (d) −1 SD to +3 SD: 34 (496 to 500) + 34 + 13.5 + 2.35 (500 to 512) = 83.85%, about 84%. The exact value is 84.00%.

Explore the Empirical Rule Widget

In the widget (Empirical rule mode), set the mean to 100 and the SD to 15. Record the endpoints of the 1, 2, and 3 SD bands.
2
Change the mean to 40 and the SD to 5 and record the endpoints and the percents again. What changed and what did not?

Read the comparison table. Which is bigger for the 1 SD band, the rule’s 68 or the exact value, and by how much? Which for the 3 SD band?

For mean 100, SD 15: 85 to 115, 70 to 130, 55 to 145. For mean 40, SD 5: 35 to 45, 30 to 50, 25 to 55. Find the Mistake
12 min
1
Dana says, “For a normal distribution, 95% of the data is within 1 SD of the mean, so for mean 72 and SD 6, 95% of scores are between 66 and 78.” Find and correct the error.
2
Lee has the 15 waiting times from this lesson (1, 1, 2, 2, 2, 3, 3, 3, 4, 4, 5, 6, 8, 11, 35; mean 6, SD about 8.47) and says, “By the Empirical Rule, 95% of waits are between −10.9 and 22.9 minutes.” Give two reasons the rule should not be used here.
3
Marco says, “A score 2 SD above the mean puts you above 95% of scores.” Is that right? What is the correct percent?
▶  Check
Dana: within 1 SD is about 68%, so 66 to 78 holds about 68%; the 95% interval is 60 to 84. Lee: the data are strongly right-skewed (median 3 against mean 6, one value of 35), the interval includes impossible negative waits, and 14 of 15 values (about 93%) fall within 1 SD, far from 68%. Marco: about 97.5% (50% below the mean + 34% + 13.5%), not 95%. The 95% is the share between −2 and +2 SD; above +2 SD the leftover 2.5% is the upper tail. Exact is 97.72%.
4
Applied Problem: Counting a Crowd
13 min
1
A constructed city has 5,000 commuters. Commute times are approximately normal with mean 30 minutes and SD 6 minutes. About how many commuters take (a) between 24 and 36 minutes, (b) more than 42 minutes, (c) between 18 and 36 minutes?
2
The city says “nearly everyone” commutes between two times that are 3 SD from the mean. Write that interval and the number of commuters expected to fall outside it.
▶  Check
(a) ±1 SD: 68% of 5,000 = 3,400. (b) 42 is +2 SD: 2.5% of 5,000 = 125. (c) −2 SD to +1 SD: 13.5 + 34 + 34 = 81.5%, so 0.815 × 5,000 = 4,075. Interval for 3 SD: 12 to 48 minutes; outside it about 0.3% of 5,000 = 15 commuters. (For (a) through (c) the exact normal counts are about 3,413, 114, and 4,093; the rule gives the quick estimate.)
