Estimating Population Percentages from a Normal Fit
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
One curve, one area, and a whole population answered at once.
Estimate the percent below, above, or between values with a normal model; work backward from a percent to a cutoff; and say honestly that the result is an estimate.
Reading & work: Read the page in order, try the widget, and keep Excel open for the click-by-click section. Plan on about 75 minutes.

I will be able to estimate the percent of a population that falls below, above, or between values by finding the area under a normal curve.
2

3

✨ A BELL OVER A WHOLE POPULATION
Picture the heights of every student in a large school, written one by one on a very long roll of paper. Nobody could read that roll and see a pattern. Yet when the heights are gathered into a histogram, a hump rises in the middle and thins out on both sides, and a single smooth curve laid over the hump seems to tell the whole story. That is the wonder of the normal model: a curve described by just two numbers, a mean and a standard deviation, that stands in for thousands of individual measurements.
In Lessons 25.1 and 25.2 you learned to describe that curve: the model, the z-score z = (x − mean) ÷ SD, the standard normal curve, and the Empirical Rule (68–95–99.7). The Empirical Rule answers questions about exactly one, two, or three SDs from the mean. Today you answer the question for any value. How many students are shorter than 160 cm? How many boxes are underweight? What score puts you in the top 10%? Order shows up inside the randomness, and now you can put a number on it.
📐 THE BIG IDEA: AREA IS A PERCENT OF THE POPULATION
The total area under a normal curve is 1, which stands for the whole population (100%). The area over any interval is the proportion of the population in that interval. If the shaded region between two heights is 0.30 of the total area, the model says about 30% of the population has a height in that interval.
Three kinds of area cover almost every question:
Question askedShaded regionExcel (cumulative, TRUE)
Percent below aeverything left of a=NORM.DIST(a, mean, SD, TRUE)
Percent above aeverything right of a (1 minus the area below)=1-NORM.DIST(a, mean, SD, TRUE)
Percent between a and barea below b minus area below a=NORM.DIST(b, mean, SD, TRUE)-NORM.DIST(a, mean, SD, TRUE)
Why only intervals? A normal model assigns 0 to any exact value. A single value has no width, so it has no area: the model says the percent of heights that are exactly 160.000… cm is 0. We ask about intervals instead (shorter than 160, between 158 and 162). For the same reason “below 160” and “at most 160” have the same area.
An estimate, not a fact. We use the model’s area as a stand-in for the real population’s percent, so the answer is only as good as the fit. Every result today carries a hidden assumption: the population is roughly normal. Week 26 gives you tools to test that assumption and shows data where it fails. Until then, write “about” or “estimate” every time.
💡 THERE IS NO PRINTED Z-TABLE IN THIS COURSE
Older books print a table of areas for the standard normal curve and ask you to look them up. You will not need one here. Technology gives the area: Excel’s NORM.DIST and NORM.S.DIST functions return it directly, and the widget shows the same numbers. What matters is that you know which area to ask for and can say what it means. You still compute z-scores yourself, because the z-score is how you know where a value sits.
🔀 TWO ROUTES TO THE SAME AREA
By-hand z route (two steps). Convert the value to a z-score with a pencil or Desmos, then ask for the area under the standard normal curve (mean 0, SD 1):
z = (x − mean) ÷ SD   →   area below = NORM.S.DIST(z, TRUE)
One-step route. Give Excel the value, the mean, and the SD, and it does the z-score internally:
area below = NORM.DIST(x, mean, SD, TRUE)
The TRUE at the end means cumulative: “the area from far left up to x.” (FALSE would return the height of the curve at x, which is not a percent and is not what we want today.) The two routes must agree, and the examples below show it. Rounding in this lesson: areas to four decimals, percents to one decimal, cutoffs to two decimals.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE A — PERCENT BELOW (HEIGHTS)
Constructed scenario, not real data. Suppose the heights of the students in a large school are modeled by a normal curve with mean 170 cm and SD 8 cm. Estimate the percent of students shorter than 160 cm.
▶ Sketch and say it. 160 is left of the mean, so I expect a smallish area in the left tail, well under 50%.
▶ By-hand z route. z = (160 − 170) ÷ 8 = −10 ÷ 8 = −1.25. In Desmos type (160-170)/8 to check.
▶ Area under the standard curve. =NORM.S.DIST(-1.25,TRUE) gives 0.1056.
▶ One-step route. =NORM.DIST(160,170,8,TRUE) gives 0.1056. The routes agree.
▶ Say it in words. The model estimates that about 10.6% of the students are shorter than 160 cm.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE B — PERCENT ABOVE (TEST SCORES)
Constructed scenario. Scores on a made-up test are modeled by a normal curve with mean 75 and SD 10. Estimate the percent of students who score above 90.
▶ Plan. Excel gives the area below, so I will find that and subtract it from 1.
▶ z-score. z = (90 − 75) ÷ 10 = 1.5.
▶ Area below. =NORM.S.DIST(1.5,TRUE) = 0.9332, and the one-step =NORM.DIST(90,75,10,TRUE) = 0.9332 agrees.
▶ Area above. 1 − 0.9332 = 0.0668. In one cell: =1-NORM.DIST(90,75,10,TRUE).
▶ Say it. About 6.7% of students are estimated to score above 90.
▶ Sense check. A score of 90 is 1.5 SDs above the mean. The Empirical Rule says about 16% lie above 1 SD and about 2.5% above 2 SDs, so an answer between 2.5% and 16%, like 6.7%, is reasonable. To test the widget on a nearby case: shade below 65 with mean 75 and SD 10 and you will see 0.1587.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE C — PERCENT BETWEEN (A MANUFACTURING TOLERANCE)
Constructed scenario. A made-up cereal company fills boxes to a labeled 500 g. The fill weights are modeled by a normal curve with mean 500 g and SD 4 g. A box is within tolerance if it holds between 495 g and 505 g. Estimate the percent of boxes within tolerance.
▶ Plan. Between means (area below 505) minus (area below 495).
▶ z-scores. z for 505 = 5 ÷ 4 = 1.25; z for 495 = −5 ÷ 4 = −1.25.
▶ Areas below. NORM.S.DIST(1.25, TRUE) = 0.8944 and NORM.S.DIST(−1.25, TRUE) = 0.1056.
▶ Subtract. 0.8944 − 0.1056 ≈ 0.7887. (Subtracting the rounded values gives 0.7888 by hand; Excel keeps full precision and gives 0.7887, so use its value.)
▶ One step. =NORM.DIST(505,500,4,TRUE)-NORM.DIST(495,500,4,TRUE) gives 0.7887.
▶ Say it. About 78.9% of boxes are estimated to be within tolerance, so about 21.1% are not: roughly 10.6% below 495 g and 10.6% above 505 g.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE D — WORKING BACKWARD FROM A PERCENT
So far we went from a value to a percent. Often the question runs the other way: what value cuts off a given percent? The function is NORM.INV(probability, mean, SD). The probability is the percent below the cutoff, written as a decimal. NORM.INV undoes NORM.DIST.
(i) The top 10% of quiz scores (mean 75, SD 10, constructed).
▶ “Top 10%” means 10% above, so 90% is below the cutoff. Use probability 0.90.
▶ =NORM.INV(0.9,75,10) gives 87.82. A score of about 87.8 or higher puts a student in the top 10%.
▶ By-hand reading. The standard normal cutoff for 0.90 is z ≈ 1.2816, so x = 75 + 1.2816 × 10 = 87.82. Check in Desmos: 75+1.2816*10.
(ii) The bottom 15% of heights (mean 170, SD 8, constructed).
▶ “Bottom 15%” is already a percent below, so the probability is 0.15.
▶ =NORM.INV(0.15,170,8) gives 161.71 cm. Students under about 161.7 cm are the shortest 15%.
▶ Sense check. The cutoff is below the mean (161.7 < 170), as it must be for a bottom percent, and about 1.04 SDs below it (z ≈ −1.0364).
▶ Round trip. =NORM.DIST(161.71,170,8,TRUE) returns about 0.15. NORM.DIST and NORM.INV undo each other.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE E — FROM PERCENTS TO COUNTS
A percent becomes a count when you multiply by the size of the population, N. Suppose the cereal company fills N = 2000 boxes in a day (constructed), with fills modeled as in Example C.
▶ Within tolerance: 0.7887 × 2000 = 1577.4, so an estimated 1577 boxes.
▶ Outside tolerance: (1 − 0.7887) × 2000 = 422.6, so an estimated 423 boxes. (And 1577 + 423 = 2000.)
▶ Say “about 1577.” The model gives an expected count; one real day may differ by chance, and a real production line may not be exactly normal. In Excel: =2000*(NORM.DIST(505,500,4,TRUE)-NORM.DIST(495,500,4,TRUE)).

💻 EXCEL: NORM.DIST, NORM.S.DIST AND NORM.INV, CLICK BY CLICK
You will build a small practice workbook. It is not uploaded. (If you keep it, save it as Wk25_L3_YourName.xlsx.) Keep this page open beside Excel and type formulas exactly as shown, with commas and no spaces.
▶ Open Excel and start a Blank workbook. Double-click the tab at the bottom (Sheet1), type Areas, press Enter.
▶ In A1 type Areas under a normal curve (constructed scenarios).
▶ In row 3 type the headings, one per cell: A3 Scenario, B3 Mean, C3 SD, D3 a, E3 b, F3 z for a, G3 Below a, H3 Above a, I3 Between a and b, J3 Check: NORM.S.DIST.
▶ Row 4, heights: A4 Heights (cm), B4 170, C4 8, D4 165, E4 180.
▶ Row 5, quiz scores: A5 Quiz scores, B5 75, C5 10, D5 62, E5 88. Row 6, cereal fill: A6 Cereal fill (g), B6 500, C6 4, D6 492, E6 506.
▶ Click F4 and type =(D4-B4)/C4, Enter. You should see −0.625. (The z-score, with cell references instead of numbers.)
▶ Click G4 and type =NORM.DIST(D4,B4,C4,TRUE), Enter. You should see about 0.2660.
▶ Click H4 and type =1-G4, Enter (about 0.7340).
▶ Click I4 and type =NORM.DIST(E4,B4,C4,TRUE)-NORM.DIST(D4,B4,C4,TRUE), Enter (about 0.6284).
▶ Click J4 and type =NORM.S.DIST(F4,TRUE), Enter. It should equal G4: this is the by-hand z route, and it must agree with the one-step route.
▶ Drag to select F4:J4. Point at the small square in the lower-right corner of the selection (the fill handle); the pointer becomes a thin black plus sign. Drag down to row 6. Excel copies each formula and moves the row numbers.
▶ Format as percent: select G4:J6, go to the Home tab, and in the Number group click the % button (Percent Style). Then click Increase Decimal once so you see one decimal place, such as 26.6%. (To see four-decimal areas instead, set the format to Number with 4 decimal places.)
▶ Check. Row 5 (62 and 88): below 0.0968, above 0.9032, between 0.8064. Row 6 (492 and 506): below 0.0228, above 0.9772, between 0.9104. Columns G and J should match in every row.
▶ Second sheet for cutoffs. Click the + next to the sheet tabs and rename the new tab Cutoffs. Type headings in row 3: A3 Scenario, B3 Mean, C3 SD, D3 Percent below, E3 Cutoff, F3 Round-trip check.
▶ Row 4: Quiz scores, top 10% in A4, 75, 10, and 0.9 in D4. Row 5: Heights, bottom 15% in A5, 170, 8, and 0.15 in D5.
▶ Click E4, type =NORM.INV(D4,B4,C4), Enter (87.82). In F4 type =NORM.DIST(E4,B4,C4,TRUE), Enter: it should return D4 (0.9). Select E4:F4 and drag the fill handle down one row. Expect 161.71 and 0.15.
▶ Format the cutoffs: select E4:E5, open the Number group drop-down on the Home tab (it says General), and choose Number (2 decimal places).
Honest note. Menu names differ a little between desktop Excel and Excel for the web, but typing a formula in a cell works the same in both. If a cell shows #NAME?, check the spelling and the dots (NORM.DIST, not NORMDIST). Older workbooks may use NORMDIST and NORMINV; this course uses the names with the dot.
💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · NORMAL AREAS IN EXCEL, Z-SCORES IN DESMOS
Excel is the star. Percent below: =NORM.DIST(x, mean, standard_dev, TRUE). Percent above: =1-NORM.DIST(x, mean, standard_dev, TRUE). Percent between a and b: =NORM.DIST(b, mean, standard_dev, TRUE)-NORM.DIST(a, mean, standard_dev, TRUE). Area below a z-score on the standard curve: =NORM.S.DIST(z, TRUE). Cutoff for a percent below: =NORM.INV(probability, mean, standard_dev). Use cell references (such as D4) so one formula can be filled down, and always include the TRUE in NORM.DIST and NORM.S.DIST.

Desmos Scientific is for the z-score arithmetic only. Type (160-170)/8 and read −1.25; type 75+1.2816*10 to turn a z-score back into a value. The areas themselves come from Excel or the widget.
🏭 WHERE PEOPLE USE THIS
Manufacturers and testing agencies often use normal models to estimate how many items fall outside a tolerance (like boxes outside 495–505 g) or how many test takers would land above a score cutoff, and to decide where a cutoff should sit to capture a chosen percent. Real quality-control systems use further checks beyond a single normal-model estimate, such as inspecting actual samples. (This is a general description; every number in this lesson is constructed.)
🤖 AI TRAINING · PROMPT → PREDICT → VERIFY
Prompt. Ask an AI tool exactly this: “Student heights in a large school are normally distributed with mean 170 cm and standard deviation 8 cm. What percent of students are between 160 cm and 180 cm? Show your steps.”
Predict. Before you read its answer, decide on your own: the Empirical Rule says about 68% lie within 1 SD (162 to 178 cm) and about 95% within 2 SDs (154 to 186 cm). Is 160 to 180 more or less than 68%? Write a range you expect.
Verify. The value to verify is 0.7887, about 78.9% (z = ±1.25). Check it with the widget (Shade an area, heights, between 160 and 180) and with Excel (=NORM.DIST(180,170,8,TRUE)-NORM.DIST(160,170,8,TRUE)). Then audit the AI. Did it round badly (for example “about 80%” or “about 68%” with no work)? Did it mix up standard deviation and variance (treating 8 as the variance makes the SD about 2.83 and gives about 99.96%, which is wrong)? Did it say the answer is an estimate that depends on the heights being roughly normal, or did it state a flat fact? Flag each problem and say what you would ask next.
