Measures of Center: Mean and Median
Lesson

One number to stand for a whole list — and the courtesy of saying which one, and why.
Mean as fair share and balance point; median as the middle; resistance and skew; choosing a center

🎯 WHAT DOES “TYPICAL” MEAN?
Someone asks, “How long is your commute?” You do not recite every morning of the year. You give one number. A single number that stands in for a whole list is a measure of center, and it is the first thing a statistician asks of a dataset: where is the middle?
But “middle” has more than one meaning. This lesson teaches two of them — the mean and the median — and, more importantly, how to tell which one is the fair choice for the data in front of you. All datasets in this lesson are constructed (made up) to teach the ideas.
A note on courtesy, this module's virtue. A summary number is a kindness to a busy reader and a possible discourtesy to the data: it hides everything it does not say. Choosing a center well, and saying what it leaves out, is how you stay courteous to both.
⚖️ THE MEAN: FAIR SHARE AND BALANCE POINT
Four students stack cubes: 3, 5, 6, and 10 cubes. They decide to be fair, so they pool all the cubes and deal them out evenly.

Total cubes: 3 + 5 + 6 + 10 = 24. Shared among 4 students: 24 ÷ 4 = 6. That fair-share number is the mean.
mean = (sum of all the values) ÷ (how many values) = (3+5+6+10)/4 = 6
The balance picture. The mean is also the point where the data would balance, like children on a seesaw. Measure how far each value sits from 6: the distances are −3, −1, 0, and +4. The negatives add to −4 and the positives add to +4, so the pulls cancel exactly. The deviations from the mean always add to zero; that is what “balance point” means. A big value far from the mean is a long lever arm, and it pulls hard.
What the mean uses. The mean uses every value, and uses its actual size. That is its strength (nothing is ignored) and its weakness (a single huge value tugs it hard).
📏 THE MEDIAN: THE MIDDLE VALUE
The median is the middle value once the data are put in order: half the values are at or below it, half are at or above it. Here are seven commute times in minutes (constructed), exactly as they were recorded: 15, 8, 22, 12, 30, 10, 18.
Worked example, odd count (n = 7).
▶ Step 1: order the list. 8, 10, 12, 15, 18, 22, 30. Skipping this step is the most common mistake: the median is the middle of the ordered list, not of the list as written.
▶ Step 2: find the middle position. With 7 values, the middle is the 4th (three on each side).
▶ Step 3: read it. The 4th value is 15. So the median commute is 15 minutes.
Worked example, even count (n = 6). Suppose the 30-minute commute is removed. The ordered list is 8, 10, 12, 15, 18, 22. With 6 values there is no single middle: the 3rd and 4th values share it. Take their mean: (12+15)/2 = 13.5 minutes. (It is fine for a median to be a number that is not in the list.)
The mean of the same seven times: (8+10+12+15+18+22+30)/7 = 115 ÷ 7, which is about 16.43 minutes. Here is where they sit:

What the median uses. The median uses only the order of the values, not how far apart they are. It ignores how large the largest values are.
💪 WHAT EACH ONE IGNORES: RESISTANCE
Take the 30-minute commute and make it a miserable 90 minutes (a long detour that day). Compare:
Original (30)Changed to 90
Ordered list8, 10, 12, 15, 18, 22, 308, 10, 12, 15, 18, 22, 90
Median1515
Mean≈ 16.43 (115 ÷ 7)25 (175 ÷ 7)
The median did not move. The mean jumped from about 16.4 to 25, and now describes none of the seven days well: six of the seven commutes are at most 22 minutes. A statistic that barely changes when one extreme value changes is called resistant. The median is resistant; the mean is not.
Neither is “wrong.” The mean answers “if everything were shared equally, what would each get?” — and the 90-minute day really did cost time. The median answers “what does a middle day look like?” They answer different questions.
📐 SHAPE DECIDES THE ARGUMENT
When a distribution is symmetric (a balanced pile with the same shape on both sides of the middle), the mean and median land close together, and either is a fair summary. When a distribution is skewed — stretched out with a long tail on one side — the mean is dragged toward the tail, away from the bulk of the data, while the median stays with the bulk.
In the widget below you will see this with made-up data. For instance, in its right-skewed dataset (a few large values on a number line from 0 to 100) the mean is 24.75 and the median is 18.5: the long right tail has pulled the mean above the median. In its symmetric dataset the mean is 50.08 and the median is 50: nearly the same.

🧭 CHOOSING A CENTER, AND SAYING WHY
There is no single best measure. Ask three questions, in this order:
▶ What is the shape? Roughly symmetric with no extreme values: the mean is fine (and it uses all the data). Skewed or with a far outlier: the median is the safer description of a typical value.
▶ What is the question? If you care about totals or fair shares (the cost per person, the total over many days), the mean is the right tool, because the mean times the count gives back the total. If you want the experience of a typical individual, the median is usually better.
▶ Are the data categories? You cannot add or order “dog” and “cat.” For categories, report the mode: the value that occurs most often.
The mode, briefly. Twelve students name a favorite pet (constructed): dog 5, cat 4, fish 2, bird 1. The mode is dog, the most common answer. For numbers the mode is less useful (a dataset can have several, or none worth naming), so we will use mean and median for numerical data.
A model sentence. A good answer always names the shape and the reason: “I would report the median because the data are skewed to the right and the mean is pulled up by a few large values.” Notice it does not just say “median is better.”
💡 A COURTESY TO THE READER
Suppose one office has seven salaries close together and one boss's salary far above them. An announcement “the average salary is $81,000” may be perfectly true and still misleading, because almost nobody earns anything near it. The courteous report gives the median too, or says outright that one very high value pulls the mean up. Both numbers are true; the fairness lies in saying which one answers the reader's question.
💻 EXCEL: AVERAGE AND MEDIAN, CLICK BY CLICK
Excel does list statistics for you. You will check it against the hand answers from this lesson: mean about 16.43 and median 15 for the seven commute times. Keep this page open beside Excel.
▶ Open Excel and start a Blank workbook. (You do not have to upload this one; it is practice. If you want to keep it, save it as Wk22_L1_YourName.)
▶ Click cell A1 and type Commute minutes, then press Enter.
▶ Starting in A2, type the seven values in this order, pressing Enter after each: 15, 8, 22, 12, 30, 10, 18. They fill A2 through A8. (Do not sort them; you will see that Excel does not need you to.)
▶ Click cell C1 and type Mean, Enter. Click C2 and type Median, Enter. Click C3 and type Count, Enter. Click C4 and type Sum, Enter.
▶ Click cell D1 and type =AVERAGE(A2:A8) then press Enter. The equals sign tells Excel a formula follows; A2:A8 means “the cells from A2 down to A8.” You should see a long decimal starting 16.428…
▶ Click D2 and type =MEDIAN(A2:A8), Enter. You should see 15.
▶ Click D3 and type =COUNT(A2:A8), Enter (it should read 7). Click D4 and type =SUM(A2:A8), Enter (it should read 115).
▶ Check the mean by hand. In your notebook compute 115 ÷ 7. It must match D1. (You can also type =D4/D3 in any empty cell: it gives the same number, because the mean is sum divided by count.)
▶ Test resistance. Click cell A6 (the 30), type 90, Enter. Watch D1 and D2: the mean becomes 25; the median stays 15.
▶ Test the even case. Click A6 and press the Delete key so the cell is empty. Excel skips empty cells, so D3 now reads 6, D2 reads 13.5, and D1 is about 14.17 (85 ÷ 6).
Honest note. Menu names and the look of the window differ a little between desktop Excel and Excel for the web, but typing a formula in a cell works the same in both. If a formula shows a message beginning with #, or shows the formula text instead of a number, check that you typed the equals sign and the parentheses, and that the cell range is right.
💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · MEAN AND MEDIAN IN DESMOS AND EXCEL
Desmos Scientific does plain arithmetic, so you type the expression yourself. Mean of 3, 9, 4, 10, 6: type (3+9+4+10+6)/5 → 6.4. Mean of the seven commute times: (15+8+22+12+30+10+18)/7 → a decimal beginning 16.428. A median with an even count needs the average of the two middle values after you order the list yourself: (12+15)/2 → 13.5. Always type the outer parentheses around the sum; without them the calculator divides only the last number.

Excel does the list work: =AVERAGE(A2:A8) for the mean and =MEDIAN(A2:A8) for the median, with =COUNT(A2:A8) and =SUM(A2:A8) as checks. Use Desmos to confirm Excel's answer on a small list; use Excel when the list is long.
🤖 AI TRAINING · PROMPT → PREDICT → VERIFY
Prompt. Paste this constructed list into an AI tool: “Here are the annual salaries, in thousands of dollars, of the 8 employees of a made-up company: 42, 45, 47, 48, 50, 52, 55, 310. Which is the better single number to report as the typical salary: the mean or the median? Give both values.”
Predict. Before you read its answer, predict which measure it will recommend and why. Then compute both by hand (or in Excel) yourself. Hint: look at the largest value, and expect the mean to land far above most of the list.
Verify. Compare. Did the AI choose the median and say the large salary pulls the mean up? Check its numbers: the sum is 649, so the mean is 649 ÷ 8 = 81.125 and the median is (48 + 50) ÷ 2 = 49. AI tools sometimes make an arithmetic slip or call the wrong value the median. If its reasoning was right but a number was wrong, say which, and what that tells you about trusting an AI tool with arithmetic.
⚡ TODAY'S ACTIVITIES (about 45 minutes total)
1
Fair Share and Balance by Hand
10 min
1
Six students report how many books they read this term (constructed): 4, 6, 6, 8, 11, 13. Compute the mean (as a fair share) and the median. Show the sum and the count.
2
Write each value's distance from the mean and add the distances. What total do you get, and why?
▶  Check
Sum 48, count 6, mean 48 ÷ 6 = 8; ordered list 4, 6, 6, 8, 11, 13, median = (6 + 8) ÷ 2 = 7. Distances from 8: −4, −2, −2, 0, +3, +5, which add to 0, because the mean is the balance point.
2
Drag the Dots
10 min
1
In the widget (Center tab), choose Symmetric. Predict: if you drag the largest dot (68) to the far right, to 100, what happens to the mean and to the median? Do it and record the new mean and median.
2
Reset, choose Right-skewed, and drag the largest dot (62) to 100. Record the new mean and median, and say which statistic followed the dot.
3
In Right-skewed, now drag the smallest dot (10) all the way to 100 and record both statistics again. Explain why the median finally moved this time.
▶  Check
Symmetric: the mean rises from 50.08 to 52.75; the median stays 50. Right-skewed, largest dot to 100: the mean rises from 24.75 to about 27.92; the median stays 18.5. Dragging the smallest dot to 100 moves a value across the middle of the list, changing the order: the mean becomes about 32.25 and the median becomes 20.5. The median responds to order (which side of the middle a value is on), not to how far a value goes beyond the others.
3
Excel Check
15 min
1
A rider logs miles on nine weekends (constructed): 6, 8, 7, 9, 5, 8, 10, 7, 30. Following the click-by-click steps above, type the data in column A and use =AVERAGE and =MEDIAN to find the mean and median.
2
Check the mean by hand with Desmos: type (6+8+7+9+5+8+10+7+30)/9.
3
In one sentence, say which statistic better describes a typical weekend and why. Then change the 30 to 10 and say what happens to each.
▶  Check
Mean 10 (sum 90 ÷ 9); median 8 (ordered 5, 6, 7, 7, 8, 8, 9, 10, 30, the 5th value is 8). The median better describes a typical weekend: eight of the nine weekends are 10 miles or fewer, and the 30-mile ride pulls the mean up. Changing 30 to 10 gives sum 70; the mean falls to about 7.78 and the median stays 8: the mean was the one being pulled.
4
Find the Mistake
10 min
1
Sam finds the median of 5, 12, 3, 9, 20 like this: “There are five numbers, so the middle one is the 3rd one I see, which is 3.” Sam also says “The mean is 9.8, so half the numbers must be above 9.8.”
2
Find and correct both errors. Count how many of the five values are above 9.8.
▶  Check
Error 1: Sam did not order the list. Ordered: 3, 5, 9, 12, 20, so the median is the 3rd value, 9. Error 2: the mean is the balance point, not the halfway point. Only two values (12 and 20) are above 9.8; the median is what splits the list into equal halves.
