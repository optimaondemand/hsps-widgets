📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A good critique is a fair question asked in seven places: what the data show, how they were gathered, and what more evidence would settle the claim.
The seven-part critique template, a worked critique of a made-up study, how to write a critique that is fair, and the order rule for checking your thinking against an AI
Reading & work: Read every section in order, work each example on paper before you read its solution, follow the Excel steps, and complete the three activities at the bottom. The reading and Excel steps take about 40 minutes and the activities about 35 more, so plan on about 75 minutes in all.

✨ A WONDER TO START WITH
Two made-up students read the same news story, which says a new club “boosts scores by 11 points.” One says, “It is a study, so it is proven.” The other says, “Statistics can say anything, so it is all junk.” Each has stopped thinking at a feeling. Neither has asked the questions that would actually tell them how much of the claim to believe.
All semester you have collected those questions: Is the graph honest? Do the summaries fit the shape? Is the model sensible? Is it association or cause? How were people chosen? How big is the uncertainty, and does “significant” mean “large”? Today you put them together into one habit, a complete statistical critique, and you use it on a whole made-up study from start to finish.
Service, this module’s virtue, is the reason. Neighbors, councils, and families make decisions from numbers they cannot check for themselves. A person who can read a report fairly, neither swallowing it nor sneering at it, protects them. This is the capstone of the statistics course: nothing new is introduced, and everything you know is used.
A note on honesty. Every report, school, researcher, and number in this lesson is constructed (made up) for teaching. Nothing here is real data or a real news story.
📑 THE SEVEN-PART CRITIQUE TEMPLATE
A critique is easier to write, and fairer, when it follows a fixed order. Here is the order. Each part is a question you already know how to ask, and the right-hand column reminds you where it came from.
PartThe question to askWhere it comes from
1. Data and displaysIs every chart honest? Does the axis start at zero? Are the gaps equal, the full range shown, the sizes true, and the labels and source given?The display-honesty checklist from the data-displays module
2. SummariesDo the summaries fit the shape of the data (mean or median, SD or IQR)? Is there an outlier, and what would the numbers be without it?Descriptive statistics: shape, center, spread, outliers as questions
3. Models and fitIs the model used (a normal curve, a line) sensible for these data? Are any values impossible? Does the fit hold up away from the middle?The normal-model checks and the regression and residual work
4. Relationships and causationWhat kind of relationship is claimed? Could a third variable explain it? Which verb does the study design allow: “is associated with,” or “causes”?Association versus causation, confounders, extrapolation
5. Sampling and designWho was studied, how many, and how were they chosen? Survey, observational study, or experiment? Random sampling (who) and random assignment (which group) are different things. Which biases are possible?Sampling methods, bias types, study design
6. InferenceIs the uncertainty reported (margin of error, interval, sample size)? Is the p-value read correctly? Is “significant” paired with the size of the effect? How many tests were run?Margin of error, intervals, shuffling and significance, many tests
7. VerdictWhat does the evidence support, as written? What does it not support? What additional evidence would settle the question?Everything above, put into one honest sentence
Two habits go with the template. First, rank your points: a critique with seven equal bullets hides the one flaw that really matters. Say which flaw is the most serious and why. Second, say “nothing to critique here” when that is true. A report with no chart has nothing for Part 1, and saying so is honest; inventing a complaint is not.
🤝 HOW TO WRITE A CRITIQUE THAT IS FAIR
A critique is not a takedown. Its job is to tell a reader how much weight a claim can bear. Five rules keep it fair.
▶ Steelman first. Begin by stating the strongest honest version of the claim, the one the authors would be happy to see you describe. If you cannot state it fairly, you are not ready to criticize it.
▶ Say what the study does support. Almost every study supports something, even if it is smaller than the headline. Name it.
▶ Name each limit and say which way it might push. A limit that could only make the effect look bigger is more serious than one that could go either way. If you do not know the direction, say so.
▶ Criticize the report, not the people. “The comparison group was chosen from one lunch line on one day” is a critique. “The researchers are sloppy” is a sneer. Assume honest mistakes unless the evidence shows otherwise.
▶ Say what would settle it. A critique that ends at “this is flawed” leaves the reader stuck. End with the data, design, or test that would make the answer clearer.
Honest limits of a critique. Finding flaws does not show a claim is false. It shows only that the evidence, as written, does not support the claim as strongly as the report says. The club in the next section might really work; the report simply has not shown it.
📰 THE CASE: A MADE-UP STUDY REPORT

📰 MADE-UP REPORT · The Pinecrest Post (a made-up newspaper about a made-up school)
READING CLUB BOOSTS SCORES BY 11 POINTS, STUDY FINDS
Pinecrest Academy’s after-school Reading Club works, according to a study by researchers Dana Whitlow and Ravi Osei (both made up). Ten students who signed up for the club this fall averaged 81 on the school’s 100-point reading test in December. “That is an 11-point jump, and the chance this happened by luck is tiny: p = 0.002, so there is only a 0.2% chance the club does nothing,” Whitlow said. The researchers’ bar chart shows the club’s bar towering over the comparison bar; its vertical axis starts at 60.
“If every student joined, we would expect the whole school’s average to rise by 11 points,” Osei said. The report gives no margin of error or interval. A note in its appendix says that 8 of the 10 club members had been rated strong readers the previous year, compared with 3 of the 10 comparison students.

📝 WORKED CRITIQUE: THE READING CLUB REPORT IN SEVEN PARTS
Opening verdict (steelman first). The strongest honest version of the claim is: “Students who joined the Reading Club scored about 11 points higher on a reading test than a comparison group, and that gap is larger than chance in a simple shuffle would usually produce.” The numbers behind that sentence check out, and the report does give the sample sizes. What the report does not establish is that the club caused the gap, or that it would work for every student.
Part 1: Data and displays. The bar chart’s axis starts at 60, not 0. The club bar is then 81 − 60 = 21 units tall and the comparison bar is 70 − 60 = 10 units tall, so the club bar looks 2.1 times as tall. The real scores differ by a factor of 81 ÷ 70 = 1.16, about 16%. The truncated axis turns a modest gap into a dramatic picture. This is the first item on the honesty checklist (Zero start?). The chart also does not show the individual scores.
Part 2: Summaries.

Club1081.081.05.48

One comparison score is 45, far below the other nine (65 to 80). An outlier is a question, not a verdict: was that student absent, ill, or was the score mistyped? Its effect is large. It drags the comparison mean to 70.0 (the median is 71.5) and inflates that group’s SD to 9.96. Without it, the comparison mean is 72.8 and the gap shrinks from 11 to 8.2 points. A fair report would show the gap both ways. Notice that the report says nothing about this.
Part 3: Models and fit. No model is fitted, so there is little to critique here. One caution: with only 10 scores per group, nobody should lean on a normal curve to describe these groups. That is a reason for the report to stay modest, not a flaw by itself. (A fair critique says so instead of inventing a complaint.)
Part 4: Relationships and causation. The verbs are boosts and works, which are causal. But students chose to join the club, so this is an observational comparison. The appendix note shows a confounder: 8 of 10 club members (80%) had been rated strong readers the year before, against 3 of 10 (30%) in the comparison group. Students who were already strong readers may have joined the club and scored higher with or without it. The honest verb is “club members scored higher,” not “the club raised scores.” Notice the direction of the limit: it could only make the club look better than it is.
Part 5: Sampling and design. There is no random sampling and no random assignment. The club group is a set of volunteers (voluntary response, so these students may differ in motivation). The comparison group is whoever was in the lunch line on one Friday (a convenience sample, which also leaves out students who were absent or ate elsewhere). Each group has only 10 students, from one school. None of this says the groups are unfair on purpose; it says the comparison cannot be trusted to be like the club group in everything except club membership.
Part 6: Inference. (a) The p-value. If you shuffle the 20 scores into two groups of 10, then of the 184,756 possible splits, 382 give a gap at least as big as 11 points in either direction: 382 ÷ 184,756 = 0.0021, about 0.002. So the report’s number is right. But Whitlow’s reading is wrong: a p-value is not “the chance the club does nothing.” It says that if the club did nothing and the groups were formed by a fair random split, a gap this big would be rare. Here the groups were not formed randomly, so even the small p-value cannot repair the bias in Parts 4 and 5. (b) The interval. The report gives none. The difference is 11. Its standard error is √(5.48² ÷ 10 + 9.96² ÷ 10) = 3.59, so a rough 95% range is 11 ± 1.96 × 3.59, which is about 4.0 to 18.0 points. That is very wide: the true gap could be small or large. (c) Significant versus important. A gap of 8 to 11 points on a 100-point test may matter, but the report never says how much it would matter, or what the typical year-to-year change is.
Part 7: Verdict and what would settle it. Supported: in this December test, the 10 club members scored about 11 points higher than 10 comparison students, and a chance-only split would rarely give a gap that big. Not supported: that the club caused the gap, that the true gap is exactly 11 (a plausible range is about 4 to 18), and that “every student” joining would raise the whole school’s average by 11, because that extends the result to students unlike the volunteers (an extrapolation beyond the kind of student studied). What would settle it: randomly assign interested students to join the club now or to a wait list, give everyone the same test in the fall and December so that you can compare gains, repeat across several schools, and report the interval and the scores with and without any outlier.
🏆 WHICH POINT IS THE STRONGEST?
The worked critique found eight separate problems: a truncated axis, an outlier, a causal verb on observational data, a confounder, volunteer and convenience groups, a misread p-value, a missing interval, and a school-wide extrapolation. A reader needs them ranked. Here is one defensible ranking, with reasons.
▶ Most serious: volunteers compared with a convenience group, so the groups differ in more than the club (Parts 4 and 5). It undermines the main claim (cause), and the appendix shows the groups really did differ before the club.
▶ Serious: the school-wide prediction. It extends the result to students the study never examined.
▶ Moderate: the misread p-value and the missing interval. They make the evidence sound stronger than it is, but the number itself was computed correctly.
▶ Smaller but easy to fix: the truncated axis and the unexplained outlier. They exaggerate the gap but do not change the direction.
Another careful critic might rank two of these differently. That is fine, as long as the ranking comes with a reason. What matters is that a reader can tell what to worry about first.
📝 THE ORDER RULE FOR USING AN AI
An AI can critique a report in seconds, and it will sound confident whether it is right or not. If you read its critique first, you will tend to copy its points and miss what it missed. So the capstone follows an order rule:
▶ Step 1: Write your own critique first, using the seven parts, with nothing from an AI in front of you.
▶ Step 2: Then ask an AI to critique the same report.
▶ Step 3: List every disagreement. A disagreement is a point you made that it did not, a point it made that you did not, or a point you both made but with different conclusions.
▶ Step 4: Decide who was right on each one, with evidence: a number you recomputed, a rule from the course, a line in the report. “The AI sounded sure” is not evidence.
This keeps your reasoning yours. It also trains the most useful skill in working with AI: not asking “is it good?” but “where exactly is it right, where is it wrong, and how do I know?”

💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · THE FULL TOOLKIT, ALL IN ONE PLACE
Excel. Summaries: =AVERAGE(range), =MEDIAN(range), =STDEV.S(range), =COUNT(range). A difference of means: =mean_B-mean_A. Its standard error: =SQRT(sA^2/nA+sB^2/nB). A rough 95% range: =diff-1.96*SE and =diff+1.96*SE. A margin of error for one proportion: =1.96*SQRT(p*(1-p)/n). A count of extreme shuffled gaps: =COUNTIF(range,">="&obs)+COUNTIF(range,"<="&-obs). Press F9 to redraw random numbers.
Desmos Scientific handles plain arithmetic: the safe margin-of-error rule is 1 ÷ the square root of n (use the square-root button), for example 1 ÷ √60 = 0.129; the false-alarm chance for 5 independent tests is 1 − 0.95 raised to the 5th power = 0.226 (use the exponent key). Desmos has no simulation here; the simulating belongs to Excel and the widgets from earlier weeks.
🤖 AI TRAINING · WRITE YOUR CRITIQUE FIRST, THEN ASK AN AI, THEN LIST EVERY DISAGREEMENT
Prompt. Use the short made-up Cedar Lane poll from Activity 1 below. First, write your own critique with the planning grid (Activity 1 and 2). Then ask an AI tool: “Critique this report. Check the sampling, the claim, the margin of error, and what the data can and cannot support. Recompute any number you cite.”
Predict. Before you open its answer, write down the two points you are sure it will raise and one point you think it might miss (look at the numbers in the report).
Verify. Make a two-column list: agree and disagree. For each disagreement, decide who was right and write the evidence: a number you recomputed (for example, the real margin of error) or a rule from the course. Watch for these common AI slips: praising a number without recomputing it, saying a small margin of error makes the poll “reliable” even when the sample is self-selected, treating a p-value as the chance the claim is true, or being vague (“the sample may not be representative”) instead of saying how. Then find one flaw it raised that you missed and one flaw you raised that it missed. Verdict. The AI is a second reader, not the judge; the evidence is the judge.

Recompute two numbers: the poll’s percent, and the margin of error with the safe rule 1 ÷ √n (the course’s rule from earlier weeks).
▶  Check
1. Part 1 (data and displays): no chart; nothing to critique, except that the headline rounds 75% to “three in four.” Part 2 (summaries): 45 ÷ 60 = 0.75, so 75% is correct for the people who answered. And the margin covers sampling variation only, not the voluntary-response bias. Part 7: see Activity 2. 2.
