Lesson 24.2
Outliers and Their Effects on Center and Spread
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A summary is only as fair as you are about what one value can do to it.
Which statistics move, how far, why n matters, what changes when every value changes, and what to do with an outlier
Reading & work: Read every section in order, follow the Excel what-if lab, run the Slide the Maximum widget, and complete the four activities. Plan on about 65 minutes.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to use a table to say which of the mean, median, range, IQR, and SD change when the largest value moves, and by about how much.
2
I will be able to explain why the same change in one value moves the mean more in a small data set than in a large one (the mean moves by the change divided by n).
3
I will be able to state and apply how adding a constant or multiplying by a positive constant changes the center and the spread, including in a unit change.
4
I will be able to decide what to do with an outlier (check for error, check the population, keep and report with and without) and say why deleting to improve the story is wrong.

🔍 ONE VALUE, MANY SUMMARIES
Week 22 gave you a rule for flagging a value, and a reminder that a flag is a question, not a verdict. This lesson asks the next question: what does one unusual value actually do to the numbers you report? Some summaries barely notice it. Others swing wildly. Knowing which is which lets you choose a fair summary and tell a reader honestly how much one value mattered.
Every data set here is constructed: made up to teach the idea, never presented as a real measurement. Our test set is the time, in minutes, that nine made-up students took to finish a puzzle, in order: 14, 15, 15, 17, 18, 20, 22, 22, 26. The largest value is 26. By hand we use the median-of-each-half method for quartiles, so for this list \(Q_1 = 15\), the median is 18, \(Q_3 = 22\), and \(\text{IQR} = 7\). The mean is \(\dfrac{169}{9} \approx 18.78\), the range is \(26 - 14 = 12\), and the sample standard deviation is about 4.02. (Excel's QUARTILE.INC gives the same quartiles, 15 and 22, for this list.)
📊 THE EFFECT TABLE: THREE MOVES OF THE MAXIMUM
We change only the largest value and recompute everything. Quartiles are by hand (median of each half).

Read the table row by row.
▶ Max doubles. The mean rises from 18.78 to 21.67, up 2.89. The range explodes from 12 to 38 and the SD nearly triples, from 4.02 to 11.76. The median stays 18 and the IQR stays 7: the 52 is still the biggest value, so every position in the middle of the list is unchanged. The new fences are still \(4.5\) and \(32.5\), and 52 is far above 32.5, so it is flagged.
▶ Max removed. With only eight values the median becomes the average of the 4th and 5th, \(\dfrac{17 + 18}{2} = 17.5\), and the upper half becomes 18, 20, 22, 22, so \(Q_3 = 21\). The mean falls slightly to 17.88, but the range collapses to 8 and the SD falls to 3.18. Removing a value changes n, which can shift the middle.
▶ Max moved to the middle. Now 18 sits where the 26 was. The mean falls to 17.89 and the median stays 18, the range drops to 8, the SD to 2.98, and \(Q_3\) drops to 21 because the upper half is now 18, 20, 22, 22. The picture has no stretched tail anymore.
⚠️ WHERE EXCEL DISAGREES
For two of these versions the spreadsheet's QUARTILE.INC gives a different upper quartile than the by-hand method: for “max removed” it gives \(Q_3 = 20.5\) (by hand 21), and for “max moved to the middle” it gives \(Q_3 = 20\) (by hand 21). The median and the other summaries agree. Both conventions are legitimate; this lesson says which one it uses each time, and the table above is by hand.
Compare the two ends of the table. A summary that resists one far value (median, IQR) tells a different story from one that follows it (mean, range, SD). That is what the word resistant means: a resistant summary is not changed much by a few extreme values.
📐 HOW FAR THE MAX MOVES MATTERS
The three moves above were each a single jump. What happens when the maximum slides away in steps? Here the 26 is replaced by 26, then 36, then 46, then 66. Nothing else changes, so the median stays 18, \(Q_1\) stays 15, \(Q_3\) stays 22, and the IQR stays 7 throughout (all checked by hand).

Notice the pattern: the range moves by exactly as much as the maximum does, point for point (12, 22, 32, 52). The mean moves by one ninth of that distance (10 ÷ 9 ≈ 1.11, 20 ÷ 9 ≈ 2.22, 40 ÷ 9 ≈ 4.44). The SD grows too, faster than the mean at first because the farther the value sits from the center the more it counts. The median and the IQR do not move at all, because the max has not crossed into the middle of the list.
⚖️ THE LEVERAGE OF POSITION: WHY n MATTERS
One far value shifts the mean by a rule you can reason out without a formula sheet. The mean is the total divided by n. If one value grows by some amount, the total grows by that same amount, and dividing by n shares the increase among all n values. So:
the mean moves by (change in the one value) ÷ n
In the puzzle set, the 26 became 52, a change of 26, and \(26 \div 9 \approx 2.89\): that is exactly how far the mean moved (18.78 to 21.67). Now compare a small set with a big one, each given the same jump of 50 in its largest value. Both are constructed.
Constructed setnMean beforeMax changedMean afterMean moved byMedian before / after
34, 37, 40, 43, 4654046 → 965010 (= 50 ÷ 5)40 / 40
the whole numbers 15 through 64, one each5039.564 → 11440.51 (= 50 ÷ 50)39.5 / 39.5
The same jump of 50 moves the mean by 10 in the group of 5 and by 1 in the group of 50. One value out of five has a fifth of the vote; one out of fifty has a fiftieth. The sample SD tells the same story: it goes from 4.74 to 25.93 in the small set (more than five times larger) but only from 14.58 to 17.68 in the large set. This is why a single odd value in a small data set is a big deal and the same odd value in a large data set is a smaller one.
🚫 A WARNING ABOUT TINY DATA SETS
With very few values, even the quartiles can be touched by the maximum: in a list of five values, the by-hand upper quartile is the middle of the top two. In the group of 5 above, the by-hand \(Q_3\) would jump from 44.5 to 69.5 when the 46 became 96. That is another reason to be careful with outliers in small samples.
▶ Courtesy to the reader: when your data set is small, say so. A mean from five values is a fragile number, and the reader deserves to know it.
🔄 TRANSFORMATIONS: WHAT HAPPENS WHEN EVERY VALUE CHANGES
So far one value moved. Now every value changes in the same way. This happens constantly: converting inches to centimeters, adding bonus points to every score, changing minutes to seconds. Two kinds of change cover almost everything.
Change to every valueMean, median, \(Q_1\), \(Q_3\), min, maxRange, IQR, SD
Add a constant \(k\) (shift)each goes up by \(k\)unchanged
Multiply by a constant \(c > 0\) (rescale)each is multiplied by \(c\)each is multiplied by \(c\)
Why a shift leaves the spread alone: adding \(k\) slides the whole picture along the number line without stretching it, so every gap between values is the same. Range, IQR, and SD are all built from gaps. Why a rescale multiplies everything: multiplying by \(c\) stretches the line, so every position and every gap is \(c\) times as large. (If \(c\) were negative the picture would also flip, and the spreads would be multiplied by \(|c|\), the positive size of \(c\); in this lesson we keep \(c > 0\).)
Worked example: inches to centimeters. Nine made-up seedlings are measured in inches: 3, 4, 4, 5, 6, 6, 7, 8, 12. One inch is 2.54 centimeters, so every value is multiplied by 2.54. Here is every summary before and after. The inch column was computed from the data (quartiles by hand); each centimeter entry is the inch entry times 2.54, and we checked it against the converted list.

Now a shift. Add 10 minutes to every puzzle time (a made-up penalty for a late start). The list 14, 15, 15, 17, 18, 20, 22, 22, 26 becomes 24, 25, 25, 27, 28, 30, 32, 32, 36. The mean becomes 28.78 (up 10), the median 28 (up 10), \(Q_1 = 25\) and \(Q_3 = 32\) (each up 10), and the range (12), IQR (7), and SD (4.02) are exactly what they were. Instead multiply every time by 2: the mean becomes 37.56, median 36, \(Q_1 = 30\), \(Q_3 = 44\), and the range doubles to 24, the IQR to 14, and the SD to 8.05. You will check these in Excel below.
🧭 WHAT SHOULD YOU DO WITH AN OUTLIER?
Now you can see what a flagged value does to your numbers, so you can see what is at stake in deciding what to do with it. The honest sequence is short.
Question to askIf yesWhat you do and say
1. Is it a data entry or recording error? (a typo, a misplaced decimal, an impossible value, a unit mix-up)Check the source record if you can.Correct it if the true value is known. If it cannot be recovered, set it aside and say so, giving the reason.
2. Does it come from a different population or condition? (a different kind of unit, a different test, a measurement taken under another procedure)It may not belong in this group.Analyze the groups separately, or leave this value out with the reason stated, and report how you did so.
3. Is it a genuine, rare value from the same population?Keep it. It is real information.Report both: the summaries with and without it, and choose resistant summaries (median, IQR) as the main report.
Look at the puzzle set with the 52 minutes. Imagine the 52 is one student's time. With it: mean 21.67, median 18, IQR 7, SD 11.76. Without it (eight values): mean 17.88, median 17.5, IQR 6, SD 3.18. A reader who sees both knows the median and IQR hardly care, while the mean and SD care a lot. That is real information. If the 52 was a stopwatch that was left running (question 1), you would correct or set it aside and say so. If that student was given a different, harder puzzle (question 2), you would keep the groups apart. If the student simply struggled (question 3), the 52 stays, because that is what happened.
🚫 NEVER DELETE TO MAKE THE STORY PRETTIER
A rule that says “remove anything flagged” is not honesty; it is tidying. Deleting a value because it spoils the average, the graph, or the result you hoped for is a discourtesy to the data and to everyone who will read your report. The test is simple: would you make the same decision if the value pulled the result the other way? If not, do not delete it. Whatever you decide, write down what you did and why so a reader can disagree.
Look at the shape of the choice: the table does not tell you to throw values away. Two of its three rows say to look into the value first; the third says to keep it. Even when you remove something, the reason has to be named and reported.

💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · COMPUTING THE EFFECTS
Excel does the list work: =AVERAGE(B2:B10), =MEDIAN(B2:B10), =QUARTILE.INC(B2:B10,1), =STDEV.S(B2:B10), with =B2+10 and =B2*2 to transform a column. Change a data cell and every formula that refers to it recalculates.

Desmos Scientific does plain arithmetic only. To see how far one change moves the mean, type 26/9 → 2.8888…, and add it: 169/9+26/9 → 21.666… To convert a mean: (55/9)*2.54 → 15.5222… Desmos Scientific does not take a list of data, so add the values yourself first.

🤖 AI TRAINING · PROMPT → PREDICT → VERIFY
Prompt. Paste these nine made-up puzzle times (minutes) into an AI tool: 14, 15, 15, 17, 18, 20, 22, 22, 26. Ask: “If the largest value, 26, doubles to 52, what happens to the mean, median, standard deviation, IQR, and range? Give before-and-after numbers, and say which quartile method and which standard deviation you used.”
Predict. Predict before you read the answer. The median and the IQR should not change, because the 26 is already the biggest value and only the top position is touched. The mean should go up by 26 ÷ 9, about 2.89. The range should grow by 26, from 12 to 38. The SD should grow a lot. If the AI says the median or the IQR changes, that is a red flag.
Verify. Check by hand. Before: mean \(\tfrac{169}{9} \approx 18.78\), median 18, \(Q_1 = 15\), \(Q_3 = 22\), IQR 7, range 12, sample SD about 4.02. After: mean \(\tfrac{195}{9} \approx 21.67\), median 18, \(Q_1 = 15\), \(Q_3 = 22\), IQR 7, range \(52 - 14 = 38\), sample SD about 11.76 (population SD about 11.09). Both quartile conventions (median of each half and QUARTILE.INC) agree for this list, before and after. Did the AI state which SD it used? Did it get all five numbers right? A confident wrong number is a number to check, not a fact.

2
Predict by n
10 min
1
A made-up group of 8 values has mean 50. One value is raised by 40 and nothing else changes. By how much does the mean move, and what is the new mean?
2
A made-up group of 40 values also has mean 50. The same jump of 40 is applied to one value. Again, by how much does the mean move? Say in one sentence why the answers differ.
▶  Check
Group of 8: \(40 \div 8 = 5\), so the mean becomes 55. Group of 40: \(40 \div 40 = 1\), so the mean becomes 51. The same change in one value is shared among 8 values in the first group and 40 in the second, so each value carries less of it in the bigger group.

10 min
1

2
Now every time is converted to seconds (multiply by 60). Without recomputing from the new list, write the mean, median, range, and IQR in seconds. Then check by converting the five values and recomputing the mean and the median.
▶  Check

4
What Would You Do?
13 min
1
A made-up class recorded the minutes each student took to eat lunch. Three flagged values turn up. For each, use the decision questions from the lesson, say what you would do first, and say what you would report. (a) A value of 480 minutes in a list where everyone else is between 10 and 40; the cafeteria is open for 60 minutes. (b) One value of 3 minutes belongs to a student who skipped the hot line and only took a drink, while all others bought the same meal. (c) One value of 55 minutes belongs to a student who was in line for a long, recorded delay, as noted by the teacher on duty.
2
Then write one sentence on why you would not simply delete all three to make the average look steadier.
▶  Check
(a) Almost certainly a recording error (480 minutes is impossible for a 60-minute lunch), so question 1: check the source and correct it; if it cannot be recovered, set it aside and say why. (b) A different condition (question 2): the student did not eat the same meal, so analyze separately or leave out with the reason stated. (c) A genuine rare value (question 3): keep it, and report the summaries with and without it, favoring median and IQR. Deleting all three without reasons would make the story prettier, not truer, and would hide what happened. The reasons must be named, and they must not depend on whether the value pulls the average up or down.
