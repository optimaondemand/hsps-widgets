✨ RESPONSIBILITY: A SAMPLE SPEAKS FOR PEOPLE WHO WERE NEVER ASKED
When you survey 100 people and report what “the town” thinks, you are speaking for thousands of people who never answered. That is a serious thing to do, and it carries a duty: to choose your sample so that the people you did not ask have a fair chance of being represented, or else to say plainly that they were not.
Each sampling method is a different answer to the question “who gets picked, and who decides?” Sometimes chance decides. Sometimes a person decides, and people are not neutral: we pick those who are near, familiar, friendly, or agree with us, often without noticing. Learning the seven methods is learning to see who decided, so that you can claim only what your method allows.
📊 TWO FAMILIES: CHANCE DECIDES OR A PERSON DECIDES
Start with one split that organizes everything else. A probability sample (also called a random sample) is one where chance does the choosing and every member of the population has a known, nonzero chance of being picked. A non-probability sample is one where a person’s choice, or the sample members’ own choice, decides who is in.
Four of the seven methods are probability methods: simple random, stratified, cluster, and systematic (with a random start). Three are non-probability methods: judgment, quota, and convenience.
Why the split matters. Because chance did the choosing, a probability sample lets us use the mathematics of chance. We can say how far an estimate will typically stray from the truth, and later in this module you will learn to put a number on it. A non-probability sample has no such tool. Worse, the errors tend to push in one direction, and taking a bigger sample does not fix that: a large convenience sample is just a large sample of the easy-to-reach. (You will see this with your own eyes in the simulator below.) One caution in the other direction: random choice protects you from the chooser’s bias, but it does not rescue a survey whose chosen people refuse to answer, and a single random sample can still be unlucky by chance.

Voluntary response adds a second problem: people with strong feelings are the most likely to respond.
A note on judgment versus convenience. Both are non-random, and the line between them can blur. The difference is intent: in a judgment sample a person deliberately aims at members they think are representative; in a convenience sample nobody aims at all, they simply take what is nearest.
🔍 LOOK-ALIKES: FOUR PAIRS THAT GET MIXED UP
Most classification mistakes come from four pairs. Keep the one-line test for each.
▶ Stratified versus cluster. Stratified takes a few from every group. Cluster takes every member of a few groups. Test: “After sampling, did every group have someone in the sample?” If yes, stratified. If whole groups were left out entirely, cluster. (Also: strata are chosen to be different from each other and alike inside; clusters are meant to be mini-versions of the whole.)
▶ Quota versus stratified. Both fill a set number from each group. Test: “How were the people inside each group picked?” By a random draw from a list, stratified. By whoever was convenient or chosen by the interviewer, quota.
▶ Systematic versus simple random. Both start from a list. Simple random draws n random numbers. Systematic draws one random start and then follows a fixed step. If the list has a hidden rhythm, the fixed step can march in time with it (see Worked Example 2).
▶ Voluntary response versus a random sample. An open poll in which anyone may respond is a convenience sample in which the respondents choose themselves. A large number of responses does not make it random.
🍳 WORKED EXAMPLE 1 — THE PLAN CLASSIFIER (SIX PLANS)
Constructed scenarios, not real. Use these three questions in order on any described plan.
▶ Question 1: Who or what decides who is in? A random device or random numbers (probability family), or a person or the volunteers themselves (non-probability family)?
▶ Question 2 (random plans): how are the groups used? No groups, just a list: simple random. A random draw from every group: stratified. Whole randomly chosen groups: cluster. A random start and a fixed step: systematic.
▶ Question 3 (non-random plans): what steered the choice? A picker’s belief about who is typical: judgment. Set counts per group filled by whoever is handy: quota. Whoever is nearest or whoever volunteers: convenience.
Now classify six plans, and for each, name where bias could enter.
▶ Plan 1. Harbor Middle (made up) has 600 students. The office numbers them 1 to 600 and a spreadsheet draws 40 numbers at random. Q1: a random device. Q2: one list, no groups. Simple random. Bias: the selection itself is fair. The remaining risks are students who do not answer, and plain bad luck from a small sample.
▶ Plan 2. A made-up orchard has 40 rows of 25 trees. The inspector draws 4 row numbers at random and counts the apples on every tree in those 4 rows. Q1: random. Q2: whole groups. Cluster (the rows are the clusters). Bias: only 4 rows were visited; if those happen to be on the sunny slope, the rest of the orchard is missed. A stratified plan would take a few trees from every row instead.
▶ Plan 3. A made-up town has four neighborhoods with a resident list for each. The survey team draws residents at random from each list, a number proportional to the neighborhood’s population. Q1: random. Q2: a random draw from every group. Stratified. Bias: small, but the plan assumes the lists are complete and that the neighborhood is a useful way to split the town.
▶ Plan 4. At a made-up mall, an interviewer must collect 30 teens and 30 adults and fills each count with whoever walks past and agrees to talk. Q1: a person (and the shoppers who agree). Q3: set counts per group, filled by who is handy. Quota. Bias: the counts look balanced, but the people at that mall at that hour are not the town. It resembles stratified, but nobody drew a random sample from each group.
▶ Plan 5. The principal of a made-up school chooses 12 students she believes are “typical of the school” to answer a survey. Q1: a person. Q3: picked because the picker thinks they are typical. Judgment. Bias: she is most likely to think of students she knows, such as the helpful, the talkative, or the ones she sees in the hall. A different principal would choose a different 12.
▶ Plan 6. A made-up town page posts a link: “Tell us what you think of the new bus route!” and 300 people respond. Q1: the respondents themselves. Q3: whoever chose to answer. Convenience, the voluntary-response variant. Bias: people who feel strongly (very happy or very annoyed) are the most likely to respond, and anyone who never sees the page cannot respond at all. Three hundred answers does not make it random.
🏭 WORKED EXAMPLE 2 — SYSTEMATIC SAMPLING AND ITS PERIODICITY DANGER
Constructed data, not real. A made-up factory stamps 500 parts, numbered 1 to 500 in the order they come off a press. The press rotates through 10 dies, so part number 1, 11, 21, … comes from die 1, part 2, 12, 22, … from die 2, and so on. Suppose die 7 is worn and every part it makes (parts 7, 17, 27, …, 497) is defective. That is 50 defective parts out of 500, so the true defect rate is 50 / 500 = 10%. The inspector does not know this and wants to estimate it from a sample of 50 parts.
Plan A (k = 10). k = 500 ÷ 50 = 10. Pick a random start from 1 to 10 and take every 10th part. Every pick has the same remainder when divided by 10, so every pick comes from the same die.
▶ If the random start is 3: parts 3, 13, 23, …, 493. That is 50 parts, all from die 3, with 0 defective: an estimate of 0%.
▶ If the random start is 7: parts 7, 17, 27, …, 497. That is 50 parts, all from die 7, with 50 defective: an estimate of 100%.
▶ Any other start also gives 0%. Not one of the ten possible samples gives the true 10%. The method is random (the start is chosen by chance), yet it is badly biased for this list, because the step k lines up with the 10-die cycle.
Plan B (k = 7). A step that does not match the cycle behaves much better. With a start of 3 and a step of 7, the parts are 3, 10, 17, 24, …, and that visits all ten dies in turn. That sample has 72 parts, of which 7 are defective, an estimate of 7 / 72 ≈ 9.7%, close to the true 10%.
The lesson. Before using a systematic sample, ask: does this list repeat on a cycle? Production runs, calendars (every 7th day is always the same weekday), seating charts, and household lists with a fixed pattern can all repeat. If you suspect a cycle, shuffle the list first, change the step so it does not match, or use simple random sampling instead.
🌳 WORKED EXAMPLE 3 — PROPORTIONAL ALLOCATION IN A STRATIFIED SAMPLE
Constructed data, not real. To draw a stratified sample in proportion to group size, give each group the same share of the sample that it has of the population:
ngroup = n × (group size ÷ N)
A made-up school has N = 1,200 students: 360 freshmen, 324 sophomores, 300 juniors, and 216 seniors. The council wants a stratified sample of n = 100.
▶ Freshmen: 100 × 360 / 1,200 = 30.
▶ Sophomores: 100 × 324 / 1,200 = 27.
▶ Juniors: 100 × 300 / 1,200 = 25.
▶ Seniors: 100 × 216 / 1,200 = 18.
▶ Check: 30 + 27 + 25 + 18 = 100. These numbers were chosen so every share comes out whole. Within each grade, the students are then chosen by a random draw from that grade’s list.
The rounding problem. Real numbers rarely come out whole. A made-up orchard has N = 1,000 trees in four blocks of 280, 270, 240, and 210 trees, and the grower wants n = 30.
BlockTrees30 × trees ÷ 1,000Rounded to a whole number
A2808.48
B2708.18
C2407.27
D2106.36
Total1,00030.029
Rounding each block gives 8 + 8 + 7 + 6 = 29, one short of 30. A tidy fix is the largest remainder rule: keep the whole-number parts (8, 8, 7, 6), then give each missing tree to the block whose decimal part was largest. Here one is missing, and Block A has the largest leftover (0.4), so the sample is 9, 8, 7, 6, which adds to 30. Any reasonable rule is acceptable as long as you state it and the numbers add to n.
⚠️ RESPONSIBILITY CHECKPOINT: WHAT EACH FAMILY LETS YOU CLAIM
A probability sample lets you say: “Because chance chose these people, my estimate should land near the truth, and I can later state how far off it is likely to be.” That is permission to generalize to the population you sampled from, as long as that population is the one you care about.
A non-probability sample lets you say only: “Here is what these people said.” It might happen to be close to the truth, but you cannot show that it is, and you cannot attach an honest margin of error to it. The responsible sentence is the narrow one: “Of the 300 people who answered our online poll, 80% disliked the new route; we cannot say how the whole town feels.” (The 80% is a made-up illustration.)

🏭 WHERE PEOPLE USE THIS · MATCHING THE METHOD TO THE JOB
Different jobs favor different methods. Quality-control teams commonly check production lines with systematic or random sampling, and have to watch for cycles in the process. Researchers often use cluster sampling when they have no list of individual people but can list schools, clinics, or city blocks. Large surveys frequently combine methods, for example grouping a population into strata and then sampling clusters within each. Details differ from one organization to the next, so look up the design before relying on any survey’s numbers.
💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · EXCEL: DRAW A SYSTEMATIC SAMPLE AND A PROPORTIONAL ALLOCATION
Systematic sample. Put your roster in column A. Number the rows with =ROW()-1 (if row 1 holds a heading, the first member is in row 2 and gets ID 1). Compute k = N ÷ n, then pick the start with =RANDBETWEEN(1,k), where k is the number or the cell holding it. A member is selected when its ID is the start plus a whole number of steps: =IF(MOD(ID-start,k)=0,"yes",""). In other words, the picks are start, start + k, start + 2k, and so on. Because RANDBETWEEN redraws every time the sheet recalculates, copy the start cell and use Paste Special → Values to freeze it before you record your sample.

Stratified, in proportion. Count each group with =COUNTIF(range,"group name"), add the counts for N, then compute each group’s allocation with =ROUND(n*group_count/N,0). Add up the rounded allocations. If they do not add to n (Worked Example 3 showed 29 instead of 30), adjust the group with the largest leftover by hand. Full click-by-click steps are in Activity 3.

Desmos Scientific is for plain arithmetic only. Type the allocation as a fraction expression: 100*360/1200 gives 30, and 30*280/1000 gives 8.4. For the systematic step, 500/50 gives 10.

Goal A. A made-up company of 2,400 employees wants the average length of the commute. It has a complete employee list, and the three departments (office staff, drivers, and warehouse) are known to have very different commutes.
3
Goal B. A made-up school district wants the share of its students who walk to school. It has 30 schools and no student-level list. Traveling to every school is costly.

▶  Check
Goal A: stratified, by department, in proportion to department size, with a random draw inside each. The groups are known to differ, so guaranteeing each its share steadies the estimate. Watch for: out-of-date lists. Goal B: cluster, treating each school as a cluster: draw some schools at random and survey everyone (or a random set of students) in the chosen schools. Goal C: systematic, taking every kth loaf from a random start. No numbering is needed because the loaves arrive in order. Watch for: a cycle (for example, an oven that holds 12 loaves would line up with k = 12). Rule: if you can list everyone and the groups differ from each other, sample from every group (stratified); if listing everyone is hard but the groups are easy to list and visit, sample whole groups (cluster).
