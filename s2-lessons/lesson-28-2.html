Correlation: Strength, Direction, and r
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Read r like a careful scientist: its sign gives the direction, its size gives the strength of a straight-line pattern, and its square tells how much of the variation the line accounts for.
Sign and size of r; computing r by hand and with CORREL; how r ties to the slope; r-squared as the share of variation accounted for by the line; what r cannot tell you
Reading & work: Read every section in order, work each example before you open its check, play the correlation game in all three modes, follow the Excel steps, and complete the four activities at the bottom. Plan on about 75 minutes.

⚖️ RESPONSIBILITY: A NUMBER IS A CLAIM
Last time you described scatter plots in words and fitted a line. Today we look hard at the number that goes with that picture: the correlation coefficient, r. A number feels official. “The correlation is 0.83” sounds like a verdict. But r is a summary of one narrow thing, and the person who reports it is answerable for what it does and does not say.
So this lesson has two jobs that go together. First, learn to read r exactly: its sign, its size, its square, and how it connects to the slope you already know. Second, learn its limits: it sees only straight-line patterns, one stray point can change it a great deal, and it can never, by itself, show that one thing makes the other happen. Every dataset below is constructed (made up) to teach the ideas.
🔢 WHAT r TELLS YOU: DIRECTION AND SIZE
You met r informally in Lesson 27.1. Now we make it precise. For two numerical variables measured on the same cases:
▶ r is always a number from −1 to +1. It can never be 1.4 or −2.
▶ The sign is the direction. Positive r: larger x tends to go with larger y. Negative r: larger x tends to go with smaller y.
▶ The size (the distance from 0, ignoring the sign) is the strength of the straight-line pattern. r = +1 or −1 means every point sits exactly on one straight line. r near 0 means no straight-line tilt.
▶ r = −0.9 is just as strong as r = +0.9. They differ only in direction. So −0.9 is stronger than +0.5, even though it is a smaller number.
Here is the same rough guide, not a rule, that you used in Lesson 27.1. Different fields draw the lines in different places, and the same r looks different with 8 points than with 800.
Size of r (ignore the sign)A rough description
about 0.8 to 1strong straight-line pattern
about 0.5 to 0.8moderate
about 0.2 to 0.5weak
below about 0.2little or no straight-line pattern
A common mistake: r = 0.8 does not mean “80% of the points are on the line” or “80% correlated.” It is a measure on a −1 to +1 scale, not a percentage. (The percentage idea belongs to r-squared, later in this lesson.)
🧮 WHERE r COMES FROM: Z-SCORES MULTIPLIED
Recall from Week 25 that a z-score says how many standard deviations a value sits from its mean: z = (value − mean) / (standard deviation). For each case, find the z-score of its x and the z-score of its y, and multiply them. Then add up all those products and divide by n − 1:
r = [ Σ ( zx · zy ) ] / ( n − 1 )
Why does this work? Think about one point. If it is above average in x and above average in y, both z-scores are positive and the product is positive. If it is below average in both, both are negative and the product is again positive. If it is above in one and below in the other, the product is negative. A rising pattern puts most points in the first two situations, so most products are positive and r comes out positive. A falling pattern gives mostly negative products and a negative r. A shapeless cloud gives a mix that cancels toward 0.
The same number can be found from three “sums of squares” computed from the deviations from the means:
r = Sxy / √( Sxx · Syy )
where Sxx = Σ(x − x̄)², Syy = Σ(y − ȳ)², and Sxy = Σ(x − x̄)(y − ȳ). This form is easier to do by hand because it needs no standard deviations. In practice Excel does all of this for you, but doing it once by hand shows you what the button is doing.

Step 2: compute r. r = 270 / √(1200 × 88) = 270 / √105600 = 270 / 324.96 = +0.8309, about +0.83.

Step 4: say it in context. r = +0.83 is positive, so among these eight made-up players, more practice minutes tended to go with more free throws made. By the rough guide it is a strong straight-line pattern. It describes these eight players; it does not say practice caused the extra shots.
🔗 HOW r TIES TO THE SLOPE
You already know the least-squares line ŷ = a + bx from Week 27. The slope and r are tied together by one rule:
b = r · ( sy / sx )
The slope is measured in units of y per unit of x, while r has no units at all. The ratio sy / sx is what converts the unit-free r into a slope with units. It also shows that r and b always have the same sign, and that b = 0 exactly when r = 0.
Check on Example 1. b = 0.8309 × (3.5456 / 13.0931) = 0.8309 × 0.27080 = 0.2250. The direct computation agrees: b = Sxy / Sxx = 270 / 1200 = 0.225. So each extra minute of practice goes with about 0.225 more free throws made, or about 2.25 more for every extra 10 minutes. (Check the arithmetic: 3.5456 / 13.0931 = 0.27080 and 0.8309 × 0.27080 = 0.22500.)
The line always passes through the point (x̄, ȳ) = (30, 12), so the intercept is a = 12 − 0.225 × 30 = 5.25. The fitted line is ŷ = 5.25 + 0.225x, and Excel’s =SLOPE and =INTERCEPT will return exactly these values, as will the trendline equation on a chart.
🎯 r-SQUARED: THE SHARE OF VARIATION THE LINE ACCOUNTS FOR
Square the correlation and you get r-squared (r², which Excel calls RSQ and the trendline label shows as R²). It is always between 0 and 1, so it is often written as a percent.
Meaning. The y values vary: the made-up players did not all make 12 shots. r² is the share of that variation in y that the straight line accounts for. In Example 1, r² = 0.8309² = 0.6903, so about 69% of the variation in free throws made is accounted for by the straight-line relationship with practice minutes. The other 31% or so is variation the line leaves unexplained: differences in skill, rest, luck, and anything else.
A numerical check that this is a real share. The total variation in y is Syy = 88 (sum of squared deviations from 12). The line’s leftover misses, the SSE from Week 27, are 88 − b·Sxy = 88 − 0.225 × 270 = 88 − 60.75 = 27.25. So the line accounts for 60.75 out of 88, and 60.75 / 88 = 0.6903 = r². The leftover share is 27.25 / 88 = 0.3097.
Careful wording. Say “about 69% of the variation in free throws made is accounted for by (or associated with) the line using practice minutes.” Do not say “practice causes 69% of the shots” or “69% of the players improved because of practice.” r² describes how well a line summarizes the data, not why the data look that way. Also note that r² loses the sign: for direction, go back to r.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 2: r IS SYMMETRIC, THE LINE IS NOT

The means are P̄ = 3 and Q̄ = 5.4. The deviations of P are −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, so SPP = 10. The deviations of Q are −3.4, −2.4, 1.6, 0.6, 3.6, so SQQ = 11.56 + 5.76 + 2.56 + 0.36 + 12.96 = 33.2. The products are 6.8, 2.4, 0, 0.6, 7.2, so SPQ = 17.
P as x, Q as y: r = 17 / √(10 × 33.2) = 17 / √332 = 17 / 18.221 = 0.9330. Slope of the line predicting Q from P: b = 17 / 10 = 1.7, and a = 5.4 − 1.7 × 3 = 0.3.
Q as x, P as y: the same three sums appear, with the roles of the two squared sums switched. r = 17 / √(33.2 × 10) = 0.9330, the same number. But the slope of the line predicting P from Q is b = 17 / 33.2 = 0.512, and its intercept is 3 − 0.512 × 5.4 = 0.235.
What to notice. r does not care which variable is called x: it is symmetric. The line does care: 1.7 and 0.512 are different slopes, because predicting Q from P and predicting P from Q are different jobs. (The two slopes multiply to 1.7 × 0.512 = 0.870, which is r² again.) So r alone can never tell you which variable is the explanatory one, and it says nothing about which causes which.
🚫 WHAT r CAN AND CANNOT DO
Four facts to keep. Each is one you can test with the game below.
▶ Straight lines only. A strong curved pattern can have r near 0, because there is no straight-line tilt. Always look at the scatter plot, not just the number.
▶ No units. Because the z-scores in the formula have no units, changing units does not change r. Re-measure the practice time of Example 1 in hours (divide each x by 60) and r is still 0.8309. The slope does change: it becomes 0.225 × 60 = 13.5 free throws per hour, the same relationship in new units.
▶ One point can move it a lot. In Example 1, change the last player’s 15 to a 5 (a bad day). Then r falls from 0.83 to about 0.19, and the pattern now looks weak. You will see this in Excel below. Always find out whether an unusual point is a mistake or a real case before you report r.
▶ Symmetric, but the line is not. Swapping x and y leaves r alone and changes the slope (Example 2).
⚠️ A HIGH r IS NOT A CAUSE
A high r does not show that x causes y. Example 1’s r = +0.83 tells us practice minutes and free throws made rise together in these eight made-up players. It does not tell us whether practice made the difference, whether more skilled players simply practice more, or whether something else drives both. When and how a strong correlation can (and cannot) support a claim about cause is the whole of Lesson 28.3. Until then, the discipline is simple: report what the number shows, and stop there.

💡 WHAT THE FOUR SETS SHOW (READ AFTER YOU HAVE WRITTEN YOUR OWN NOTES)
Each set in When r misleads teaches one limit of r:
▶ A. A curve. r is about −0.003, yet the points follow a clear curve. r sees only straight-line tilt.
▶ B. An outlier. r = +0.377 with the far-off point and +0.975 without it. One case moved r from strong to weak.
▶ C. Two clusters. Combined, r = +0.956. Taken one cluster at a time, r is −0.088 in one and +0.036 in the other. The strong combined r comes from the two groups sitting in different places, not from a pattern inside either group. Whenever your data are really two groups mixed together, ask whether r is describing the groups or the pattern inside them.
▶ D. A straight line. r = +0.972. Here r does its job well: a strong straight-line pattern. But even this does not show that x causes y.

▶ Now the chart. Select A1:B9, click Insert, click Scatter (X, Y), and choose the dots-only option. Click the chart, click the + (Chart Elements) button, and tick Axis Titles, Chart Title, and Trendline. Type titles with units, and call the chart Practice and free throws (made-up data).

▶ Test an outlier. Change B9 from 15 to 5. Watch E2 fall to about 0.188, the chart’s trendline flatten, and R² shrink. Then type 15 back into B9.
If a value looks wrong, the usual causes are a typo in the data, the two ranges in the wrong order inside SLOPE or INTERCEPT, or a range that does not match the data. 💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · EXCEL DOES r; DESMOS DOES THE ARITHMETIC
Excel. With the x values in A2:A9 and the y values in B2:B9: =CORREL(A2:A9,B2:B9) gives r; =SLOPE(B2:B9,A2:A9) gives the slope b; =INTERCEPT(B2:B9,A2:A9) gives the intercept a; =RSQ(B2:B9,A2:A9) gives r-squared. Argument order matters: for SLOPE and INTERCEPT the y range goes first. CORREL and RSQ are symmetric (swap the two ranges and the answer is the same), but SLOPE and INTERCEPT are not: swapping the ranges fits a different line, the one that predicts x from y. The trendline equation on a chart should agree with SLOPE and INTERCEPT.

Desmos Scientific handles plain arithmetic only here. To get r-squared from a value of r, multiply it by itself: type 0.8309^2 and read about 0.69 (the exact r gives 0.6903; rounding r first changes the fourth decimal). To check the slope rule, type 0.8309*3.5456/13.0931 and read about 0.225. Desmos is not set up to compute r from a list of data here; use Excel for that.
🌐 WHERE THIS MATTERS
Reports in many fields, from education studies to sports analytics, summarize a relationship with a correlation and often with r-squared. A reader who knows today’s lesson can ask the right questions: Which direction? How strong a straight-line pattern? What share of the variation is accounted for? Was there an outlier or a mixture of groups? And is the writer claiming more than a correlation can show?
🤖 AI TRAINING · PROMPT → PREDICT → VERIFY
Prompt. Ask an AI tool: “In a made-up survey of 50 students, the correlation between hours of video games played per week and quiz score was r = −0.4. Interpret r = −0.4: describe the direction and strength, and say what r-squared means here.”
Predict. Before you read its answer, write your own interpretation. The sign is negative, so more play tended to go with lower scores. The size 0.4 is on the weak side of moderate (the rough guide says weak: 0.2 to 0.5). And r² = 0.4² = 0.16, so about 16% of the variation in scores is accounted for by the straight-line relationship with hours played; most of the variation is not.
Verify. Check the AI’s language against yours, line by line. Did it get the direction right (negative: more play, lower scores)? Did it call the strength weak or weak-to-moderate rather than “strong”? If it gave r², is it 0.16, a share of the variation, not “40%”? Did it say “40% of students” or “40% correlated” (wrong)? Did it say that gaming lowers scores (a cause claim the data cannot support)? Did it mention that r describes the straight-line pattern across the group, not every individual?
Flag the errors. Write three sentences: what the AI got right, the first thing it got wrong or overstated (quote it), and the corrected sentence. The most common slips in this topic are treating r as a percent, mixing up direction and strength, and sliding from “is associated with” to “causes.”
