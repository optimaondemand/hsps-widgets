🧭 THE ARC
A year ago this course gave you probability — the grammar: sample spaces, rules, conditioning, distributions. This semester gave you statistics — the rhetoric: how to speak honestly with data when you only ever see part of the picture. The bridge between them is inference, and the cleanest way to say it is this: inference is probability read in reverse. Probability starts from a known population and asks what samples will look like. Inference starts from the one sample you have and asks what population could have produced it.
So today's tour is not six topics to re-memorize. It is one chain with the doors between rooms thrown open — because the final, like real problems, does not announce which room it is standing in. (Lightly, as you walk: the semester also carried a character thread — Honesty in how we display, Courtesy in how we summarize, Wonder at the normal curve, Responsibility in what we claim and in how we sample, and Service in what inference is for. The tour below is that same thread seen as mathematics.)
📊 ROOMS 7 & 8 · DISPLAYS AND SUMMARIES, TWO LANGUAGES FOR ONE DISTRIBUTION
Module 7 taught you to draw a distribution: bar charts and circle graphs for categories; line plots, histograms, and box plots for numbers; and the discipline of spotting a display that lies. Module 8 taught you to say a distribution in numbers: center (mean, median), spread (SD, IQR), shape, and outliers. These are not two topics. They are two languages for the same object, and fluency means translating: a long right tail in the histogram is the mean pulled above the median in the summary; a tall box in the box plot is a large IQR.
Every later module reads in both languages. When you checked a normal model you looked at a histogram and computed a mean and SD. When you inspected residuals you plotted them and summarized them. If a question on the final feels like it is about a graph, ask what number it is secretly about — and the reverse.
🔔 ROOM 9 · THE NORMAL MODEL, MEAN AND SD PROMOTED INTO A PREDICTIVE MACHINE
Module 9 took Module 8's two summaries and promoted them. Once you decide a distribution is well described by a mean \(\mu\) and an SD \(\sigma\), the normal model lets you predict: the percent below a value, between two values, the cutoff for the top 10%. The empirical rule gave the quick version (68, 95, 99.7); the z-score \(z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\) and the normal-area tools gave the exact one. A z-score is just “how many SDs from the mean” — Module 8's SD used as a ruler.
And Galton's board replays Semester 1. Each ball makes a string of independent left-or-right choices — a binomial count — and the pile of balls settles into the bell. The binomial you built in the fall is the normal curve's ancestor, and Wonder was the right virtue: order emerging from many small random pushes.
✍️ CHECK YOURSELF 1 · A SAMPLE-MEAN QUESTION, MODULES 8, 9 AND 12 IN ONE
A made-up population of quiz scores has mean \(\mu = 70\) and SD \(\sigma = 12\). A random sample of n = 36 scores is taken. (a) What is the standard error of the sample mean? (b) What is the probability the sample mean exceeds 73? Before opening the answer: compute the SE, convert 73 to a z-score on the sample-mean scale, and find the area to the right.
▶  Worked it? Check here
(a) The standard error is \(\mathrm{SE} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{12}{\sqrt{36}} = \frac{12}{6} = 2\). It is the SD of the sample mean — how far a typical sample mean lands from \(\mu\). (b) The sample mean has mean 70 and SD 2, and with n = 36 the normal model applies to it whatever the population's shape (the Central Limit Theorem, below). \[z = \frac{73 - 70}{2} = 1.5, \qquad P(\bar{x} > 73) = 1 - 0.9332 \approx 0.0668.\] About a 6.7% chance. Notice the contrast: one individual score above 73 has probability about 0.401 (z = 0.25 on the SD-12 scale), but a mean of 36 above 73 is far less likely — averages cluster. Desmos: (73-70)/(12/sqrt(36)) for z. Excel: =1-NORM.S.DIST(1.5,TRUE) or =1-NORM.DIST(73,70,2,TRUE). (All numbers are made up for practice.)
📈 ROOM 10 · REGRESSION: DESCRIPTION, RESIDUALS REBORN, AND THE CAUSATION QUESTION
Module 10 described two numerical variables at once. A scatterplot is Module 7's display; the correlation \(r\) and the least-squares line are Module 8's summaries stretched to a pair. The slope says how much the response changes, on average, per one unit of the explanatory variable; the intercept is the predicted value at zero — meaningful only if zero is a sensible input. And residuals are Module 8's deviations reborn: observed minus predicted is the same “distance from the center” idea, with the line replacing the mean as the center. A residual plot with no pattern says the line is a fair summary; a curve says try another model (the log-and-straighten move for exponential growth).
Then the semester's hardest honest sentence: correlation is not causation. A strong \(r\) from observational data tells you the variables move together, not why. Module 11 supplies the answer: randomized assignment breaks the link between the explanatory variable and everything lurking behind it, which is the only thing that licenses a causal claim. Responsibility, again: say only what the design supports.
✍️ CHECK YOURSELF 2 · READING A FITTED LINE (MODULE 10)
A made-up study of used cars (ages 1 to 8 years) gives the fitted line \(\hat{y} = 24.0 - 1.8x\), where \(x\) is age in years and \(\hat{y}\) is predicted price in thousands of dollars. (a) Interpret the slope and the intercept in context. (b) Predict the price of a 5-year-old car. (c) Why is a prediction at \(x = 15\) unsafe? (d) In one sentence: why can these data alone not say that age causes the price drop? Before opening the answer: write one sentence for each part.
▶  Worked it? Check here
(a) Slope: for each extra year of age, the predicted price drops by about 1.8 thousand dollars ($1,800). Intercept: the predicted price at age 0 is 24.0 thousand dollars ($24,000), a sensible reading here because a brand-new car sits just outside the observed range of 1 to 8 years. (b) \(\hat{y} = 24.0 - 1.8(5) = 24.0 - 9.0 = 15.0\), about $15,000, a safe in-range prediction. (c) At \(x = 15\) the line gives \(24.0 - 27.0 = -3.0\), a negative price: the line was fit on ages 1 to 8 only, and going outside that range is extrapolation, where nothing in the data supports the straight-line pattern. (d) Age and price were only observed, not assigned, so something else that goes with age (mileage, wear, model year) could be doing the work — only a randomized experiment, where the explanatory variable is assigned by chance, lets us claim it causes the response. Desmos: 24-1.8*5. Excel for real data: =SLOPE(y_range,x_range) and =INTERCEPT(y_range,x_range).
🎲 ROOM 11 · SAMPLING, WHERE HONEST DATA COMES FROM
Module 11 asked the question every earlier module quietly assumed away: where did the data come from? A simple random sample gives every member of the population the same chance of selection, which is what lets a sample stand in for the whole; stratified, cluster, and systematic samples trade convenience for structure, and convenience or voluntary-response samples fail by design. Study design is the same discipline pointed at cause: surveys, observational studies, and experiments differ in what they allow you to conclude, and random assignment, control groups, and blinding are what let an experiment speak to cause.
And Module 11 named the thing Module 12 would measure: sampling variation. Two honest random samples from the same population will not give the same answer. That wobble is not an error to be ashamed of; it is the structure of the problem, and a margin of error is simply the size of that wobble, written down.
💡 ROOM 12 · THE CLT, INTERVALS, AND TESTS WITH REAL DATA
Module 12 gathered every thread. The Central Limit Theorem is why Module 9's normal curve governs sample means whatever the population's shape: take samples of a decent size from a skewed, lumpy, or lopsided population and the sample means still pile into a bell centered at \(\mu\) with spread \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\) — the standard error. Check Yourself 1 used exactly this. A confidence interval then turns that bell around: estimate \(\pm\) z* \(\times\) SE, the estimate plus or minus a margin of error that is a multiple of the standard error.
Here is the promissory note from the fall. Semester 1's Module 6 left two schools arguing about what probability numbers mean and said: wait for real data. Now you have it. A simulation test asks the significance question — if nothing were going on, how often would chance alone produce a result this extreme? — and answers it by repeating the random process thousands of times and counting. An interval answers the estimation question. Both are probability read in reverse, and both rest on one fact: the sample is random, so its variation is predictable. Service is the point: so that decisions affecting many people can rest on evidence rather than impression.
✍️ CHECK YOURSELF 3 · AN INTERVAL AND ITS LIMITS (MODULES 11 AND 12)
In a made-up random poll of n = 400 voters, y = 220 say they favor a proposal. (a) Find the 95% interval for the population proportion using \(\hat{p} \pm 1.96\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n}}\). (b) Give the correct one-sentence reading of “95% confident.” (c) Name one thing this interval does not cover. Before opening the answer: find \(\hat{p}\), then the SE, then the margin, then both endpoints.
▶  Worked it? Check here
(a) \(\hat{p} = \frac{220}{400} = 0.55\). \(\mathrm{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{0.55 \times 0.45}{400}} = \sqrt{0.00061875} \approx 0.0249\). Margin \(= 1.96 \times 0.0249 \approx 0.0488\). Interval: \(0.55 \pm 0.0488\), or about (0.501, 0.599), roughly 50.1% to 59.9%. (b) “95% confident” describes the method: if we repeated the poll many times and built an interval each time, about 95% of those intervals would capture the true proportion. It does not say there is a 95% probability that this interval contains it, and it does not say 95% of voters fall in the interval. (c) The interval measures only random sampling variation. It does not cover bias: a badly worded question, nonresponse (people who never answered may differ), or a list that missed part of the population — a small margin of error can sit on top of a badly wrong estimate. Desmos: sqrt(0.55*0.45/400), then 1.96*0.0249. Excel for the endpoints: =B1-1.96*SQRT(B1*(1-B1)/B2) and =B1+1.96*SQRT(B1*(1-B1)/B2), with the proportion in B1 and n in B2.
🤖 AI TRAINING · PROMPT → PREDICT → VERIFY
Prompt. Ask an AI tool: “A poll of 400 random voters found 55% in favor, margin of error about 5 points. Does that mean there is a 95% chance the true support is between 50% and 60%? Explain.”
Predict. Write your own answer first using Check Yourself 3: the 95% belongs to the method, not to this one interval, and the margin of error says nothing about bias or nonresponse.
Verify. Grade the AI on the one distinction that matters. Does it slide into “95% probability the truth is in this interval”? Does it mention what the margin does not cover? If it gets the reading wrong, you have caught the most common misreading of the year in the wild.

💬 WHAT TO SAY TO YOURSELF ON EXAM DAY
You are not being asked to be impressive; you are being asked to be honest. Read each question for what it actually says, not for the question you were hoping for. If you do not know, reason from what you do know: what is the population, what is the sample, what is the model, what does the number actually measure? Choose the answer you can defend, not the one that merely sounds statistical. A calm, careful reader of data is exactly what this year was training, and the fifty questions in front of you are fifty chances to be one.
