🕵️ A GRAPH CAN TELL THE TRUTH AND STILL MISLEAD
Every display you have drawn so far followed the rules. But the same numbers can be drawn in ways that make a small difference look huge, a falling trend look like a rise, or a doubling look like a quadrupling — and no number on the graph is false. The numbers are right; the picture misleads. Sometimes the cause is dishonesty; sometimes it is carelessness or a software default. The honest reader does not need to know which. She only needs to know how to read a graph defensively.
This lesson teaches five questions that catch the common distortions. The examples are constructed to show the techniques; none is taken from a real newspaper, company, or person. Real graphics that use these tricks are easy to find — once you know what to look for, you will start seeing them.
📏 DISTORTION 1: THE CUT-OFF AXIS — “ZERO START?”
A bar chart encodes a value in a bar's length. That only works if the bars begin at zero, because the eye compares lengths. Suppose 52% of customers of Brand A and 55% of Brand B say they are satisfied (constructed). Here is the same data twice:

Looks dramatic.
The numbers behind the pictures. Truthfully, \(\dfrac{55}{52} \approx 1.058\): Brand B is about 5.8% higher. In the cut-off chart the Brand B bar rises \(55 - 50 = 5\) units above the baseline while Brand A rises only \(52 - 50 = 2\), so B's bar is drawn \(\dfrac{5}{2} = 2.5\) times as tall — a 150% difference to the eye, for a 5.8% difference in fact.
The rule. For bar charts and pictographs, the axis must start at zero. Line graphs and scatter plots encode values by position, not length, so a labeled axis that starts higher is acceptable — but the label must be there, and the next distortion still applies.
📅 DISTORTION 2: THE CHERRY-PICKED WINDOW — “FULL RANGE?”
A line graph shows change over time, and which stretch of time you show decides the story. Monthly riders (in thousands) on a made-up bus line, January to December: Jan 82, Feb 80, Mar 79, Apr 77, May 76, Jun 74, Jul 73, Aug 72, Sep 71, Oct 73, Nov 75, Dec 77.
The whole year
Horizontal: month (0 = Jan, 11 = Dec) · Vertical: riders (thousands)
Only Sep to Dec
Horizontal: month (8 = Sep, 11 = Dec) · Vertical: riders (thousands)
Over the whole year the line fell from 82 to 77, a change of \(77 - 82 = -5\) thousand. Since September it has risen from 71 to 77, a change of \(77 - 71 = +6\) thousand. A headline reading “Ridership is climbing!” and the second picture are not lying about the last four months — they simply do not show the first eight. Ask what comes before the graph begins.
📆 DISTORTION 3: UNEQUAL GAPS DRAWN EQUAL — “EQUAL GAPS?”
Suppose a town's recycling (in tons, constructed) is measured in 2000, 2010, 2020, and 2021: 2000: 10, 2010: 20, 2020: 60, 2021: 61. If the four readings are placed at equal spacing along the axis, the gaps of 10 years, 10 years, and 1 year look the same. But the rates are very different: \(\dfrac{20 - 10}{10} = 1\) ton per year, then \(\dfrac{60 - 20}{10} = 4\) tons per year, then \(\dfrac{61 - 60}{1} = 1\) ton per year. Equal spacing hides that growth quadrupled and then fell back. The axis must be scaled by the true gap, not by the number of readings.
(This is why, in Lesson 21.3, you will build line graphs with the scatter chart type: it spaces points by their true values.)
🖼️ DISTORTION 4: ICONS THAT GROW IN TWO DIRECTIONS — “SIZES TRUE?”
A pictograph uses pictures instead of bars. If the value doubles and the artist draws the icon twice as tall and twice as wide, the eye reads area, not height:
Value 100
icon 40 px wide and tall
Value 200
icon 80 px wide and tall
The second icon is \(\dfrac{80 \times 80}{40 \times 40} = 4\) times the area of the first, though the value only doubled. Honest pictographs repeat a same-size icon (two icons for 200, one for 100) so that the count carries the value.
🏷️ DISTORTION 5: MISSING CONTEXT — “LABELS, SOURCE?”
A graph with no title, no axis labels, no units, no sample size, and no source cannot be checked at all. You learned in Lesson 19.3 what an honest label contains: what was measured, on whom, in what units, how many, and where the data came from. Last week's histogram lesson added one more: bin width. A histogram drawn with the wrong bin width can erase a real gap or invent a bump.
The defensive reader's five questions (keep these; the widget below uses the same five):
✅ READING A GRAPH DEFENSIVELY
(1) Zero start? For bars and pictographs, does the axis begin at zero. (2) Full range? Is the whole time range shown, or only a convenient slice. (3) Equal gaps? Are equal gaps on the axis equal intervals of the variable. (4) Sizes true? Do the sizes of icons and bars match the values (length for bars, same-size icons for counts). (5) Labels, source? Are the title, units, sample size, bin width, and source given.

💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · A LITTLE DESMOS: SIZE OF A DISTORTION
Quantify a distortion with one visible expression. The true percent difference between 52 and 55 is (55-52)/52*100 → 5.77. The drawn ratio of the cut-off bars is (55-50)/(52-50) → 2.5. And the icon area ratio is (80/40)^2 → 4 (type the caret with the ^ key). When a graph looks dramatic, writing the two numbers side by side — what the picture suggests and what the data say — is the quickest audit.
🤖 AI TRAINING · PROMPT → PREDICT → VERIFY
Prompt. Describe this graph to an AI tool and ask what is wrong with it: “A bar chart titled ‘Satisfaction’ shows two bars, 71% and 74%. The vertical axis runs from 70 to 75. There are no axis labels or source.”
Predict. Audit it yourself first with the five questions: the axis does not start at zero, there are no labels, no source, no sample size. How tall would the second bar look compared with the first? (The bars rise 1 and 4 units above the 70 baseline, so 4 times as tall, for a true ratio of about 1.04.)
Verify. Compare the AI's list with yours. Which problems did it catch, and which did it miss? Did it compute the apparent ratio? Then ask it a harder follow-up and watch for a confident but wrong claim, such as saying that every line graph must start at zero.
⚡ TODAY'S ACTIVITIES (about 45 minutes total)
1
Audit the Graphs
15 min
1
Run all five questions on each (all constructed): (a) a bar chart of two sports teams' wins, 18 and 20, on an axis from 16 to 20; (b) a line graph titled “Sales Skyrocket!” showing only the last three of 24 months, during which sales rose from 410 to 430 after falling from 600; (c) a pictograph of cars sold, 100 versus 200, where the 200-car icon is twice as tall and twice as wide as the 100-car icon.
2
For each, write which question it fails and what the true comparison is.
▶  Check
(a) Fails zero start: the bars rise 2 and 4 units above the 16 baseline, so the 20-win bar looks twice as tall, but the true ratio is 20/18, about 1.11. (b) Fails full range: over 24 months sales fell from 600 to 430, while the last three months rose 20. (c) Fails sizes true: the icon has four times the area of the other, though the value is only doubled.
2
Fix It
15 min
1
Redraw (a) from the audit with an honest axis. Then describe, in two sentences, how you would redraw (b) and (c).
2
Explain to a partner why a labeled nonzero axis can be acceptable on a line graph but not on a bar chart.
▶  Check
(a) Axis from 0 to 20 or 25; bars of 18 and 20 look nearly equal. (b) Show all 24 months (and the labeled axis). (c) Use one same-size icon per 100 cars: one icon versus two. The nonzero line graph is acceptable because the reader judges position and slope, not bar length — though the label must be present and the time range must still be full.
3
Spot the Spacing
15 min
1
A graph shows a club's membership in 2010, 2015, 2020, and 2021 with the four points equally spaced on the horizontal axis. The values are 40, 55, 100, and 104. Compute the rate of change (members per year) for each interval and say what the equal spacing hides.
2
Write one sentence a younger student could use to remember the five questions.
▶  Check
Rates: (55 − 40)/5 = 3 per year; (100 − 55)/5 = 9 per year; (104 − 100)/1 = 4 per year. Equal spacing treats 5-year and 1-year gaps alike, hiding that growth tripled between 2015 and 2020 and then slowed. Memory sentences will vary; look for all five ideas.
