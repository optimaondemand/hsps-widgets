Fitting a Line: Slope and Intercept in Context

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A line is a model. Say what it says, and no more.
The line ŷ = a + bx; slope and intercept in context with units; building a line from two points; why we need a rule for the best line; least squares and the sum of squared misses; a trendline in Excel; association is not cause

✨ A WONDER TO START WITH
Last lesson you looked at a cloud of points and described it in words: rising, falling, tight, loose. Words are a fine start, but a cloud of points is hard to use. Suppose a student studied 4 hours. What score should we expect? You could squint at the cloud and guess. Or you could draw one straight line through it and read the answer off the line.
That one line is a small piece of mathematics doing a large job. Two numbers, where the line starts and how steeply it climbs, compress a whole cloud into a sentence you can say aloud and use to make predictions. This is an old trick of applied mathematics: replace a messy world with a simple model, and then be honest about what the model can and cannot tell you.
That honesty is this module's virtue, responsibility. A line makes claims. Today you will learn to read those claims exactly, to say them with their units, and to refuse the claims the line does not make.
A note on honesty. Every scenario, dataset, and equation in this lesson is constructed to teach the idea. None of it is real data about real people, cars, cafes, or plants.
📏 A LINE AS A MODEL
A straight-line model has the form
ŷ = a + bx
Here x is the explanatory variable (the one we use to predict, from last lesson), and ŷ (read “y-hat”) is the predicted value of the response variable. The little hat is a promise to be careful: it means predicted by the model, not observed. A real data point has a y; the line gives a ŷ. They are usually not equal.
The two numbers that define the line:
▶ a, the intercept. The predicted value ŷ when x = 0. On a graph, it is where the line crosses the vertical axis.
▶ b, the slope. How much ŷ changes when x goes up by 1. Rise over run: the change in ŷ divided by the change in x.
A positive slope means the line climbs as you move right; a negative slope means it falls. A slope of 0 would be a flat line: the model predicts the same ŷ whatever x is.
🗣️ READING SLOPE AND INTERCEPT, WITH UNITS
A slope is never just a number. It is a number with units: units of y per unit of x. And it is a statement about what the model predicts. Use this frame:
For each additional [1 unit of x], the model predicts [y] to change by about [b units of y].
The intercept has a frame too: when x is 0, the model predicts [y] to be about [a units of y]. But before you say that sentence, ask one question: is x = 0 a sensible place to look? If 0 lies far outside the x values in the data, the line out there is a guess about territory we never measured. Then the intercept is just the number that anchors the line, and it has no real-world meaning.
Here are three constructed models side by side.
Constructed modelSlope in contextIntercept in context
ŷ = 49.5 + 6.5x
(x = hours studied, data from 0.5 to 6 hours; ŷ = quiz score in points)		For each additional hour studied, the model predicts a quiz score about 6.5 points higher.The model predicts about 49.5 points at 0 hours. Zero hours is just outside the data (the smallest is 0.5), so read it as a rough anchor, not a measured result.
ŷ = 28 − 2x
(x = age of a car in years, data from 1 to 10 years; ŷ = asking price in $1000s)		For each additional year of age, the model predicts an asking price about $2 thousand lower.At age 0 the model predicts about $28 thousand. A brand-new car is just outside the data, so treat that as a loose guide.
ŷ = −80 + 0.85x
(x = height in cm, data from 150 to 190 cm; ŷ = weight in kg)		For each additional centimeter of height, the model predicts a weight about 0.85 kg higher.No sensible meaning. It would say a person 0 cm tall weighs −80 kg. Zero is far outside 150 to 190, and the number is negative. The intercept just positions the line.
Two habits to build. First, say “predicts”, not “causes” or “makes.” Second, say “about”, because a line is an approximation and points do not sit exactly on it.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 1: A LINE FROM TWO POINTS (POSITIVE SLOPE)
Problem. On a constructed scatter plot of hours studied (x) against quiz score in points (y), you draw a straight line by eye. It passes through the two plotted points (1, 58) and (5, 85). Find the equation of your line, write the slope in context, and predict the score for 3 hours.
Teacher's voice: The slope is rise over run, so I take the change in score over the change in hours. Then, to find the intercept, I take any point on the line, put it into ŷ = a + bx, and solve for a, which gives a = y − bx.
▶ Slope. b = (85 − 58) ÷ (5 − 1) = 27 ÷ 4 = 6.75 points per hour.
▶ Intercept. Use the point (1, 58): a = 58 − 6.75 × 1 = 51.25 points.
▶ Equation. ŷ = 51.25 + 6.75x.
▶ Check with the other point. At x = 5: 51.25 + 6.75 × 5 = 51.25 + 33.75 = 85. It lands on (5, 85), so the line passes through both of my points.
▶ Slope in context. For each additional hour studied, the model predicts a quiz score about 6.75 points higher.
▶ Prediction at 3 hours. ŷ = 51.25 + 6.75 × 3 = 51.25 + 20.25 = 71.5 points.
Sense check. 3 hours lies between 1 hour (58) and 5 hours (85), and 71.5 lies between 58 and 85. Reasonable.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 2: A LINE FROM TWO POINTS (NEGATIVE SLOPE)
Problem. A constructed plot of car age in years (x) against asking price in $1000s (y) has a line drawn through (2, 24) and (10, 8). Find its equation and read the slope.
Teacher's voice: This line falls as it goes right, so I expect a negative slope. I will keep the sign carefully: the change in price is 8 − 24, which is negative.
▶ Slope. b = (8 − 24) ÷ (10 − 2) = −16 ÷ 8 = −2 ($1000s per year).
▶ Intercept. a = y − bx = 24 − (−2)(2) = 24 + 4 = 28.
▶ Equation. ŷ = 28 − 2x. Check: at x = 10, 28 − 20 = 8. ✓
▶ Slope in context. For each additional year of age, the model predicts an asking price about $2 thousand lower.
The classic slip. Writing a = y + bx instead of y − bx. With a negative slope that slip gives 24 + (−2)(2) = 20, and the line would miss the point (2, 24). Always check the line against the second point.
⚖️ WHY WE NEED A RULE
You drew your line through two points you chose. A classmate looking at the very same cloud may choose two different points and draw a different line. Both lines can look reasonable. Which is better? “It looks better to me” is not an answer a scientist can use, so we need a rule.
Here is a concrete case from the Hours studied scenario in the interactive below (15 constructed students). Two classmates draw lines by eye:
ClassmatePoints chosenLineSum of squared misses
Classmate 1(1, 58) and (5, 85)ŷ = 51.25 + 6.75xabout 286.59
Classmate 2(1, 49) and (6, 90)ŷ = 40.8 + 8.2xabout 510.26
What is a sum of squared misses? For each data point, the miss is the vertical distance from the point to the line: the observed y minus the predicted ŷ. Square each miss (so that misses above and below the line both count as positive, and big misses count much more than small ones), then add them all up. This total is called the SSE, the sum of squared errors. A small SSE means the line stays close to the points. (You will study these misses, called residuals, in detail in Week 28; today you only need the idea.)
Now the rule. The least-squares line is the one line that makes the SSE as small as it can possibly be. No other straight line has a smaller sum of squared vertical misses. For the Hours studied scenario that line is
ŷ = 49.404 + 6.5542x     with SSE about 196.19
so it beats both classmates (286.59 and 510.26). Notice also how close a tidy rounded line comes: ŷ = 50 + 6.5x has an SSE of about 199.13. Rounded lines can be good, but only the least-squares line is the smallest of all. We will not derive how it is found; spreadsheets and software do that arithmetic. What you need is to know what the rule is, and one fact about the answer:
The least-squares line always passes through the point (x̄, ȳ), the mean of the x values and the mean of the y values.
In Hours studied the means are x̄ = 3 hours and ȳ = 69.067 points, and indeed 49.404 + 6.5542 × 3 = 69.07. The line pivots through the center of the cloud.

🎯 WORKED EXAMPLE 3: READING THE LEAST-SQUARES LINES
Problem. The least-squares lines for the two scenarios you just fitted are ŷ = 49.404 + 6.5542x (hours studied to quiz score in points) and ŷ = 138.998 − 1.4329x (outdoor temperature in °F to cups of hot chocolate sold). Interpret both slopes, comment on both intercepts, and predict hot chocolate sales at 50°F.
Teacher's voice: For each line I write the slope sentence first, with units on both sides of the word per. Then I check where x = 0 sits compared with the data before I say anything about the intercept.
▶ Hours studied slope. For each additional hour studied, the model predicts a quiz score about 6.55 points higher.
▶ Hot chocolate slope. For each additional degree Fahrenheit, the model predicts about 1.43 fewer cups sold. The slope is negative, so the line falls.
▶ Hours studied intercept. The data run from 0.5 to 6 hours, so 0 is just outside. The model's 49.4 points at 0 hours is a rough anchor.
▶ Hot chocolate intercept. The temperatures in the data run from 28°F to 80°F. Zero degrees is far outside, and 138.998 cups at 0°F is the line carried well beyond anything measured. Do not report it as a finding.
▶ Prediction at 50°F. ŷ = 138.998 − 1.4329 × 50 = 138.998 − 71.645 = 67.4 cups (about 67). Fifty degrees is inside the data, so this is a fair use of the line.
Be careful with the sentence you write. The model does not say that a colder day makes people buy hot chocolate, and it does not say that studying makes a score rise. It says how the predictions move. More on that below.
📜 A MOMENT IN THE HISTORY OF MATHEMATICS · GALTON AND REGRESSION TOWARD THE MEAN
Francis Galton (1822–1911), the English scientist you met in Week 26, also studied how traits pass from parents to children. In the later 1800s he compared the heights of parents with the heights of their adult children. He noticed a pattern. The children of very tall parents tended to be tall, but on average they were closer to the average height than their parents were. The reverse held for very short parents: their children tended to be short, but on average closer to the average. He described this with the word regression, using phrasing such as regression toward mediocrity. That is why a method for fitting a line to data still carries the word regression, which sounds like going backward even though the method is simply fitting a line. And note what the pattern is not: it is a statistical effect, not a force that makes families pull back toward the average. It appears whenever two measurements are related but not perfectly. See it yourself: in the interactive above, choose Regression toward the mean (40 made-up parent and child heights, measured in standard deviations from average). The least-squares slope is about 0.5, so a parent at +2 SD has a predicted child at about +1.00 SD: tall, but closer to average. Switch on Show the line y = x (a child exactly as tall as the parent) and compare it with the fitted line. This is a short sketch; historians add many details beyond it.

Translate it. Excel writes the equation with plain y and x, and puts the slope first. In this lesson's notation, with names and units, the model is ŷ = 15.393 + 0.6857x, where x is minutes of daily practice and ŷ is predicted words per minute. The slope sentence: for each additional minute of daily practice, the model predicts typing speed about 0.69 words per minute higher. The intercept says 15.4 words per minute at 0 minutes, but the data start at 10 minutes, so treat it as an anchor, not a finding.
Check the fact about the middle. The mean of the minutes is 27.5 and the mean of the words per minute is 34.25. Compute 15.393 + 0.6857 × 27.5 and you get about 34.25: the line passes through (x̄, ȳ), as the least-squares line must. (An independent least-squares calculation for these eight points gives a slope of 0.68571 and an intercept of 15.3929, matching Excel.)
About R-squared. The label also shows R². It is a number between 0 and 1 that Week 28 will explain. For now, just copy it down and do not interpret it yet.
A prediction in a cell. Click A11, type 30, press Enter. Click B11 and type =15.393+0.6857*A11, then press Enter. Excel shows about 35.96, the model's predicted words per minute at 30 minutes. This is a plain formula built from the numbers on the chart.
Honest note. Menu names and button positions differ a little between desktop Excel and Excel for the web, and the Format pane can look different. If you cannot find a button, search the ribbon for trendline. Your chart must show a straight trendline, the equation, and axis titles with units.
⚖️ RESPONSIBILITY: WHAT A LINE SAYS, AND WHAT IT DOES NOT
A fitted line describes an association in the data you have. It does not, by itself, show that changing x would change y. Look at the difference in these constructed sentences about the hours-studied model.
Says more than the model doesSays what the model says
Each extra hour of studying raises your score by 6.55 points.For each additional hour studied, the model predicts a quiz score about 6.55 points higher.
A student who studies 0 hours will score 49.4.The model predicts about 49.4 points at 0 hours, but 0 hours is outside the data, so this is a rough anchor.
This line proves studying causes higher scores.Students who studied more tended to score higher in these data; the line summarizes that association.
Why the care? Other things may travel with hours studied: how much a student already knew, how much sleep they got, how much they care. A line fitted to data we merely observed cannot separate these. Week 28 returns to this carefully. For today, the habit is: say what the model predicts, give the units, and stop there. To say more is to claim what you have not shown.
💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · A LINE FROM TWO POINTS IN DESMOS, A TRENDLINE IN EXCEL
Desmos Scientific does plain arithmetic, so type the formulas the way you write them. Slope from the two points (1, 58) and (5, 85): type (85-58)/(5-1) and you get 6.75. Put each subtraction in its own parentheses so the division covers the whole difference. Intercept: type 58-6.75*1 and you get 51.25. A prediction at 3 hours: type 51.25+6.75*3 and you get 71.5. For the negative-slope line through (2, 24) and (10, 8), type (8-24)/(10-2) to get −2, then 24-(-2)*2 to get 28. A common slip is to subtract the points in a different order on the top and the bottom, which flips the sign of the slope.

Excel draws the line for you: select the two columns, Insert a Scatter chart, add a Trendline, and check Display Equation on chart and Display R-squared value on chart. The equation shows the slope first, as y = bx + a. To use the equation in a cell, type its numbers into a formula, such as =15.393+0.6857*A11 for a value in A11. Week 28 teaches the spreadsheet functions that compute the slope and intercept directly.
🤖 AI TRAINING · PROMPT → PREDICT → VERIFY
Prompt. Ask an AI tool: “A made-up café fitted a line to 14 days of data: predicted cups of hot chocolate sold = 139 − 1.43 × (outdoor temperature in °F). The temperatures in the data ranged from 28°F to 80°F. Interpret the slope and the intercept in context, with units, and predict sales at 50°F.”
Predict. Before you read its answer, write your own interpretation of the slope and the intercept, and a prediction at 50°F. (You should get: for each additional degree, about 1.43 fewer cups; 139 cups at 0°F, which is far outside 28 to 80, so no real meaning; and 139 − 1.43 × 50 = 67.5 cups.)
Verify. Check its arithmetic in Desmos by typing 139-1.43*50 (67.5). Then check the units (cups per degree Fahrenheit), the wording (does it say predicts, or does it say temperature causes or makes sales change?), and what it does with the intercept. Watch for these common errors: causal language (each extra degree reduces sales by 1.43 cups, as if the line proved it); a wrong or missing unit (for example, 1.43 percent); reading the intercept as a real finding (at 0°F the café sells 139 cups) when 0°F is outside the data; and swapping the roles of x and y. Flag anything wrong, vague, or too confident, and say what you would ask next.
The verified interpretation, for comparison: for each additional degree Fahrenheit, the model predicts about 1.43 fewer cups of hot chocolate sold. The intercept, 139 cups at 0°F, is far outside the temperatures in the data (28°F to 80°F), so it has no sensible meaning here. At 50°F, which is inside the data, the model predicts about 67.5 cups. The model describes an association in these made-up data and does not show that temperature causes the change.
