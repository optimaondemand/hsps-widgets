Lesson 29.1
Exponential Fits and the Log Transform
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Some things grow by the same percent each step. A logarithm can straighten that curve so we can fit it.
Same amount versus same percent; what a natural log is; fitting y = a · bx by fitting a straight line to (x, ln y); checking the transformed scatter and its residuals; converting back; reading a and b; predicting with care; when logs do not help
Reading & work: Read every section in order, work each example on paper before you read its solution, use the Straighten the Curve interactive, follow the Excel steps, and complete the three activities at the bottom. Plan on about 75 minutes.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to recognize exponential growth or decay (the same percent change each step, not the same amount) from a table, a scatter plot, and the curved residual pattern of a straight-line fit.
2
I will be able to fit y = a·bx by taking the natural log of y, fitting a straight line to (x, ln y), checking the transformed scatter and its residuals, and converting back with a = eintercept and b = eslope.
3
I will be able to interpret b as the multiplier per step (percent change = (b − 1) × 100) and a as the starting value at x = 0, and predict inside the data range while cautioning about extrapolation and impossible values.
4
I will be able to say honestly when a log transform does not help.

✨ A WONDER TO START WITH
Fold a sheet of paper in half, again, and again. Each fold doubles the thickness. The thickness does not go up by the same amount each fold; it goes up by the same multiplier. Things that grow this way start out looking harmless and then climb faster and faster, so a straight line is the wrong shape for them. Last lesson you learned that a curved pattern in the residuals means a straight line is not adequate. Today you meet a famous cure: change the scale so the curve becomes a straight line, fit the line there, and change back.
The tool is the logarithm, an idea that turns the awkward operation of repeated multiplying into plain adding. That is a genuine trick of thought, and it deserves a careful, honest use. Responsibility, this module's virtue, means we also say when the trick fails and how far a fitted curve can be trusted.
A note on honesty. Every scenario, dataset, and equation in this lesson is constructed (simulated) to teach the idea. None of it is real data about real bacteria, accounts, or products.
📏 THE SAME AMOUNT OR THE SAME PERCENT?
Two constructed plans each start at 100 and run for four steps. Plan A adds 50 every step. Plan B multiplies by 1.5 every step.
Step (x)01234
Plan A (add 50)100150200250300
Plan B (multiply by 1.5)100150225337.5506.25
Plan B: ratio to the step before—150 ÷ 100 = 1.5225 ÷ 150 = 1.5337.5 ÷ 225 = 1.5506.25 ÷ 337.5 = 1.5
Plan A has a constant difference (+50 every step): that is a straight line, the model you already know, y = a + bx. Plan B has a constant ratio (× 1.5 every step, a 50% increase each step): that is exponential growth, written y = a·bx. Here a is the starting value (at x = 0) and b is the multiplier per step.
▶ If b is greater than 1, the quantity grows (b = 1.5 means +50% per step).
▶ If b is between 0 and 1, the quantity decays (b = 0.8 means −20% per step: each value is 80% of the one before).
▶ The percent change per step is (b − 1) × 100.
With measured data the ratios are never exactly equal, because of noise. You look for ratios that are roughly constant. Two other clues: on a scatter plot the points curve upward (or flatten toward a floor, for decay), and a straight line fitted to the data leaves a curved residual pattern, as you saw last lesson.
💡 WHAT IS A LOG, IN PLAIN WORDS?
The number e is about 2.71828. It shows up everywhere in growth, and it is the base used for the natural logarithm, written ln. Here is the whole idea in one sentence: ln y is the exponent you must put on e to get y.
▶ Since e2 ≈ 7.389, we have ln(7.389) ≈ 2. (Check on a calculator: type e^2, then ln(7.389); you should get about 7.389 and about 2. Verify on your own calculator.)
▶ ln(5) ≈ 1.6094, because e raised to about 1.6094 is 5.
▶ ln(1) = 0, because e0 = 1.
Why does this help? Logs turn multiplying into adding: ln(A × B) = ln A + ln B. So if a quantity is multiplied by the same number each step, its log goes up by the same amount each step. Watch it happen with a quantity that doubles (3, 6, 12, 24):
y361224
ln y1.09861.79182.48493.1781
Change in ln y—0.69310.69310.6931
The ratio is constant (× 2), so the log changes by a constant amount, 0.6931, which is ln 2. A constant ratio becomes a constant difference, and a constant difference is exactly what a straight line has. In symbols, if y = a·bx, then
ln y = ln a + (ln b)·x
That is a straight line in x, with intercept ln a and slope ln b. So: take ln of every y, fit a straight line to the points (x, ln y), then undo the log to get a = eintercept and b = eslope. (We take the log of the dependent variable y, not of x.)

📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 2: FIT IT WITH A LOG, STEP BY STEP
Problem. Fit an exponential model to the seven bacteria counts above, check the fit on both scales, and compare it with a straight-line fit.
Teacher's voice: First I take ln of every count, since the log goes on y. Then I fit an ordinary straight line to (hours, ln count), just as in the last two lessons. Then I check the residuals, and only then convert back.
Step 1: transform. Compute ln y for each count (for example ln 210 − ln 120 = 0.5596; the table below lists all seven).
Step 2: fit a straight line to (x, ln y). The least-squares line is
ln ŷ = 4.7259 + 0.6680x    (r = 0.9996 on the log scale)
Step 3: convert back.
▶ a = e4.7259 = 112.83 (the model's starting count at hour 0).
▶ b = e0.6680 = 1.9504 (the multiplier per hour).
ŷ = 112.83 · 1.9504x

The straight-line residuals run +, +, −, −, −, −, +: a smile. That curved streak pattern says a straight line is not adequate (and the line even predicts a negative count at hour 0, which is impossible). The log fit, with random residuals, is the better model.

The residual shapes are the stronger evidence, and in some data a straight line has a high r2 and still leaves a curved residual pattern (you will see this in the interactive).
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 3: READ a AND b, THEN PREDICT
Problem. Use ŷ = 112.83 · 1.9504x (hours 0 to 6, simulated counts). (i) Interpret a and b. (ii) Predict the count at 4.5 hours. (iii) Is it sensible to predict the count at hour 10?
▶ (i) b = 1.9504. Each hour the predicted count is multiplied by about 1.95. The percent change is (1.9504 − 1) × 100 = +95.0% per hour, roughly a doubling. a = 112.83. The model's starting count at hour 0 is about 113 cells. (The observed count at hour 0 was 120; the model gives a smoothed value.)
▶ (ii) Hour 4.5. This is inside the data range (0 to 6): interpolation. ŷ = 112.83 · 1.95044.5 = about 2,280 cells (equivalently e4.7259 + 0.6680·4.5). That sits between the observed counts at hour 4 (1,650) and hour 5 (3,100), a sensible spot.
▶ (iii) Hour 10. The formula gives ŷ = 112.83 · 1.950410 = about 89,886 cells. But 10 is beyond the data (0 to 6): extrapolation. We have no evidence that the same doubling continues. A real dish would eventually run short of room and food, and growth would level off, while the formula keeps multiplying forever. Trust the model inside the data range; treat anything beyond as a clearly labeled guess.
A reminder about wording. The model describes a pattern in these data. It does not explain what is driving the growth; association is not causation, and a good fit is never proof of a mechanism.

⚠️ WHEN LOGS DO NOT HELP
A log transform is a tool, not a magic wand. Be ready to say “no” in these cases:
▶ The data add about the same amount each step. Then a straight line already fits, and the transformed scatter would curve the wrong way (the last widget scenario shows this: steady growth, and the log makes the pattern worse).
▶ The transformed scatter still curves, or the residuals on the log scale show a streak pattern. Then y = a·bx is not the right shape either, and you should say so rather than force it.
▶ Some y values are zero or negative. ln of 0 or of a negative number does not exist, so the transform cannot be done on those points. You must not quietly delete them; report the problem.
▶ The pattern levels off. Many growth processes speed up and then slow down. An exponential fit may be fine for the early part and wrong afterwards. Predict inside the data range, and treat extrapolation as a labeled guess. (A decay model never reaches 0 and a growth model never stops, which is another way the formula can go beyond what is possible.)
The honest report names the checks you ran: the transformed scatter, both residual plots, and where the model stops being believable.
🌎 WHERE YOU MIGHT SEE THIS: GROWTH THAT MULTIPLIES
Exponential models are a common first tool for processes where each step builds on the last: the size of a population, money earning compound interest, the early spread of an illness through a group. In each, the “same percent per step” idea is a reasonable starting description, and a log transform is one way analysts check it and estimate the multiplier. Real growth, though, almost always levels off eventually (resources run out, people change their behavior, a market fills). So an exponential model is best treated as a short-range tool, used inside the range where you have data and checked against new observations. And a good fit describes a pattern; it does not by itself show what is causing it.
💻 EXCEL: THE LOG FIT, CLICK BY CLICK
You will redo Worked Example 2 in a spreadsheet. Use the same constructed bacteria counts: hours 0 to 6 and counts 120, 210, 430, 800, 1650, 3100, 6500.
▶ Open Excel and start a Blank workbook. Save it as Wk29_L1_YourName (practice; you do not upload it).
▶ In A1 to D1 type the headings Hours, Count, LnCount, LnResidual, pressing Tab after each. Type the hours 0 to 6 in A2:A8 and the counts in B2:B8.
▶ Click C2 and type =LN(B2), then press Enter. Click C2 again and drag the small square at its bottom right corner down to C8 (filling down). Column C is now ln y: the first value should be about 4.7875.
▶ In F1 type Slope (ln scale) and in G1 type =SLOPE(C2:C8,A2:A8). The y range comes first: here the ln y column. You should see about 0.6680.
▶ In F2 type Intercept (ln scale) and in G2 type =INTERCEPT(C2:C8,A2:A8) (about 4.7259).
▶ In F3 type a and in G3 type =EXP(G2) (about 112.83). In F4 type b and in G4 type =EXP(G1) (about 1.9504). In F5 type Percent change and in G5 type =(G4-1)*100 (about 95.0). In F6 type R-squared (ln scale) and in G6 type =RSQ(C2:C8,A2:A8) (about 0.9991).
▶ Residuals on the log scale: click D2 and type =C2-($G$2+$G$1*A2), press Enter, and fill down to D8. (The dollar signs keep the intercept and slope cells fixed as you fill down.) In D10 type =SUM(D2:D8): it should be 0 or a number extremely close to 0.
▶ Chart the log scale. Select A1:A8, then hold Ctrl and select C1:C8. Click Insert > Scatter (X, Y) (dots only). Add a chart title and axis titles (the green + Chart Elements button). Tick Trendline there, then right-click the trendline, choose Format Trendline, and tick Display Equation on chart and Display R-squared value on chart. The chart's equation should match G2 and G1 (it is a linear trendline on ln y).
▶ Check with Excel's own Exponential trendline. Select A1:B8 and insert a Scatter chart of count against hours. Add a trendline, choose Exponential in Format Trendline, and display its equation. It should look like y = 112.83e^0.668x. Excel's Exponential trendline is a least-squares fit on ln y, the same method, so its constant should match your a and its exponent should match your slope (0.668 = ln b). Its R² is computed on the log scale, so it should match your RSQ. Check it against your formulas rather than trusting either blindly; displayed digits can differ in the last place.
Honest note. Menu names and button positions differ a little between desktop Excel and Excel for the web, and selecting two separate columns with Ctrl can behave differently in the web version. If it does not work, copy the Hours and LnCount columns side by side and select those two together.
💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · LN AND ex IN DESMOS, THE WHOLE FIT IN EXCEL
Desmos Scientific evaluates typed expressions, so try ln(5) (about 1.6094) and e^2 (about 7.389) and confirm that they undo each other: ln(e^2) should give 2. To convert back from a fitted line ln ŷ = 1.5 + 0.25x, type e^1.5 for a and e^0.25 for b (about 4.4817 and 1.2840), and to predict at x = 6 type e^(1.5+0.25*6) (about 20.09). If a key or typed form behaves differently from what is written here, verify against the live calculator and trust a number you can check by hand (for example, ln(1) must be 0 and e^0 must be 1).

Excel does the whole fit: =LN(y) makes the log column (fill down); =SLOPE(lny,x) and =INTERCEPT(lny,x) give the straight line on the log scale (the y range first, here the ln y column); =EXP(intercept) gives a; =EXP(slope) gives b; =RSQ(lny,x) gives r-squared on the log scale. Chart ln y against x with a linear trendline to see the straight line. As a cross-check, Excel's own Exponential trendline fits the same kind of model by least squares on ln y, so its constant and exponent should match your a and ln b.
🤖 AI TRAINING · ASK AN AI WHEN TO USE A LOG TRANSFORM; VERIFY ON DATA
Prompt. Ask an AI tool: “When should I use a log transform on the y variable before fitting a line, and when should I not? Then use it on this made-up data: hours 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and counts 120, 210, 430, 800, 1650, 3100, 6500. Give the fitted a and b and the percent change per hour.”
Predict. Before you read its answer, decide what clues in the table say “exponential” (ratios near 2), and expect a near 113 and b near 1.95.
Verify. Check the AI's a and b against your Excel or worked-example values (112.83 and 1.9504). Check that it took the log of y (not x), converted back with eslope and eintercept (not by using the slope itself as b), and used (b − 1) × 100 for the percent. Did it mention checking residuals or the limits of extrapolation? Common errors to look for: reporting ln b as if it were b, mixing base-10 logs with natural logs, and a confident claim that the model will keep working far beyond the data. Report what it got right, what it got wrong, and anything stated too confidently.
