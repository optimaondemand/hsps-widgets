💡 STATISTICS IS THE SCIENCE OF LEARNING FROM DATA
Every claim you have heard this month that begins “studies show” or “most people prefer” rests on one thing: somebody collected data. Statistics is the discipline that asks how to collect it, how to display it, and how far it can honestly be trusted. Semester 1 taught you to reason about chance when the rules of the game are known — a fair coin, a well-shuffled deck. Semester 2 turns the problem around. Now the rules are not known; all we have is what we observed, and we must reason backward from it.
Honesty is this module's virtue, and it begins in a very plain place: saying exactly what was measured, on whom, and in what units. A graph can be drawn beautifully and still deceive if nobody can tell what its numbers mean. So before we draw anything, we learn the vocabulary of data itself.
👥 INDIVIDUALS, VARIABLES, AND DATA
The individuals are the people, animals, or things we collect information about — students, trees, batteries, cities. A variable is a characteristic that can take different values from one individual to the next: a student's favorite subject, a tree's height, a city's population. Data are the recorded values of the variables for the individuals we examined.
Notice the word variable: if every student answered the same way, there would be nothing to study. Statistics exists because of variation. Here is a small, made-up table — every row is an individual, every column a variable:

🌎 POPULATION AND SAMPLE
The population is the entire group we want to learn about. A sample is the part of the population we actually examine. If you want to know how many hours the 800 students at a school sleep on a school night, the population is those 800 students. Asking all 800 is called a census; asking 60 of them gives a sample of size \(n = 60\).
Why not always take a census? Three honest reasons. It can cost too much time or money. The population may be too large or spread out to reach. And sometimes the measurement destroys the thing measured: a company that tests how long its batteries last under constant use cannot test every battery, because a battery tested to failure is a battery that can no longer be sold. So we sample — and the price of sampling is that we must be honest about the difference between “what the sample showed” and “what the whole population is like.” Module 11 is devoted to doing that well; for now, hold on to one warning: a sample is only as trustworthy as the way it was chosen.
🔢 THE TWO KINDS OF VARIABLE
Variables come in two families, and which family a variable belongs to decides which displays and calculations make sense.
▶ Categorical variables place each individual into a group or category: favorite subject, rides the bus (yes/no), eye color. Counting how many fall in each category makes sense; averaging them does not.
▶ Numerical variables are measurements or counts for which arithmetic is meaningful: hours of sleep, number of siblings, height. Numerical variables split once more: discrete variables are counted and take separated values (0, 1, 2, 3 siblings), while continuous variables are measured and can take any value in an interval (7.5 hours, 6.25 hours, 7.3333 hours…).
One trap deserves its own warning. A number written down is not automatically a numerical variable. A locker number is a label: locker 407 is not “twice” locker 214 in any way that matters, and the average of two locker numbers means nothing. Jersey numbers, zip codes, and student ID numbers are the same — they are categorical variables that happen to use digits. The test is simple: would adding or averaging these values tell you anything? If not, the variable is categorical.

⚖️ ONE VARIABLE OR TWO?
Data that records one variable for each individual is univariate (“one variable”): the list of everyone's hours of sleep. Data that records two variables for each individual is bivariate: each student's hours of sleep and whether they ride the bus. Bivariate data lets us ask whether two variables are associated — whether knowing one tells us something about the other.
Bivariate data comes in three flavors, each with its own display that you will meet in this module and in Module 10: two categorical variables (a two-way table), two numerical variables (a scatter plot), and one of each (comparing a numerical variable across groups). A caution to carry the whole semester: even a strong association between two variables does not by itself show that one causes the other. Seeing a pattern is the first step; explaining it honestly is a much longer one.
📜 A MOMENT IN THE HISTORY OF MATHEMATICS · JOHN GRAUNT AND THE BILLS OF MORTALITY
John Graunt (1620–1674) was a London merchant, not a university mathematician. In 1662 he published Natural and Political Observations Made upon the Bills of Mortality. The “bills” were weekly lists, kept by London parishes, of burials and christenings with the reported causes of death — a pile of records that most people saw only as a grim list of individual losses. Graunt did something new: he gathered years of bills, sorted the entries into categories, and counted. As the story is usually told, the counts revealed regularities no single entry could show — the number of deaths from many causes stayed strikingly steady from year to year, and slightly more boys than girls were christened. He also questioned how reliable the recorded causes were, which is exactly the honest habit this module is about. Graunt is often credited as an early pioneer of the idea that sorting and counting data can reveal the shape of a population. That is where we begin: with categories, counts, and the care to ask where the numbers came from.
💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · YOUR FIRST EXCEL TABLE
From here on, Excel carries our data work. Today is just the foundation: how a dataset should be laid out.

1. Open Excel and start a Blank workbook.
2. Click cell A1 and type Student, press Tab, type Favorite subject, Tab, Hours of sleep, Tab, Siblings, then Enter. Row 1 holds the variable names.
3. In rows 2–6, type the five students from the table above, one student per row, one variable per column. One row per individual, one column per variable is the layout every statistics tool expects.
4. Look at how Excel aligned your entries: text hugs the left edge of its cell, numbers hug the right. Excel is guessing the variable type — and it will guess wrong for a locker number, so you must not trust it blindly.
5. Name the sheet: double-click the tab at the bottom that says Sheet1, type Sleep survey, and press Enter.
6. Save with Ctrl+S, using a clear file name such as Wk19_YourName.

🤖 AI TRAINING · PROMPT → PREDICT → VERIFY
Prompt. Give an AI tool this list and ask it to classify each variable as categorical, numerical discrete, or numerical continuous: “zip code; number of pets; height; eye color; jersey number; temperature in degrees Celsius.”
Predict. Classify them yourself first. Zip code and jersey number are categorical (labels). Eye color is categorical. Number of pets is numerical, discrete. Height and temperature are numerical, continuous.
Verify. Compare line by line. The usual slip is calling zip code or jersey number “numerical” because they are made of digits. If the AI did, ask it: “Would the average of two zip codes mean anything?” and see whether it corrects itself. Note whether it explains its reasoning or only labels. A tool that is wrong about this will also be wrong about which graph to draw.
⚡ TODAY'S ACTIVITIES (about 45 minutes total)
1
Population or Sample?
12 min
1
A cereal company weighs the contents of 40 boxes pulled from today's production line, which turned out 9,000 boxes. Name the population, the sample, and the sample size \(n\).
2
A principal reads the attendance record of every one of the school's 612 students for the whole term. Is this a sample or a census? Explain in one sentence what the variable is if the principal is studying absences.
3
Give one reason a census would be impractical or impossible for the cereal company.
▶  Check your answers
(1) Population: all 9,000 boxes made today (the variable is the weight of contents). Sample: the 40 boxes pulled. \(n = 40\). (2) A census — every individual in the population was examined. The variable is the number of days absent (numerical, discrete). (3) Weighing every box would be slow and costly; if the check were destructive (e.g., opening and testing the cereal), nothing would be left to sell.
2
Classify the Variables
15 min
1
A bike-share program records, for each trip: trip length in minutes, start station name, number of riders in the group, membership type (member or visitor), and bike ID number. (Constructed example.) Classify each variable as categorical, numerical discrete, or numerical continuous.
2
Say whether each question uses univariate or bivariate data: (a) “How long is the typical trip?” (b) “Do members take shorter trips than visitors?” (c) “Which station is most popular?”
▶  Check your answers
(1) Trip length: numerical, continuous. Start station: categorical. Number of riders: numerical, discrete. Membership type: categorical. Bike ID number: categorical (a label — averaging IDs means nothing). (2) (a) univariate (trip length only). (b) bivariate — membership type and trip length. (c) univariate (start station only).
3
Design a Dataset
15 min
1
Choose a question about your own week (for example, how you spend your evenings). Design a small data table with one individual per row and three variables: one categorical, one numerical discrete, one numerical continuous.
2
Fill in at least four rows with your own real values, and label each column with its variable type. Then, in one sentence, say what honest conclusion four rows cannot support (hint: think population versus sample).
