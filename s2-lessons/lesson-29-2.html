Two-Way Frequency Tables: Joint and Marginal

✨ A WONDER TO START WITH
Every scatter plot you have drawn this module pairs two numbers for each person or thing: hours studied and a score, practice days and free throws. But much of what we want to know is not a number at all. Which lunch do you pick? Which study method did you use? Did you sign up or not? These are categories, and they come in pairs too: lunch and grade, study method and result.
There is no line to fit through categories, and no slope. Instead there is something quieter and very useful: a grid that counts how many fall into each combination. The remarkable thing is how much a small grid of counts can tell you, if you read it patiently and make sure every count is accounted for. Careful accounting is this module's virtue, responsibility, in its plainest form.
In Semester 1 you used two-way tables to find probabilities. Today you step back and treat the table as what it first is: a summary of data. The next lesson will use it to compare groups.
A note on honesty. Every student, record, and count in this lesson is constructed to teach the idea. None of it is real data about real people.
📑 TWO CATEGORIES AT ONCE: WHAT A TWO-WAY TABLE IS
A variable that sorts people into groups (lunch choice, grade level, yes or no) is a categorical variable. When each person in a data set has a value for two categorical variables, the data are bivariate categorical data. A two-way table (also called a two-way frequency table) shows how many fall into each combination.
▶ Rows list the categories of one variable; columns list the categories of the other.
▶ Each inner cell holds a joint frequency: the number of individuals who have both that row category and that column category.
▶ The row totals and column totals, written in the margins of the table, are the marginal frequencies: how many are in each row, or each column, ignoring the other variable.
▶ The number in the bottom right corner is the grand total: how many individuals there are altogether.
Two checks that always work. (1) The row totals must add to the grand total. (2) The column totals must add to the grand total. If either fails, a count is wrong somewhere. These two checks are the table's built-in honesty test.
How this differs from a scatter plot. A scatter plot needs two numerical variables and shows each individual as a point. A two-way table is for two categorical variables and shows counts, not individuals. Different kinds of data, different display. Choosing the display that fits the data is part of telling the truth about it.

📐 RELATIVE FREQUENCIES: WHAT SHARE OF EVERYONE?
Counts depend on how many people were surveyed. A relative frequency turns a count into a share of the whole, so you can talk about “what fraction” or “what percent” of everyone.
joint relative frequency = cell count ÷ grand total      marginal relative frequency = row (or column) total ÷ grand total
Both use the grand total as the denominator. That is what makes them answer “what share of everyone…?” The joint relative frequency is the share who are in one particular combination; the marginal relative frequency is the share who are in one whole row or column.
Percent rounding. When you round each percent, the rounded numbers may not add to exactly 100. That is not a mistake in the table; it is a side effect of rounding. If your rounded percents total 99.9 or 100.1, check that the fractions add to 1 and then move on.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 2: THE LUNCH TABLE AS RELATIVE FREQUENCIES
Problem. Using the 24-student lunch table, find each joint and marginal relative frequency (percent, to the nearest tenth of a percent). Then answer: what share of all students (a) are in Grade 11 and eat off-campus, (b) eat in the cafeteria, (c) are in Grade 10?
Teacher's voice: Every denominator is 24, the grand total. I divide each count by 24 and multiply by 100. For example, Cafeteria and Grade 9: 5 ÷ 24 = 0.2083…, which is 20.8%.
Lunch \ GradeGrade 9Grade 10Grade 11Total
Cafeteria5/24 ≈ 20.8%3/24 ≈ 12.5%2/24 ≈ 8.3%10/24 ≈ 41.7%
Packed3/24 ≈ 12.5%4/24 ≈ 16.7%1/24 ≈ 4.2%8/24 ≈ 33.3%

▶ (a) Off-campus and Grade 11 is a joint cell: 4 ÷ 24 = 16.7% of all students.
▶ (b) Cafeteria is a marginal (row total): 10 ÷ 24 = 41.7% of all students.
▶ (c) Grade 10 is a marginal (column total): 8 ÷ 24 = 33.3% of all students.
Check the totals. The three row marginals add to 41.7 + 33.3 + 25.0 = 100.0%, and the three column marginals to 37.5 + 33.3 + 29.2 = 100.0%. The nine joint percents, however, add to 100.1%, not 100%. That is the rounding effect described above: for instance, three cells are really 4.1666…% and were each rounded to 4.2%. The fractions themselves, 24 ÷ 24, add to exactly 1.
One caution. These percents each answer “what share of all 24?” They do not tell you, say, how likely a Grade 9 student is to eat in the cafeteria. That is a different question, with a different denominator, and it is the subject of the next lesson.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 3: A MISSING CELL FROM THE MARGINALS
Problem. A made-up class of 50 students was split into a morning group (30 students) and an afternoon group (20 students). Altogether, 26 students visited the library and 24 did not. Seventeen of the morning students visited the library. Fill in the rest of the table.
Teacher's voice: I draw the empty table first and write in everything I know, then I use the rule that every row and every column must add to its total.
Group \ LibraryVisitedDid not visitTotal

▶ Morning, did not visit. The morning row must add to 30: 17 + ? = 30, so ? = 30 − 17 = 13.
▶ Afternoon, visited. The Visited column must add to 26: 17 + ? = 26, so ? = 26 − 17 = 9.
▶ Afternoon, did not visit. The afternoon row must add to 20: 9 + ? = 20, so ? = 20 − 9 = 11.
▶ Check with the last column. The Did not visit column should add to 24: 13 + 11 = 24. It does, so every cell is consistent.

💻 EXCEL: TALLY RAW RECORDS WITH COUNTIFS, CLICK BY CLICK
You will tally the 24 raw records into a two-way table with a formula, add the totals, and turn the counts into joint and marginal relative frequencies.
▶ Open Excel and start a Blank workbook. Save it as Wk29_L2_YourName (practice; you do not upload it).
▶ In A1 to C1 type the headings Student, Lunch, Grade, pressing Tab after each. Type the 24 records from Worked Example 1 in rows 2 to 25: student numbers 1 to 24 in column A, the lunch in column B, the grade in column C. Type the grade exactly the same way every time (for example Grade 9), because the counting formula treats different spellings as different categories.
▶ Build the empty table to the right. Leave E1 blank; in F1 to H1 type Grade 9, Grade 10, Grade 11, and in I1 type Total. In E2 to E4 type Cafeteria, Packed, Off-campus, and in E5 type Total.
▶ Click F2 and type =COUNTIFS($B$2:$B$25,$E2,$C$2:$C$25,F$1), then press Enter. This counts the records whose Lunch matches the row label in column E and whose Grade matches the column label in row 1. It should show 5. (The dollar signs lock the ranges and labels so the formula can be filled into the other cells.)
▶ Click F2, drag the small square at the bottom right corner of the cell across to H2, then, with F2:H2 still selected, drag that square down to row 4 (this is called filling). All nine joint counts appear. Compare them with the table in Worked Example 1.
▶ Click I2 and type =SUM(F2:H2), press Enter, and fill down to I4. Click F5 and type =SUM(F2:F4), press Enter, and fill right to I5. The corner cell I5 is the grand total and should show 24; also check that =SUM(I2:I4) gives 24.
▶ For the joint relative frequencies, click F7 and type =F2/$I$5, press Enter. The $I$5 keeps the denominator fixed on the grand total as you fill. Fill right to H7 and then down to row 9. Select F7:H9, click the Home tab, and in the Number group click the % button (Percent Style). Use the Increase Decimal button once so each percent shows one decimal place.
▶ Add the marginal relative frequencies the same way: in I7 type =I2/$I$5 and fill down to I9; in F10 type =F5/$I$5 and fill right to H10; format all of them as percents. Check that the column marginals add to 100% (use =SUM(F10:H10)).
▶ Optional. Excel can also build a count table for you with Insert > PivotTable. You are not required to use it; the formulas above show you exactly where each number comes from.
Honest note. Menu names and button positions differ a little between desktop Excel and Excel for the web. If a percent in your sheet differs from this lesson in the last digit, check how many decimals are shown; Excel stores the full value and only displays a rounded one.
⚖️ RESPONSIBILITY: A TABLE SHOWS ASSOCIATION, NOT CAUSE
When a two-way table compares groups, such as students who used flashcards and students who reread, it is tempting to read a gap as proof that one thing caused the other. A table of counts cannot say that. It shows which combinations occurred and how often. Whether a difference means cause depends on how the data were collected: students who chose their own study method may differ in many other ways, while a comparison where people were assigned to groups at random is built to isolate the effect. You will study that difference in Module 11.
There is a second, smaller caution. A joint relative frequency answers “what share of everyone?” It is not a fair way to compare two groups of different sizes. For that you will need a different denominator, which is exactly what the next lesson adds.
Organizations that run online experiments and surveys often report results in tables like these. Whether a given table supports a claim of cause still depends on the study design behind it.
💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · COUNTIFS AND PERCENTS IN EXCEL, ARITHMETIC IN DESMOS
Excel counts for you. To tally a cell from raw records, type =COUNTIFS(range1,"A",range2,"B"): it counts the rows where the first range equals A and the second range equals B. For example =COUNTIFS(B2:B25,"Packed",C2:C25,"Grade 10") returns 4 for the lunch records. Use =SUM(...) for row totals, column totals, and the grand total. To turn a cell into a share of everyone, divide by the grand total (for example =F2/$I$5) and click Home > Percent Style. A PivotTable (Insert > PivotTable) can also build counts, but it is optional.

Desmos Scientific does plain arithmetic, so a relative frequency is a division. Type 5/24 to get 0.2083…, and 5/24*100 to get about 20.8 as a percent. For a marginal, type 10/24*100 (about 41.7). To check a total, type the sum, for example 10+8+6, and compare it with the grand total.
🤖 AI TRAINING · PROMPT → PREDICT → VERIFY
Task: have an AI complete a partial two-way table, then verify it by arithmetic.
Prompt. Ask an AI tool: “A made-up survey of 60 students has this two-way table. Rows: Plays a sport, Does not play a sport. Columns: Grade 9, Grade 10, Grade 11. Plays a sport: 12, ?, 7, and the row total is 28. Does not play: ?, 11, ?, and the row total is 32. Column totals: 18, 20, 22. The grand total is 60. Fill in the three missing cells, then give the joint relative frequency of ‘plays a sport and Grade 10’ and the marginal relative frequency of ‘plays a sport’ as percents.”
Predict. Before you read its answer, fill in the cells yourself using the row and column totals (you should get 9, 6, and 15), then work out the two percents (15.0% and about 46.7%).
Verify. Check every cell the AI gives against its row total and its column total, and add the rows and columns for yourself. Common errors to look for: a cell that fits its row but breaks its column, totals that no longer add to 60, a percent computed with a row or column total instead of 60, and a confident explanation that skips the checking. Report which items it got right, which it got wrong, and anything it stated too confidently.
