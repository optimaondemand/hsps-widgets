Lesson 34.1
Comparing Two Populations from Two Random Samples
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Two groups give two sample averages, and the gap between them wobbles from sample to sample. Resampling shows how much, and gives an honest range for the true gap.
The difference of two sample means or proportions (B minus A); sampling variation in a difference; the bootstrap; a plausible range for the true difference; reading that range next to zero; a formula check in Excel; what a comparison of two existing groups can and cannot tell us
Reading & work: Read every section in order, work each example on paper before you read its solution, use the Two-Sample Comparison interactive, follow the Excel steps, and complete the three activities at the bottom. The reading, interactive, and Excel steps take about 35 minutes and the activities about 40 more, so plan on about 75 minutes in all.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain that the difference of two sample means (or proportions), B minus A, is a statistic that changes from one pair of random samples to the next, and say what it estimates.
2
I will be able to use the bootstrap (resample each group with replacement, many times) to build a plausible range for the true difference between two populations, and check it with the standard-error formula in Excel.
3
I will be able to read a range for a difference next to zero without overclaiming: what “contains 0” and “does not contain 0” do and do not tell us.
4
I will be able to choose between the two designs (two random samples versus random assignment in an experiment), and state what a comparison of two existing groups can and cannot show about cause.

✨ A WONDER TO START WITH
Two large groups of students take the same quick reaction-time test: a light flashes, and the student taps a key as fast as possible. A researcher cannot test every student, so she draws a random sample from each group: 14 students from Class A and 16 from Class B. Class B’s sample is slower on average, by about 28.7 milliseconds. A headline writer would say “Class B is slower.” A careful reader asks two better questions. Is that gap a real feature of the two groups, or just the luck of which students landed in the samples? And even if the gap is real, can this study tell us why?
In Weeks 31 and 33 you learned that one random sample gives one estimate, and that a different random sample would give a slightly different one. That wobble is sampling variation. Today you meet the same wobble in a new place: the gap between two samples. You will use a computer-powered idea, the bootstrap, to measure the wobble, and you will use it to give the honest answer that a number alone cannot.
Service, this module’s virtue, shows up in a plain way. A principal deciding where to put a new program, or a clinic comparing two neighborhoods, deserves the gap and an honest statement of how far off it could be. Reporting the range, and not only the exciting number, is a small act of service.
A note on honesty. Every group, number, and sample in this lesson is constructed (made up) or simulated by a computer to teach the idea. None of it is real data.
🎲 TWO GROUPS, ONE NUMBER
Call the two groups Group A and Group B. We draw a random sample from each population and compute a mean (for a number such as a reaction time) or a proportion (for a Yes/No outcome such as “walks to school”). The statistic we care about is the observed difference (B minus A):
x-barB − x-barA   (or  p-hatB − p-hatA for proportions)
A positive difference means Group B’s sample is higher; a negative one means lower. We always subtract in the same order, B minus A, so the sign never confuses us. This one number is our estimate of the true difference between the two populations, which we cannot see.
Here is the catch. If the researcher drew two fresh random samples tomorrow, she would get a different difference. The statistic varies from one pair of samples to the next. So the real question is not “what is the difference in my samples?” but “how far from the truth could it be?” To answer that, we need to know how much the difference wobbles, and we cannot draw new samples from the populations. We have only the samples we already have.
🔄 THE BOOTSTRAP: USE THE SAMPLE AS A STAND-IN
Here is the clever move. A random sample is a small picture of its population. So we let each sample stand in for its population and re-run the study on the stand-ins. One resample works like this:
▶ Draw a new Group A the same size as the original by picking values with replacement from the original Group A values (a value can be picked more than once, and some are left out).
▶ Do the same for Group B from the original Group B values, with the original Group B size.
▶ Compute the new difference (B minus A) and record it.
Repeat this thousands of times. The recorded differences pile up into a picture of how much the difference wobbles. This method is called the bootstrap. The middle 95% of the resampled differences is a plausible range for the true difference between the two populations: values that the data leave plausible. (It plays the same role as the interval estimates from last week, but we got it by simulation, with no formula.)
Reading the range next to zero. Zero means “no difference between the populations.” If the plausible range contains 0, the data are consistent with no difference (and also with a difference of either sign, up to the ends of the range). If the range does not contain 0, a real difference is plausible, and “no difference” looks like a poor fit for the data. We will not call anything a “test” yet. Lesson 34.3 turns this idea into a yardstick for deciding how surprising a result is.
Two limits to keep in mind. First, the bootstrap only reuses what the samples contain: if the samples were not random, or are biased, resampling cannot repair them (Weeks 30 and 31). Second, results move a little each time you run it, because the resamples are random. That is why we say “about.”
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 1: THE REACTION-TIME GAP
Problem. A made-up researcher draws a random sample of nA = 14 students from Class A and nB = 16 from Class B and records each reaction time in milliseconds (ms). The summary: Class A has mean 315.29 ms and standard deviation 24.69 ms; Class B has mean 343.94 ms and standard deviation 20.43 ms. Estimate the true difference and give a plausible range for it.
Teacher’s voice: Step 1, the observed difference (B minus A). 343.94 − 315.29 = 28.65 ms. In these samples, Class B is slower. Step 2, the plausible range by bootstrap. Resampling each group with replacement 20,000 times and taking the middle 95% of the new differences gives a range of about 13 to 44 ms. (You will reproduce this in the interactive below; yours will be a little different, which is normal.) Step 3, a quick check with a formula. The wobble of a difference of two means is measured by a standard error that combines both groups: SE = √( sA² ÷ nA + sB² ÷ nB ) = √(609.76 ÷ 14 + 417.53 ÷ 16) = √(43.55 + 26.10) = √69.65 = 8.35 ms. Reaching out about two standard errors each way (1.96 × 8.35 = 16.36) gives 28.65 ± 16.36, or 12.3 to 45.0 ms. That is close to the bootstrap range, so the two methods agree.
Say it in a sentence. “In these random samples, Class B averaged about 29 ms slower than Class A. A plausible range for the true difference between the two classes is about 13 to 44 ms, and 0 is not in that range, so a real gap of some size is plausible.” What we cannot say. We cannot say why Class B is slower. The two classes may differ in age, time of day, sleep, or many other ways at once. Comparing two groups that already exist does not show cause. We return to this below.

📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 2: A DIFFERENCE OF PROPORTIONS
Problem. In two made-up towns, a researcher draws a random sample of 60 students from each and asks whether the student walks to school. In Town A, 31 of 60 walk; in Town B, 38 of 60 walk. Estimate the true difference in the share who walk, and give a plausible range.
Teacher’s voice: Step 1, the sample proportions. p-hatA = 31 ÷ 60 = 0.517 and p-hatB = 38 ÷ 60 = 0.633. Step 2, the observed difference (B minus A). 0.633 − 0.517 = 0.117, about 11.7 percentage points. Step 3, the conditions. Both samples are random and small compared with their towns. Every count (31 and 29, 38 and 22) is at least 10, so the method is reasonable (some sources use 5). Step 4, the bootstrap range. For a Yes/No outcome, each resample draws 60 values from Group A (0.517 of them “yes”) and 60 from Group B (0.633 “yes”). The middle 95% of the resampled differences is about −0.07 to 0.28. Step 5, the formula check. SE = √( p-hatA(1 − p-hatA) ÷ nA + p-hatB(1 − p-hatB) ÷ nB ) = √(0.00416 + 0.00387) = 0.0896. Then 0.117 ± 1.96 × 0.0896 = 0.117 ± 0.176, or -0.059 to 0.292.
Say it in a sentence. “In these samples, a higher share of Town B students walk (about 63% against 52%). A plausible range for the true difference is about −0.06 to 0.29, and 0 is inside it, so the data are consistent with no difference between the towns.” Notice what that does not say. It does not say the towns are the same, and it does not say Town B walks more. The samples are simply too small to tell, and the plausible range reaches from a small advantage for Town A to a large advantage for Town B.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 3: FOUR TIMES THE STUDENTS, HALF THE WOBBLE
Problem. Suppose the same proportions had come from samples four times as large: 124 of 240 walk in Town A (0.517) and 152 of 240 walk in Town B (0.633). Build the formula range and compare it with Worked Example 2.
Teacher’s voice: The difference is the same, 0.117. The standard error is SE = √(0.2497 ÷ 240 + 0.2322 ÷ 240), which works out to 0.0448, exactly half of 0.0896. (Four times the sample size divides the SE by √4 = 2, the same pattern you saw with margins of error.) The range is 0.117 ± 1.96 × 0.0448 = 0.029 to 0.204. Now 0 is not in the range. The samples did not change what they say about the gap; they just shrank the wobble, so the data can now rule out “no difference” as a good fit. A bigger sample narrows the plausible range.
💭 WHICH TAB FITS? RANDOM SAMPLES VERSUS RANDOM ASSIGNMENT
The interactive has two tabs because there are two different ways to get two groups, and they answer different questions.
▶ Two random samples of two populations (today). We draw samples from two groups that already exist and ask how big is the difference between the populations? We estimate the difference and give a plausible range. Use the first tab, Two random samples.
▶ One set of subjects split by random assignment into two treatments (Lesson 34.2). We ask could chance alone explain the gap? Use the second tab, Randomized experiment.
What random samples let us do. If both samples are random, the results about the difference can be carried to the populations they came from (with the honest wobble shown by the range). That is a claim about two groups, such as “all the students in Class A and Class B.”
What they do not let us do. Two existing groups differ in many ways at once. If Class B is slower, we do not know whether age, sleep, the time of day, or something else is behind it. This is the old rule again: correlation is not causation, and comparing two existing groups shows a difference, not a cause. Only a randomized experiment (Week 32 and the next lesson) lets us say a treatment caused a difference. And if the samples are not random (volunteers, whoever was easy to reach), neither the bootstrap nor a formula can fix that: the range can be tight and still wrong.
One sentence about the real world. Real studies often use a formula-based method for the same job, one you may meet later. Resampling gives the same idea in a form you can watch.
💻 EXCEL: SUMMARIZE TWO GROUPS AND BUILD THE RANGE, CLICK BY CLICK
You will summarize two groups and build the difference and its range. Everything here is made up. This is a practice build; you do not upload it (Assignment 34.1 has you build a larger one).
▶ Open Excel and start a Blank workbook. Save it as Wk34_L1_YourName. Rename the first sheet Readings (right-click the sheet tab at the bottom, choose Rename, type the name, press Enter).
▶ Enter the data. This is a made-up set of daily reading minutes: random samples of 12 younger and 14 older students. In A1 type Group A and in B1 type Group B. Type the 12 Group A values in A2:A13: 22, 35, 18, 40, 28, 31, 25, 37, 20, 33, 29, 26. Type the 14 Group B values in B2:B15: 30, 42, 27, 38, 45, 33, 36, 29, 41, 34, 39, 31, 44, 35. The groups may have different sizes.
▶ Labels. In D1 type Statistic, in E1 Group A, in F1 Group B. In D2 type n, in D3 Mean, in D4 SD. Then in D6 type Difference (B minus A), in D7 Standard error, in D8 Lower end (about 95%), in D9 Upper end (about 95%).
▶ Group summaries. In E2 type =COUNT(A2:A13) and in F2 type =COUNT(B2:B15). In E3 type =AVERAGE(A2:A13) and in F3 type =AVERAGE(B2:B15). In E4 type =STDEV.S(A2:A13) and in F4 type =STDEV.S(B2:B15). Press Enter after each. You should see n = 12 and 14, means 28.67 and 36.00, and SDs 6.85 and 5.71.
▶ The difference and its standard error. In E6 type =F3-E3 (Group B’s mean minus Group A’s). In E7 type =SQRT(E4^2/E2+F4^2/F2).
▶ The range. In E8 type =E6-1.96*E7 and in E9 type =E6+1.96*E7. Check: the difference is about 7.33, the standard error about 2.50, and the range about 2.44 to 12.23. This formula range approximates the bootstrap range.
▶ Cross-check with the interactive. In the Two-Sample Comparison, use Paste my own data, paste the two columns into Group A values and Group B values, press Use my data, and run 1000 resamples on the Two random samples tab. The plausible range should land within a few tenths of a minute of your Excel range.
▶ Try it. Change one Group B value in B2:B15 to 60 (a very high reading). Which cells change? What happens to the range, and why?
💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · TWO-GROUP SUMMARIES IN EXCEL; STANDARD ERROR IN DESMOS
Excel. =AVERAGE(range), =STDEV.S(range), and =COUNT(range) summarize each group. The difference is =B-A, the standard error of the difference is =SQRT(sA^2/nA+sB^2/nB), and the approximate 95% range is =diff-1.96*SE and =diff+1.96*SE. Use the same pattern for proportions: =SQRT(pA*(1-pA)/nA+pB*(1-pB)/nB) is the standard error of a difference of two sample proportions.
Desmos Scientific handles plain arithmetic only. For Worked Example 1, type 24.69 × 24.69 ÷ 14 + 20.43 × 20.43 ÷ 16 and then use the square-root button on the result; you should see about 8.3. Multiply by 1.96 for the margin, then add and subtract it from 28.65.
🤖 AI TRAINING · GIVE AN AI TWO CLASSES’ SUMMARY NUMBERS AND AUDIT ITS CONCLUSION
Prompt. Ask an AI tool: “A researcher took random samples of reaction times. Class A: n = 14, mean 315.3 ms, SD 24.7 ms. Class B: n = 16, mean 343.9 ms, SD 20.4 ms. What can we conclude about the two classes?”
Predict. Before you read its answer, write what a careful answer must include: the difference (about 28.7 ms, B minus A); a plausible range for the true difference (about 12 to 45 ms by formula); the idea that the samples are random, so the claim is about the two populations; a warning that this does not show why the classes differ; and mention of sample sizes and spread.
Verify. Read the answer sentence by sentence. Typical AI errors: (1) a causal claim (“Class B’s program slows reactions”) from two existing classes; (2) ignoring the spread and the overlap of the two groups (many Class A students are slower than many Class B students); (3) saying “statistically significant” with no basis shown; (4) ignoring the sample sizes. Did it do any of these? Check its numbers yourself in Excel or Desmos, then rewrite its weakest sentence so that it is true. Verdict. A confident conclusion is not a justified one; the data and the design set the limits.
