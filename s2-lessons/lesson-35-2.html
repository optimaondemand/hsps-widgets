Lesson 35.2
Evaluating Data-Based Reports in the Wild
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A news story can quote a real study and still claim more than the study can carry. Six questions, built from everything you have learned this year, show what a report supports and what it does not.
A reader’s checklist for data-based reports: who and how many, how measured, what kind of study, whether the display is honest, whether the numbers carry their uncertainty and size, and who benefits or what is missing; a full critique of a made-up article; honest limits on what a critique proves
Reading & work: Read every section in order, look for flaws in the made-up article before you read the worked critique, use the Misleading Graph Lab, try the optional Excel check, and complete the three activities at the bottom. The reading, the interactive, and the Excel check take about 35 minutes and the activities about 40 more, so plan on about 75 minutes in all.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to use a six-question reader’s checklist (who and how many, how measured, what kind of study, honest display, uncertainty and size, who benefits or what is missing) to evaluate a data-based report.
2
I will be able to name the flaws in a report in the vocabulary of this course (voluntary response, nonresponse, measurement, causal verb from observational data, truncated axis, extrapolation, tiny effect called significant, many tests) and say which verb the study design allows.
3
I will be able to recompute a margin of error or a percent change from a report’s own numbers, and tell a result that is statistically significant from one that is big enough to matter.
4
I will be able to write a fair critique: say what the evidence does support, what it does not, and what additional evidence would settle the question.

✨ A WONDER TO START WITH
A neighbor forwards you a news story with the message, “Look! Science says this app is the secret to better math grades. We should all sign up.” The story quotes a researcher, a p-value, and a colorful chart. Every one of those things is a number you now know how to question. Is the story wrong? You cannot tell yet, and that is the point. A careful reader does not shout “fake!” and does not nod along. She asks six plain questions and lets the answers decide how much to trust the headline.
Over the last two quarters you have collected tools one at a time: honest displays, summaries that fit the shape, relationships that do not prove causes, samples and study designs, margins of error, intervals, and significance. Today you use them all at once, on a single page of text, the way they are used in real life. (Lesson 35.1 gave the formal logic of tests; Lesson 35.3 will ask you to write a complete critique.)
Service, this module’s virtue, is concrete here. Your neighbors will act on what they read: pay for an app, change a schedule, vote for a plan. Reading numbers fairly, and explaining them kindly, protects them from being misled. It also protects them from being talked out of something that might really be good.
A note on honesty. Every article, researcher, company, town, and number in this lesson is made up (constructed) to teach the idea. None of it is real, and no real outlet, person, or study appears anywhere. In the AI Training task you will bring in a real report of your own, but this page never names one.
✅ THE SIX QUESTIONS: A READER’S CHECKLIST
Earlier you learned a five-point checklist for judging a graph (Zero start? Equal gaps? Full range? Sizes true? Labels, source?). Here is a six-point checklist for judging a whole report. Each question points back to a module you have already finished. Keep it beside you while you read.
▶ 1. Who, and how many? Who is the population, who is in the sample, how were they chosen (random, or by choice and convenience), and how many are there? (Sampling and bias.)
▶ 2. How was it measured? What exactly was asked or recorded, in what words, by what instrument, and was it self-reported? (Wording, response bias, measurement.)
▶ 3. What kind of study, and so which verbs are allowed? Survey, observational study, or experiment? Was anything randomly assigned? Does the report stay inside the range of the data? (Study design, association versus causation, extrapolation.)
▶ 4. Is the display honest? Run the five-point graph checklist: Zero start? Equal gaps? Full range? Sizes true? Labels, source? (Data displays.)
▶ 5. Are the numbers reported with their uncertainty, and is “significant” paired with the size of the effect? Is there an n, a margin of error or interval, and the size of the gap in plain units? (Margin of error, intervals, significance.)
▶ 6. Who benefits, and what is missing? Who paid for it and who gains from the headline? Were many things checked and only one reported? Does the headline match the body? (Source, purpose, many tests.)
Which verbs does the design allow? This is the question that catches the most errors, so keep this table in mind. A random sample lets you describe the population the sample came from. Observational data (people sort themselves into groups) allow words like “is associated with” and “goes with,” but not “causes,” “boosts,” “raises,” or “proves.” Only random assignment in an experiment, with a gap larger than chance would produce, supports “caused” (in those subjects). A result is never proved by one study.
A flaw is a question, not a verdict. Just as an outlier is a question to ask about a data point, a flaw in a report is a question to ask about a claim. It tells you what evidence is missing. It does not tell you the claim is false.
📰 A MADE-UP NEWS ARTICLE: READ IT AS A SKEPTICAL NEIGHBOR
Made up. The Riverbend Courier, Riverbend, the Riverbend Learning Lab, StudyBuddy, and every person and number below are invented for this lesson. Nothing here is real.
App Boosts Riverbend Math Scores, Study Proves
By Jo Marsh · The Riverbend Courier (made up)
RIVERBEND. A new study proves that the StudyBuddy homework app boosts math scores, researchers at the Riverbend Learning Lab said Tuesday.
Last spring the Lab posted a survey link on the app’s home screen. Students who clicked it typed in their weekly app hours and their latest math test score.
“Students who use the app more than three hours a week averaged 80.4 on their last test, against 80.0 for everyone else,” said Lab director Dr. Mara Quill. “That difference is statistically significant, with a p-value of 0.03.” The Courier’s chart is shown below.
Most respondents reported between zero and eight hours a week. Each extra weekly hour went with about 0.1 more points, Quill said, “so a student who used the app 50 hours a week could gain five points.” Nearly two in three respondents (64%) said the app helps them “stay organized.”
“The app is clearly the reason Riverbend students are doing better,” Quill said. The Lab checked 20 school outcomes, among them reading, science, attendance, and homework completion; Quill called math “the standout.” Quill said she will ask the school board to promote the app in every Riverbend math class.
The project was paid for by StudyBuddy’s maker, which called the results “a win for every family.”
🔍 YOUR TURN FIRST: FIND THE FLAWS
Before you read the worked critique, spend three minutes with the article. Use the six questions. Write down every place where the story claims more than the evidence can carry, and next to each one the number (1 to 6) of the question that caught it. Then read on and compare. You will probably find several; the article has been built with nine.
📝 WORKED CRITIQUE: THE NINE FLAWS, SORTED BY QUESTION
Question 1: Who, and how many? Flaw 1, voluntary response. A link on the app’s home screen reaches only students who already use the app, and only the ones who chose to click it. That is a self-selected group, not a random sample (Weeks 30 and 31), so nothing here describes Riverbend students in general. Flaw 2, no n. The article never says how many students answered, so a reader cannot judge the study at all.
Question 2: How was it measured? Flaw 3, self-reported scores. Students typed in their own test scores. People misremember, round up, and sometimes exaggerate, which is the response and measurement bias you met in Weeks 30 and 31. The Lab did not check the scores against school records, at least not in the article.
Question 3: What kind of study, and which verbs? Flaw 4, a causal verb from observational data. Students chose how much to use the app, so this is observational. “Boosts,” “proves,” and “clearly the reason” are not allowed. Students who use an app more may study more, have more help at home, or be more motivated, and any of those could raise scores by itself (the confounders you met in the regression module). The allowed verb is “is associated with,” and only for the students who answered. Flaw 5, extrapolation. Most respondents reported 0 to 8 hours a week, yet the claim is about 50 hours: 0.1 × 50 = 5 points. A straight line fitted to 0–8 hours says nothing reliable about 50 hours, which is more than a full-time job (the regression module).
Question 4: Is the display honest? Flaw 6, a truncated axis. The chart’s vertical axis starts at 79.8, not at 0 (Zero start? No). The two bars are 80.0 and 80.4, but the drawn bars have heights 0.2 and 0.6 above the baseline, so the right bar looks 3 times as tall. The true ratio is 80.4 ÷ 80.0 = 1.005, a difference of half of one percent. Same numbers, very different picture.
Question 5: Uncertainty and size? Flaw 7, a tiny effect headlined as “significant.” The gap is 80.4 − 80.0 = 0.4 points on a 100-point test. A p-value of 0.03 says the gap is unlikely to come from chance alone if the survey were a fair comparison; it says nothing about size or importance (Week 34). To get a p-value this small from a gap this small, the study must have been huge. Here is a rough check, using an assumed spread of about 10 points in each group (made up). A two-sided p-value of 0.03 corresponds to a z of about 2.17, so the standard error of the gap is about 0.4 ÷ 2.17 = 0.184. With equal groups, SE = √(2 × 10² ÷ n), which gives n ≈ 5,900 students in each group, so roughly 11,800 in all. A sample that large detects almost any gap, however small, and the report hides the size behind the word “significant.” Flaw 8, no margin of error or interval. The 64% “stay organized” claim comes with no margin. If the Lab’s fact sheet says about 11,800 answered, the safe rule gives 1 ÷ √11,800 ≈ 0.9 points. That looks wonderfully precise, but the margin of error covers only chance. It cannot repair a self-selected sample (Flaw 1). A tight margin on a biased sample is precisely wrong.
Question 6: Who benefits, and what is missing? Flaw 9, selective reporting and the funder. The Lab checked 20 outcomes and reported the standout. If none of the 20 had any real effect, the chance that at least one would look significant at the 5% level is 1 − 0.9520 = 0.64, about 64% (Week 34). So “math was the standout” is exactly what chance alone would often produce. And the work was paid for by the company that sells the app, a fact placed in the last sentence. Funding does not make results false, but it is a reason to want the evidence to be stronger, not weaker.

What the evidence does support. A fair summary is: “Among the students who chose to answer a survey inside the app, those who reported more than three hours a week of use also reported test scores that averaged 0.4 points higher, a very small difference. The survey cannot say whether the app is the reason, or whether students elsewhere in Riverbend would show the same pattern.” That sentence is true to the data. The headline is not.

🌎 WHERE YOU MIGHT SEE THIS: A FULL-ARTICLE CRITIQUE IN EVERYDAY LIFE
Stories about studies show up in news feeds, school newsletters, product pages, and group chats. Headlines are short, so they tend to squeeze a careful finding into a bold claim. Practices differ from one outlet and one field to another, and many reports are careful, so treat this as a habit of mind, not an accusation.
▶ Read the body before the headline. The number in the headline is often softer in the paragraphs below it.
▶ Look for the sample and the n. If you cannot find them, say so out loud: “the story does not tell me.”
▶ Match the verb to the design. “Linked to” and “associated with” are honest for observational work; “causes” needs an experiment.
▶ Ask for the size, not just “significant.” How big is the gap in the units of the thing measured?
▶ Look for what was not reported. How many things were checked, and who paid?
This is a service to the people around you. When a neighbor asks, “Should we buy this?”, you can say what the evidence supports, what it does not, and what would settle it.
⚖️ HONEST LIMITS OF A CRITIQUE
Finding flaws does not mean the claim is false. The StudyBuddy app might really help students. It might do nothing. It might help some students and not others. What the critique shows is narrower and more useful: the evidence in this article does not support the claim as it is written. A good reader keeps those two statements apart.
Two habits keep a critique fair. Say what the study does support (as in the summary above), and say what would settle it. For the app, a better design would randomly assign students to get the app or not, measure scores from school records, report how many students were in each group, and report the size of the gap with an interval. Critique the argument, not the person: a kind, exact sentence helps your neighbor more than a sneer.
💻 EXCEL (OPTIONAL): RECOMPUTE A MARGIN OF ERROR FROM A REPORT’S COUNTS, CLICK BY CLICK
You will recompute two margins of error from a report’s own counts. Everything here is made up. This is optional practice; you do not upload it.
▶ Open Excel and start a Blank workbook. Save it as Wk35_L2_YourName. Rename the first sheet Check (right-click the sheet tab at the bottom, choose Rename, type the name, press Enter).
▶ Headings. In A1 type Claim, in B1 p-hat, in C1 n, in D1 Safe rule, and in E1 Formula MOE.
▶ Row 2, the made-up Courier claim. In A2 type Stay organized, in B2 type 0.64, and in C2 type 11800 (the Lab’s fact sheet in this made-up story).
▶ In D2 type =1/SQRT(C2) and in E2 type =1.96*SQRT(B2*(1-B2)/C2). Select D2:E2, then choose Home > Number and the % button, and add decimals with Increase Decimal. You should see about 0.9% and 0.9%.
▶ Row 3, a made-up claim from a different report. In A3 type Support the plan, in B3 0.58, and in C3 120. Copy the formulas from D2:E2 down to row 3 (select D2:E2, drag the small square at the corner down one row). You should see about 9.1% and 8.8%.
▶ Compare. The first margin looks tiny, but the sample was self-selected, so the margin covers chance and nothing else. The second report claimed “give or take 2 points.” In F3 type =ROUNDUP((1/0.02)^2,0) to see how many replies a 2-point margin needs (2,500). Why is the report’s own claim not believable with 120 replies?
💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · FULL-TOOLKIT CHECKS: MARGIN OF ERROR, PERCENT CHANGE, AND INTERVALS
Excel. The safe-rule margin is =1/SQRT(n) and the formula margin is =1.96*SQRT(p*(1-p)/n). Interval ends are =p-moe and =p+moe. A percent change is =(new-old)/old formatted as a percent. A sample size for a target margin m (as a proportion) is =ROUNDUP((1/m)^2,0).
Desmos Scientific handles plain arithmetic only. For a margin from n = 120, press the square-root button on 120 (about 10.95), then enter 1 ÷ 10.95 to get about 0.091. For a percent change from 4.0 to 3.1, enter (3.1 − 4.0) ÷ 4.0 and read −0.225, a drop of 22.5%. For the bar-height ratio, enter 0.6 ÷ 0.2 (3) and compare it with 80.4 ÷ 80.0 (1.005).
🤖 AI TRAINING · CO-AUDIT A REAL ARTICLE WITH AN AI, THEN FIND THE FLAW IT MISSED (PROMPT → PREDICT → VERIFY)
Choose. This task uses a real article or a real statistic that you find yourself, from a news site, a school or community newsletter, or a product page, about a study or a survey (pick one your teacher or parent is comfortable with; this lesson names none). Copy the key paragraphs or write down the main claims and numbers.
Predict. Before you ask the AI anything, run the six questions yourself and write down every flaw or missing piece you find, with the question number. Include at least one thing you could not judge because the article does not say.
Prompt. Give the AI the same text and ask: “Audit this report. Who was studied and how many? How was it measured? What kind of study is it, and which verbs are allowed? Is any graph or number misleading? Does it report uncertainty and size? Who benefits or what is missing?”
Verify (the co-audit). Check every claim the AI makes against the article. Typical AI errors: (1) inventing details the article never gave (a sample size, a funder, a quote); (2) repeating the article’s causal verbs instead of questioning them; (3) a long list of generic cautions with no specific numbers; (4) declaring the claim “false” because of a flaw, which is not what a flaw shows. Mark which AI points were right, which were wrong, and which were unsupported. Then do the step AIs rarely do for you: find one flaw or missing question the AI missed, and write it with the evidence. Verdict. The AI is a second pair of eyes, not a judge; the critique is yours.
⚡ TODAY’S ACTIVITIES (about 40 minutes, plus the reading, the interactive, and the optional Excel check above)
1
Which Question Caught It?
10 min
1
Each line below is an excerpt from a different made-up report. For each, write the number (1 to 6) of the checklist question that catches the problem, and name the flaw in a few words. Some excerpts could fit two questions; choose the best and say why.
2
(a) “Visitors to our website were invited to rate the new library hours.” (b) “Students said how many minutes they read last night.” (c) “Neighborhoods with more trees have fewer car thefts, so planting trees stops thefts.” (d) “The bar for 31% is drawn three times as tall as the bar for 29%.” (e) “The new tutoring program is significant (p = 0.001).” The report never gives the size of the gain. (f) “The author sells the supplement the study praises.”
▶  Check
1. (a) Question 1, voluntary response: only visitors who chose to rate it. (b) Question 2, self-reported measurement: students may misremember minutes. (c) Question 3, a causal verb from observational data; neighborhoods with more trees may differ in income, lighting, and many other ways. (d) Question 4, a truncated axis or a distorted bar: a 2-point gap should not look three times as tall (zero start? no). (e) Question 5, significance without size: a tiny p-value can come with a tiny gain. (f) Question 6, who benefits: the author has a stake in the result. Possible double fits: (c) could also be Question 1 if the neighborhoods were not chosen at random, and (d) is both Question 4 and Question 5, but the best match is the one that names the actual problem.
2
Critique a Second Report (Independent Practice)
20 min
1
Read the second made-up report below, then critique it with the six questions. Find at least five flaws, name each by flaw and question number, and recompute at least two numbers from the report itself.
2
Made up. The Maple Ridge Gazette, Maple Ridge, the Maple Ridge Wellness Center, and every person and number are invented.
Walking Clubs Cut Sick Days in Half, Survey Finds
MAPLE RIDGE. Joining a walking club cuts sick days in half, according to a survey released by the Maple Ridge Wellness Center. The Center mailed its survey to 400 households chosen at random from the town directory. Only 120 households sent it back. Households with a member in a walking club reported an average of 3.1 sick days last year, against 4.0 for the others. “Walking clearly keeps people healthy,” said Dr. Ines Calloway, who directs the Center’s walking-club program. In the same survey, 58% of replies backed town money for more clubs, “give or take 2 points.” The Gazette’s chart draws one sneaker for each group, scaled so that the members’ sneaker is 0.775 times as tall and 0.775 times as wide as the other group’s. The Center said the survey results “confirm” its program works. “These numbers speak for themselves,” Calloway said.
3
Finish with a fair two-sentence summary of what the report does support, and one sentence naming evidence that would settle the question.
▶  Check
1. Flaws (six planted). Nonresponse (Question 1): only 120 of 400 households replied, a response rate of 120 ÷ 400 = 30%, and the 70% who did not reply may differ from those who did; the original random mailing does not protect a survey that most people ignore. Causal verb from observational data (Question 3): “cuts,” “keeps people healthy,” and “confirm” are not allowed because households chose whether to join; healthier or more active households may be the ones that join (reverse direction and confounding). Headline does not match the body (Question 6): “in half” would mean 2.0 days, but 3.1 against 4.0 is a drop of (4.0 − 3.1) ÷ 4.0 = 0.225, or 22.5%, not 50%. Pictograph scaling (Question 4): scaling the sneaker in both height and width makes its area (0.775)² = 0.60 of the other, so it looks about 40% smaller when the real gap is 22.5%. A margin of error that cannot be right (Question 5): with 120 replies the safe rule gives 1 ÷ √120 ≈ 9.1 points (the formula at 58% gives about 8.8 points), not 2; a 2-point margin would need about 2,500 replies. And even the right margin would cover chance only, not nonresponse. Who benefits (Question 6): the person quoted directs the program being evaluated, and the Center ran the survey. (A seventh point: neither sick-day average comes with an n for each group, an interval, or any significance information.) 2. Fair summary: “In the 120 households that replied, those with a walking-club member reported fewer sick days on average (3.1 against 4.0), about 22% fewer. Because only 30% replied and households chose whether to join, the survey cannot show that walking clubs reduce sick days.” Settling evidence: randomly assign willing households to join a club or wait, count sick days from records for everyone, and report both group sizes and an interval for the difference.
3
Rewrite It Fairly
10 min
1
Return to the Riverbend Courier article. Write (a) a new headline that the data actually support, and (b) one replacement sentence for “The app is clearly the reason Riverbend students are doing better.”
2
In two sentences, say what the Lab should have reported to serve Riverbend families honestly (think about the six questions).
3

▶  Check
1. (a) Example: “Survey: App Users Report Slightly Higher Scores, but Study Cannot Say Why.” (b) Example: “Students who chose to answer the survey and reported more app use also reported test scores about 0.4 points higher on average; the study cannot show whether the app is the reason.” 2. Example: the Lab should have said how many students answered and how they were chosen, how scores were checked, the size of the gap with an interval, that 20 outcomes were checked, and that the app’s maker paid for the work; a randomized comparison would let it say more. 3. Example: “Flaws mean the article does not prove the app works; they do not prove it is useless. The app may help, and a better-designed study could tell us.”
