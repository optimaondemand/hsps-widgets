🔗 WHEN EACH INDIVIDUAL HAS TWO NUMBERS
Until now every display has described one variable at a time. But some of the best questions in statistics involve two: Do students who study more score higher? Does a drink cool at a steady rate? Is a higher price tied to fewer sales? These questions need bivariate data — two variables recorded for each individual — and two new displays: the scatter plot and the line graph.
Bivariate data raises a new kind of question. With one variable we ask what is typical and what is spread out. With two we ask how are they related? Today you learn to see a relationship; Module 10 teaches you to measure and model it.
📐 EXPLANATORY AND RESPONSE VARIABLES
When we suspect that one variable helps explain another, we call the first the explanatory variable and the second the response variable. In “Do students who study more score higher?”, hours studied is the explanatory variable and the quiz score is the response. The explanatory variable goes on the horizontal axis and the response on the vertical axis — always, so that readers know which way to look.
A word of care, because it is this module's honesty at work: calling a variable explanatory is a statement about how we are asking the question. It does not claim that it causes the response. We return to that gap below.

How to describe a scatter plot. Use four words, in this order:
▶ Direction. Do the points rise from left to right (positive association), fall (negative association), or show no clear tilt?
▶ Form. Do they follow a straight line, a curve, or no shape?
▶ Strength. How tightly do they cling to that form — closely (strong), loosely (weak)?
▶ Unusual points. Is any point far from the pattern (an outlier), or are there clusters?
Here: the points rise from left to right, they follow a roughly straight line, and they cling to it fairly closely — a positive, roughly linear, fairly strong association, with no obvious outliers. (Week 28 gives “strong” a number.) Two other patterns for comparison:

⚠️ ASSOCIATION IS NOT CAUSATION
Seeing a pattern in a scatter plot shows that two variables are associated. It does not by itself show that one causes the other. In the study-hours plot it is very plausible that studying helps — but the plot alone cannot rule out a third factor, such as how motivated each student is. A famous classroom example: in a (made-up) town, monthly ice-cream sales and monthly swimming-pool visits rise and fall together. Neither causes the other; hot weather lifts both. Whenever you describe a relationship in this course, say “is associated with,” not “causes,” unless the data came from a carefully designed experiment (Module 11).
📈 THE LINE GRAPH
When the explanatory variable is time — the same thing measured again and again — we usually connect the points in time order. The result is a line graph, and its purpose is to show change. A cup of tea is measured every 2 minutes after it is poured (constructed values):

Tea Temperature After Pouring (constructed data)
Horizontal: minutes since poured · Vertical: temperature in °C
Reading it. The temperature falls the whole time, but not at a steady rate: it drops 12° in the first two minutes and only 3° in the last two (the drops are 12, 8, 6, 4, 3). The line is steep at first, then flattens — the shape of the cooling, which no single number would show.
Two rules for honest line graphs. First, connect points only when the order matters and in-between values make sense (time, distance). Do not connect the points of a scatter plot like the study-hours data — there is no “in between” student. Second, space the points by the true time gaps; Lesson 21.2 shows what goes wrong when they are not.
💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · A SHORT WEEK FOR CALCULATORS
Desmos Scientific does not draw scatter plots, so today's plots are drawn by hand on graph paper (or in the Excel lab in Lesson 21.3). Use Desmos for the arithmetic that describes them. The tea fell from 90° to 57° in 10 minutes: the average drop per minute is (57-90)/10 → −3.3. And the drop between two readings is a subtraction you can see: 78-90 → −12.

Excel will draw the plots for you in Lesson 21.3; the skill there is not the button-clicking but checking that the chart is honest.
🤖 AI TRAINING · PROMPT → PREDICT → VERIFY
Prompt. Paste the table of hours studied and quiz scores above into an AI tool and ask it to describe the pattern in the scatter plot: direction, form, strength, and any unusual points.
Predict. Sketch the plot first (or use the picture above) and write your own four-word description before you read the AI's.
Verify. Compare. Does the AI say positive? Does it say anything about form and strength? Look hard for the moment it slips from “is associated with” to “causes” or “leads to” — and mark it. A language tool cannot see the plot unless you give it the numbers, so ask it to say which numbers it used.

Plot and Describe
15 min
1
Eight summer days (constructed): temperature in °F and cups of lemonade sold — 60°F, 20 cups; 65°F, 28 cups; 70°F, 31 cups; 75°F, 45 cups; 80°F, 52 cups; 85°F, 60 cups; 90°F, 71 cups; 95°F, 75 cups. Identify the explanatory and response variables, then draw the scatter plot on graph paper with labeled axes.
2
Describe it in four words: direction, form, strength, and unusual points. Then write one sentence on why the plot alone does not prove that heat causes lemonade sales.
▶  Check
Explanatory: temperature (horizontal). Response: cups sold (vertical). The points rise left to right and follow a roughly straight line closely: positive, roughly linear, strong, no obvious outliers. The plot shows association; it cannot rule out other factors (a festival on the hottest days, for instance), and a lemonade stand is not an experiment.
2
Draw the Growth Line
15 min
1
A seedling's height in centimeters, measured weekly (constructed): week 0: 2, week 1: 4, week 2: 7, week 3: 11, week 4: 14, week 5: 16, week 6: 17. Draw the line graph with time on the horizontal axis.
2
Compute the growth in each week (week-to-week differences) and say, in one sentence, when the seedling grew fastest and when it slowed.
▶  Check
Weekly growth: 2, 3, 4, 3, 2, 1 centimeters. It grew fastest in week 3 (4 cm, from week 2 to week 3) and slowed after that, so the line is steepest in the middle and flatter at the end.
3
Which Display?
15 min
1
Choose scatter plot, line graph, or neither (and name a better display) for each: (a) hours of sleep and reaction time for 40 students; (b) the price of a stock each day for a month; (c) favorite music genre for 40 students; (d) the heights of 40 students; (e) a town's population every 10 years.
2
For (a), say which variable is explanatory and why you chose it.
▶  Check
(a) Scatter plot (two numerical variables, each individual measured on both). (b) Line graph (one quantity over time). (c) Neither: a bar chart (categorical). (d) Neither: a histogram or stem-and-leaf plot (one numerical variable). (e) Line graph (over time), with points spaced by the true 10-year gaps. For (a), sleep is the natural explanatory variable and reaction time the response; the choice reflects the question, not a claim of cause.
