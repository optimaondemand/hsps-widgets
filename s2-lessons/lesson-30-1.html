Lesson 30.1
Parameter or Statistic? Why We Sample
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
We cannot ask everyone, so what do we do with the answers we can get?
Population and sample; parameter and statistic; the symbols for a mean and a proportion; why we sample instead of counting everyone; when counting everyone is practical; why a statistic changes from sample to sample but a parameter does not
Reading & work: Read every section in order, work each example on paper before you read its solution, follow the Excel steps, and complete the three activities at the bottom. Plan on about 70 minutes.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to tell a population from a sample, and a parameter from a statistic, in a described situation, using the symbols μ and x for means and p and p̂ for proportions.
2
I will be able to explain why people sample instead of taking a census (cost, time, destructive testing, populations that cannot be fully reached, populations that keep changing), and say when a census is practical.
3
I will be able to decide whether a variable is categorical (summarize with a proportion) or numerical (summarize with a mean), and compute the matching statistic from a sample.
4
I will be able to explain that a statistic is a number we can compute but that changes from sample to sample, while a parameter is one fixed number that is usually unknown, and that a small or convenient sample still produces only a statistic.

✨ A WONDER TO START WITH
A cook lifts a spoon to her lips and tastes the soup. One spoonful, a tiny fraction of the pot, and she announces: “needs salt.” Nobody asks her to drink the whole pot first. We trust the spoonful because the soup has been stirred, so a spoonful is a fair stand-in for the pot.
Almost everything we want to know is like the pot, not the spoon. How long will the batteries a factory ships last? What share of the voters in a town want a new library? How many minutes of homework does a typical student in a school really do? We can never taste the whole pot. We taste a spoonful and then make a careful claim about the pot.
This module is about doing that responsibly: choosing the spoonful fairly, saying honestly what it shows, and never pretending it shows more. Today we begin with the vocabulary that keeps us honest: which numbers describe the pot, and which describe only the spoon.
A note on honesty. Every school, factory, town, club, and count in this lesson is constructed to teach the idea. None of it is real data about real people or products.
🏢 POPULATION AND SAMPLE
The population is the entire group we want to learn about: all the students in a school, all the batteries from one production run, all the registered voters in a town. A sample is the part of that population that we actually collect data from. A census is a sample that is the whole population: every individual is measured.
The people or things in a population are the individuals, and what we record about each one is a variable. Before you do anything else with a data set, answer two questions in words: who or what is the population? and who or what is the sample? Writing them down keeps you from claiming more than you measured.
The population is a choice we make. If a teacher wants to describe only her own 25 students, those 25 are the whole population for her question. If she wants to say something about every student in the school, those same 25 are only a sample. The question decides which is which.
📑 PARAMETER AND STATISTIC
A number that describes a population is a parameter. A number that describes a sample is a statistic. Statisticians use different symbols so that we never mix them up:
What it measuresParameter (the whole population)Statistic (the sample)
Mean of a numerical variableμ (mu, said “myoo”)x (x-bar)
Proportion for a categorical variablepp̂ (p-hat)
How many individuals(not needed today)n, the sample size
That is all the notation we need today. The pattern to remember: Greek letter or plain letter for the population, a letter with a mark over it for the sample.
Which summary fits which variable? The type of variable chooses the summary. A categorical variable puts individuals into groups (yes or no, bus or walk), so we summarize it with a proportion: the number in the group divided by the total. A numerical variable is a measurement (minutes, hours, dollars), so we summarize it with a mean: the sum divided by the count.
proportion = number in the category ÷ total number      mean = sum of the values ÷ number of values
The same formulas work for both worlds. Divide over the whole population and you get the parameter (μ or p). Divide over a sample and you get the statistic (x or p̂).
🍽️ WORKED EXAMPLE 1: THE SCHOOL CAFETERIA
Problem. A made-up school has 800 students. The cafeteria manager wants to know what share of all 800 students would buy from a new salad bar, and how much a student spends on lunch on an average day. On one Tuesday she asks the first 40 students who come through the lunch line. Of the 40, 26 say they would buy from the salad bar, and the 40 students spent a total of $174.00 on lunch. Identify the population, sample, variables, parameters, and statistics.
Teacher's voice: I start by naming the groups in words. The population is all 800 students at the school. The sample is the 40 students she asked. Next, the variables. “Would buy from the salad bar” is yes or no, so it is categorical, and the summary is a proportion. “Amount spent” is a dollar measurement, so it is numerical, and the summary is a mean.
▶ Statistic for the categorical variable. p̂ = 26 ÷ 40 = 0.65, or 65%.
▶ Statistic for the numerical variable. x = $174.00 ÷ 40 = $4.35.
▶ Parameters. The true share p of all 800 students who would buy from the salad bar, and the true mean μ spent by all 800 students. Neither is known. We have not asked them all.
Responsibility check. Students who eat in the lunch line are not all the students: someone who brings lunch or skips it never walked through that line. So p̂ = 65% and x = $4.35 are honest numbers about these 40 students, and only an estimate of p and μ. How well they estimate is the question the rest of this module answers. Notice what we did not write: “65% of the school would buy from the salad bar.” We do not know that.
🔋 WORKED EXAMPLE 2: A BATTERY FACTORY AND TESTING TO DESTRUCTION
Problem. A made-up factory finishes one production run of 12,000 batteries. The company wants to say how many hours the batteries last in a standard device. A battery's lifetime can be measured only by running it until it is dead. The quality team tests 10 batteries and records these lifetimes in hours: 38, 41, 44, 40, 39, 43, 42, 37, 45, 41. Why not test them all, and what are the parameter and the statistic?
Teacher's voice: If we tested every one of the 12,000 batteries, we would use up every battery we made, and there would be nothing left to sell. A measurement that destroys the thing measured is called destructive testing, and it makes a census pointless. We must sample.
▶ Population: all 12,000 batteries in the run. Sample: the 10 tested. Variable: lifetime in hours, which is numerical, so the summary is a mean.
▶ Statistic. Add the lifetimes: 38 + 41 + 44 + 40 + 39 + 43 + 42 + 37 + 45 + 41 = 410. Then x = 410 ÷ 10 = 41.0 hours.
▶ Parameter. μ, the mean lifetime of all 12,000 batteries. It is one fixed number, but we will never know it exactly, because finding it would require destroying the whole run.
A statistic changes. Suppose the team tests a second 10 batteries from the same run and gets 42, 40, 38, 44, 41, 39, 43, 40, 42, 38. Their sum is 407, so this sample has x = 407 ÷ 10 = 40.7 hours. Same run, same population, same μ, and two different statistics: 41.0 and 40.7. Neither is “wrong.” Each is the mean of the batteries that happened to be tested. That is what we mean by sample-to-sample variation.
🗳️ WORKED EXAMPLE 3: A TOWN POLL
Problem. A made-up town has 6,000 registered voters. A newspaper phones 150 of them and asks, “Do you favor building a new library?” Of the 150, 87 say yes. (a) Name the population, the sample, and the type of variable. (b) Find the statistic. (c) Suppose the town later counts a mail-in ballot from all 6,000 voters and 3,300 say yes. What is the parameter, and how does it compare?
Teacher's voice: The population is all 6,000 registered voters; the sample is the 150 who were phoned. The answer is yes or no, so the variable is categorical and the summary is a proportion.
▶ (a) Population: 6,000 voters. Sample: 150 voters. Variable: categorical.
▶ (b) p̂ = 87 ÷ 150 = 0.58, or 58%.
▶ (c) Once every voter is counted, the population proportion is known: p = 3,300 ÷ 6,000 = 0.55, or 55%. The poll's statistic (p̂ = 0.58) was close, but it was not equal to the parameter (p = 0.55). Before the full count, nobody could have known how far off it was.
The lesson inside the example. The parameter is fixed the whole time. It was 55% before the poll, during the poll, and after. What changed is what we knew. Statistics are how we learn about a parameter we cannot count directly. That idea, using statistics to learn about parameters, is called inference, and it is where this course is heading.
🔍 WHY SAMPLE AT ALL? AND WHEN IS A CENSUS FINE?
If a census gives the exact parameter, why does anyone settle for a sample? There are good reasons, and each shows up in the examples above.
▶ Cost. Interviewing or measuring every individual in a large population can cost far more than a careful sample.
▶ Time. A decision may be needed this week, but a full count takes months.
▶ Destructive testing. If measuring an individual uses it up (the batteries, a crash test, a strength test on a bridge cable sample), testing everything leaves nothing to use.
▶ A population that cannot be fully reached. You cannot list or contact every fish in a lake, every person who might read a website, or everyone who will ever visit a park.
▶ A population that keeps changing. The set of people shopping in a store, or the cars on a road, is different by the time you finish counting. A full count would describe a moment that is already gone.
A census is the right tool when the population is small and reachable and measuring is cheap and harmless. The 20 members of a small club, the 25 students in one class, every lunch recorded by the cafeteria register today: counting all of them is easy, and then the number you compute is a parameter, with no estimating needed. Choosing a sample when a census is easy would just throw away information.
🔁 A STATISTIC VARIES; A PARAMETER STAYS PUT
This is the single most important idea in the lesson, so it is worth saying slowly.
▶ A parameter (μ or p) is one fixed number describing the whole population. It is usually unknown, because we did not measure everyone.
▶ A statistic (x or p̂) is a number we can compute, because we have the sample in hand. But a different sample from the same population would give a different statistic.
So a statistic is a clue, not an answer. It is evidence about a parameter, and later lessons will show how to say how good that evidence is. For now, hold on to this: we calculate statistics so that we can say something careful about parameters.
⚖️ RESPONSIBILITY: A SMALL OR CONVENIENT SAMPLE IS STILL ONLY A STATISTIC
Suppose a student asks the five friends sitting nearest to her whether they like the new lunch menu, and four say yes. She now has a number, 4 out of 5, or 80%. That number is a perfectly good statistic. It is not the share of the school that likes the menu. It is only the share of those five friends.
Two things are tempting and both are wrong. The first is to treat a statistic as if it were the parameter, because it came out of a careful-looking calculation. The second is to think that the number becomes more trustworthy simply because it is precise (80.0%) or because the sample is large. Even a large sample is only a statistic, and a sample taken the easy way (the five people next to you, the first people through a door) may lean in a particular direction. Calculating carefully does not repair a sample that was gathered carelessly.
The responsible sentence is: “Of the five friends I asked, four like the menu.” Claiming more than that requires a better way of choosing the sample, which is the work of Lesson 30.2.
❌ A COMMON ERROR: “THE AVERAGE OF THE POPULATION”
Read this sentence from a made-up student report: “I measured 30 students and the mean was 6.8 hours of sleep, so the average sleep of the population is 6.8.” What is wrong?
The number 6.8 is the sample mean x, a statistic. It is the average of the 30 students measured. It is not the average of the population, which is μ. The two can be close, and the sample mean is a sensible estimate of μ, but they are different numbers with different jobs, and in general they are not equal.
A repaired sentence: “In my sample of 30 students, x = 6.8 hours. That is my estimate of μ, the mean for all students, which I do not know.”

🌎 WHERE PEOPLE USE THIS: POLLS AND QUALITY CHECKS
Newspapers and polling organizations typically report a percent from a sample of voters, which is a statistic, in order to estimate a parameter, the percent of the whole population that holds the view. Manufacturers do much the same when they test a few items from a batch. In each case, how the sample was chosen matters as much as the number itself, and Lesson 30.2 shows why.
💻 EXCEL: A POPULATION, A SAMPLE, AND THE COMPARISON, CLICK BY CLICK
You will build a small population of 20 values, compute its parameters with formulas, then compare them with statistics from a sample of 5 that you choose. All data are made up.
▶ Open Excel and start a Blank workbook. Save it as Wk30_L1_YourName (practice; you do not upload it).
▶ In A1 to C1 type the headings Member, Minutes, Bus, pressing Tab after each. In rows 2 to 21 enter members 1 to 20 in column A, the homework minutes in column B, and Yes or No in column C (does the member ride the bus?), exactly as in the table below.

▶ The parameters (the whole club). In E1 type Mean of all 20. In F1 type =AVERAGE(B2:B21) and press Enter. It gives 47.5: this is μ. In E2 type Share who ride the bus and in F2 type =COUNTIF(C2:C21,"Yes")/ROWS(C2:C21). It gives 0.4: this is p. (COUNTIF counts the Yes entries; ROWS counts how many rows are in the range.)
▶ A sample you choose. Pick 5 members from the list yourself (for example, members 3, 7, 12, 15, 19). Copy those five rows of minutes and bus answers into H2:I6 (type the headings Minutes and Bus in H1 and I1).
▶ The statistics. In K1 type =AVERAGE(H2:H6) for x and in K2 type =COUNTIF(I2:I6,"Yes")/ROWS(I2:I6) for p̂. For the example members 3, 7, 12, 15, 19 you should get 53.0 and 0.6.
▶ Compare. Put a different set of 5 members into H2:I6 (change the values in place). Watch K1 and K2 change while F1 and F2 do not. The statistics move with the sample; the parameters stay where they are.
Be honest about this sample. You picked these five members by hand, which is a quick way to see the idea and not a fair way to choose a sample. Lesson 30.2 shows how to choose one that is.
💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · AVERAGE, COUNTIF AND ROWS IN EXCEL; PLAIN ARITHMETIC IN DESMOS
Excel counts and averages for you. For a numerical variable, =AVERAGE(range) returns the mean. For a categorical variable, =COUNTIF(range,"Yes")/ROWS(range) returns the proportion of Yes entries: COUNTIF counts the matching cells and ROWS counts all the cells in the range. Use the whole list to get a parameter and the sample rows to get a statistic; the formula is the same, only the range changes.

Desmos Scientific does plain arithmetic, so type the sum and divide. For the battery example, type (38+41+44+40+39+43+42+37+45+41)/10 to get 41. For the poll, type 87/150 to get 0.58 and 87/150*100 to get 58. For the cafeteria, type 174/40 to get 4.35.
🤖 AI TRAINING · QUIZ AN AI ON FIVE CASES AND GRADE IT (PROMPT → PREDICT → VERIFY)
Task: quiz an AI on five cases, and grade it.
Prompt. Give an AI tool these five cases, one per line, and ask: “For each one, say whether the number is a parameter or a statistic, name the population, and explain in one sentence. Do not skip any.”
  1. The mean homework time of all 20 members of a small club, with every member measured.
  2. In a made-up poll of 150 voters from a town of 6,000, 58% favor a new library.
  3. The true percent of all batteries in one production run that last more than 40 hours.
  4. The mean lifetime of 10 batteries tested to failure from a run: 41.0 hours.
  5. A teacher wants the mean number of absences for her own class of 25 students, and she uses the attendance record for all 25.
Predict. Before you read the AI's answers, write your own answer key for all five: parameter or statistic, and the population. Then read the AI's answers and mark each as matching or not matching your key.
Verify. For every miss, decide who is right by asking what group the number describes and whether every individual in that group was measured. Flag any AI answer that is right but for a weak reason (for example, “it is a statistic because it has a decimal”). Count how many of five the AI got right, and write one sentence about the kind of case it found hardest.
Check against an answer key (only after you have written your own)
1. Parameter. The club is the whole population and a census was taken.
2. Statistic. It describes the 150 sampled voters.
3. Parameter. It describes the entire run, even though no one can know it without destroying the batteries.
4. Statistic. Ten batteries is a sample.
5. Parameter, because her own 25 students are the whole population for her question. (If she wanted to describe the whole school, the same number would be a statistic.)
Case 5 is the twist: an AI may call it a statistic automatically because it is “a class,” or call it a parameter without checking the group of interest. The correct reasoning depends on the stated goal.
⚡ TODAY'S ACTIVITIES (about 35 minutes, plus the Excel steps and the AI task above)
1
Name Everything
12 min
1
For each made-up situation, write the population, the sample, the variable, whether it is categorical or numerical, and the statistic with its symbol. (a) A school newspaper asks 50 of the school's 600 students how many hours they slept last night; the 50 answers total 360 hours. (b) A greenhouse plants 500 seedlings; a worker checks 40 of them and finds that 31 have sprouted. (c) A teacher computes the mean of all 28 quiz scores in her class and gets 81.
2
For (c), is 81 a parameter or a statistic? Explain what question decides your answer.
▶  Check
(a) Population: 600 students. Sample: 50 students. Variable: hours slept, numerical. x = 360 ÷ 50 = 7.2 hours (a mean). (b) Population: 500 seedlings. Sample: 40 seedlings. Variable: sprouted or not, categorical. p̂ = 31 ÷ 40 = 0.775, or 77.5% (a proportion). (c) Population: the 28 students in the class; every score was used. 81 is a mean, and it is a parameter (μ = 81) if the question is about this class only. It would be a statistic only if she were using these 28 scores to say something about a larger group. The question decides.
2
Same Club, Different Samples
12 min
1
Use the 20 homework times from the Excel table (member 1 to member 20 in order). Sample A is members 1, 5, 9, 13, 17, and Sample B is members 2, 6, 10, 14, 18. Find the mean minutes for each sample by hand or in Desmos.
2
Compare both means with the population mean μ = 47.5. Which sample statistic is closer? Is either equal to μ?
3
Use the Bus column to find p̂ (the share who ride the bus) for each sample, and compare with p = 0.4. In two sentences, say what this shows about statistics versus parameters.
▶  Check
Sample A: the minutes are 35, 45, 25, 40, 30, with sum 175, so x = 175 ÷ 5 = 35.0. Sample B: 50, 30, 70, 35, 45, with sum 230, so x = 230 ÷ 5 = 46.0. Sample B is closer to 47.5 (off by 1.5, compared with 12.5). Neither equals the parameter. Bus riders: Sample A has members 1 and 17, so p̂ = 2 ÷ 5 = 0.4; Sample B has only member 14, so p̂ = 1 ÷ 5 = 0.2. The parameter p = 8 ÷ 20 = 0.4 does not move, but the two statistics differ from each other (and one happens to match p by luck). A statistic changes from sample to sample; the parameter stays fixed. Samples of only 5 are very changeable, so none of these should be trusted much.
3
Error Analysis: Repair the Claim
11 min
1
A classmate writes: “I tested 8 batteries and the mean was 41.0 hours, so the average life of all the batteries is 41.0 hours.” Find the mistake and rewrite the sentence correctly, using the symbols x and μ.
2
Another classmate says: “The poll asked 1,000 people and 52% said yes. Because 1,000 is so large, 52% is the parameter.” Find the mistake and rewrite the sentence.
3
In one sentence, explain why asking all 20 members of a small club for their homework time would be a reasonable census, but testing all 12,000 batteries would not.
▶  Check
1. The classmate treated a statistic as the parameter. Repair: “In my sample of 8 batteries, x = 41.0 hours. That is my estimate of μ, the mean life of all the batteries, which I do not know.” 2. A sample is a statistic no matter how large it is. Repair: “In my sample of 1,000 people, p̂ = 0.52, my estimate of the population proportion p.” (A bigger sample usually helps, but it never turns a statistic into the parameter.) 3. The club is small, reachable, and measuring minutes harms nothing, so counting everyone is easy and gives a parameter. Testing a battery to the end of its life uses it up, so a census of the batteries would leave none to sell.
