📝 WHEN THE DATA ARE NUMBERS
Last week you displayed categorical data: counts in groups. This week we turn to numerical data, where the values themselves are measurements or counts and the spread of those values is the story. Where are most of the values? Are there gaps? Is there one value far from the rest? A good display answers those questions at a glance — and, because this module's virtue is honesty, it does so without hiding anything.
Today's two displays are the simplest in the family, and both have a pleasant property: they keep every individual value visible. A line plot stacks a mark above a number line; a stem-and-leaf plot uses the digits of the numbers themselves as the bars. Both are best for small datasets.
✖ THE LINE PLOT
A line plot (also called a dot plot) draws a number line and puts one mark — an × or a dot — above the line for every observation. Equal values stack up. It suits discrete numerical data with a modest number of distinct values. Here are the quiz scores (out of 10) of 20 students — made-up data for teaching:

Reading it. The scores pile up around 8; the single × marks at 4 and 5 form a tail running to the left. We call a shape like this skewed left: the longer tail points toward the smaller values. The most common score (the mode) is 8, and the median is also 8, because the 10th and 11th of the ordered scores are both 8.
🌿 THE STEM-AND-LEAF PLOT
The line plot works when the same value repeats. With measurements that rarely repeat — minutes, heights, prices — a better idea is to split each number into a stem (all digits but the last) and a leaf (the last digit). The stems go down a column; each value's leaf is written beside its stem. Turn your head sideways and the rows become bars — but unlike bars, you can read every original value back.
Worked example. Twenty-two students record their walking time to school, in minutes (constructed): 6, 8, 9, 12, 13, 15, 15, 17, 18, 19, 21, 22, 24, 24, 27, 28, 31, 33, 35, 38, 41, 52. Teacher's voice: these run from 6 to 52, so the stems are the tens digits 0 through 5, and the leaves are the ones digits. I put every value in once, then I order the leaves in each row, then I write the key.
0 | 6 8 91 | 2 3 5 5 7 8 92 | 1 2 4 4 7 83 | 1 3 5 84 | 15 | 2
Key: 2 | 4 means 24 minutes. n = 22.
Three habits make a stem-and-leaf plot honest: (1) a key, so the reader knows what “2 | 4” means; (2) ordered leaves in each row, so you can read off the median; (3) every stem shown, even a stem with no leaves, so that gaps stay visible. Repeated values get repeated leaves — the two 15s appear as 5 5, the two 24s as 4 4.
Reading it. Most students walk 10 to 40 minutes. The single leaf 2 on stem 5 (52 minutes) stands well apart from the rest — a possible outlier, a value unusually far from the others. The median is the middle of 22 ordered values: the average of the 11th and 12th, which are 21 and 22, so the median is 21.5 minutes.
🌱 DECIMALS, AND THE TWO-SENTENCE RULE
Leaves may be the tenths digit. Fifteen seedlings are measured in inches (constructed): 3.2, 3.8, 4.1, 4.4, 4.7, 5.0, 5.1, 5.3, 5.6, 5.9, 6.2, 6.4, 6.8, 7.1, 9.4. The stem is the whole-inch part, the leaf the tenths digit:
3 | 2 84 | 1 4 75 | 0 1 3 6 96 | 2 4 87 | 18 | 9 | 4
Key: 5 | 3 means 5.3 inches. n = 15. Note the empty stem 8.
The empty stem 8 is deliberate: it shows that nothing lay between 7.1 and 9.4, which makes the 9.4 seedling jump out. Whenever you describe a display, say two things: where the data are centered or concentrated, and anything unusual — gaps, outliers, a long tail. Here: “Most seedlings are between 3 and 7 inches, centered near 5.3 inches; one seedling at 9.4 inches stands well apart from the rest.”
⚖️ STRENGTHS AND LIMITS
▶ Both keep every value. You can recover the original data from the plot — something a bar chart or histogram cannot offer.
▶ Both are for small datasets — roughly under 50 values. With 400 values a stem-and-leaf plot becomes a wall of digits and a line plot runs off the page.
▶ A line plot needs repeating values (discrete data, or measurements rounded to whole numbers). For measurements like 5.3 and 6.8 that almost never repeat, use a stem-and-leaf plot, or in the next lesson, a histogram.
Tomorrow's histogram trades away the individual values to handle bigger data. Keep today's two in your toolbox for the small cases — they are the most transparent displays there are.

💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · EXCEL: COUNTIF BUILDS THE FREQUENCY TABLE
You made frequency tables by hand with a tally. Excel counts for you with COUNTIF, which counts the cells in a range that match a condition. Here is the whole recipe for the quiz scores.

1. Open a Blank workbook. In cell A1 type Score. In A2:A21 type the 20 scores, one per cell: 7, 6, 7, 7, 9, 9, 6, 8, 4, 5, 8, 8, 10, 8, 9, 8, 6, 9, 10, 7.
2. In D1 type Value and in E1 type Frequency. In D2:D8 type the values 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, one per cell — every value in the range, including any that do not occur.
3. Click cell E2 and type =COUNTIF($A$2:$A$21,D2), then press Enter. It should show 1. The dollar signs lock the data range; D2 is free to change as you copy down.
4. Click E2 again, point at the small square at its lower-right corner until the pointer becomes a thin +, and drag down to E8. Each cell now counts the matching score in column D.
5. In E9 type =SUM(E2:E8) — the total check. It must say 20. As a second check, =COUNT(A2:A21) counts how many numbers you typed. If the two disagree, a score is missing from column D or mistyped in column A.

Your frequencies should read 1, 1, 3, 4, 5, 4, 2. From these you can draw the line plot by hand, one × per count. In Desmos, relative frequencies stay one-line entries: 5/20 → 0.25 for the share of students who scored 8.
🤖 AI TRAINING · PROMPT → PREDICT → VERIFY
Prompt. Give an AI tool this stem-and-leaf plot as text and ask it to list every data value, then the minimum, maximum, and median: Key: 2 | 4 means 24. Stem 1: 2 3 5 5 7. Stem 2: 1 4 4 8. Stem 3: 0 6.
Predict. Read it yourself first. Values: 12, 13, 15, 15, 17, 21, 24, 24, 28, 30, 36 — eleven values. Minimum 12, maximum 36, median (the 6th value) 21.
Verify. Compare the AI's list with yours, value by value. Did it count the repeated leaves (the two 15s, the two 24s)? Did it say 11 values? A language tool can misread a plot written as text, especially repeated leaves; do not take its median on trust — count to the middle yourself.

▶  Check your plot
Frequencies: 0 days: 5, 1: 6, 2: 3, 3: 2, 4: 1, 5: 0, 6: 1 — total 18. The center is around 1 day. Unusual: nobody missed exactly 5 days, and the single student with 6 days is separated from the rest by a gap. The shape is skewed right: a longer tail toward larger values.
2
Grow the Seedling Plot
15 min
1
Make a stem-and-leaf plot for these twelve heights in centimeters (constructed): 14.2, 15.8, 16.1, 16.4, 17.0, 17.3, 17.9, 18.2, 18.5, 19.4, 20.1, 23.6. Use stems of whole centimeters and write a key.
2
Find the median from the plot, and say what is unusual.
▶  Check
Stems 14 through 23 — including the empty stems 21 and 22. Row 17 reads 0 3 9. Key: 17 | 3 means 17.3 cm. The median of 12 values is the average of the 6th and 7th, 17.3 and 17.9, which is 17.6 cm. The 23.6 stands apart after a gap (empty stems 21 and 22).
3
Find the Flaws
15 min
1
A classmate shows this stem-and-leaf plot of bike-trip lengths in kilometers: 1 | 5 2 8; 2 | 4 1; 4 | 0 3. There is no key. List every problem and write a repaired version, assuming the trip lengths were 12, 15, 18, 21, 24, 40, and 43.
2
In one or two sentences, explain to a partner why a stem-and-leaf plot is a poor choice for 400 trip lengths.
▶  Check
Problems: no key; leaves not in order (5 2 8 should be 2 5 8; 4 1 should be 1 4); stem 3 is skipped, which hides the gap between 24 and 40. Repaired: 1 | 2 5 8; 2 | 1 4; 3 | (empty); 4 | 0 3, with the key 1 | 2 means 12 km. For 400 values the plot would run to many hundreds of digits — it stops summarizing and just reprints the data.
