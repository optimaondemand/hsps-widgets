✨ A WONDER TO START WITH
Pour out a thousand grains of sand and weigh each one. Measure the heights of a thousand people. Time how long a thousand identical machines take to fill a thousand identical boxes. The things being measured have nothing in common, and yet when you stack the results into a histogram, something odd keeps happening: the stack leans toward a middle value and falls away on both sides in nearly the same way, left and right. A bell.
Nobody told the grains of sand to do that. Each measurement is the sum of many small, independent pushes, a little too heavy here, a little too light there, and when enough small pushes are added up, they tend to form the same shape. Order out of randomness: this is the wonder of the module you are beginning. This week you will learn to describe the bell exactly, with two numbers, and then to measure any value against it with a single new number called a z-score.
A note on honesty. Every dataset and scenario in this lesson is constructed (made up) to teach the ideas. And a warning that matters all week: plenty of real data are not bell-shaped. The normal model is a tool that fits some data well and other data badly, and a careful statistician checks before using it. You will practice that check in Week 26.
🔔 THE NORMAL MODEL
A normal distribution is a particular bell-shaped curve. It is a model: a smooth idealization of what a large collection of measurements might look like. It has these features:
▶ It is symmetric about its center. The left half is the mirror image of the right half.
▶ It is unimodal: one peak, exactly at the center. The mean, the median, and the mode all sit at that same point.
▶ The curve is highest in the middle and falls away toward both tails, getting closer and closer to the horizontal axis without ever touching it. Values far from the center are possible but increasingly rare.
▶ It is completely determined by two numbers: the mean (written μ, the Greek letter mu) fixes where the center is, and the standard deviation (written σ, sigma) fixes how spread out the bell is. Change the mean and the whole bell slides left or right. Change the SD and the bell gets narrower and taller, or wider and flatter. Nothing else about the curve is free to change.
We write X ~ N(μ, σ) and read it “X follows a normal distribution with mean μ and standard deviation σ.” For example, X ~ N(75, 10) is a bell centered at 75 with an SD of 10 points. (For a data sample you have used x̄ and s; here we describe a model, so μ and σ appear.)
Two bells, one family. A bell of quiz scores N(75, 10) and a bell of cereal-box weights in grams N(500, 4) have different centers, different units, and different widths. But they have exactly the same shape. The next idea explains how to see that.
📏 THE z-SCORE: A RULER MEASURED IN STANDARD DEVIATIONS
Suppose a quiz has mean 75 and SD 10. A student scores 90. Is that high? You can say it is 15 points above the mean. But “15 points” means different things on different tests: on a test with an SD of 3, 15 points would be extraordinary, and on a test with an SD of 30 it would be ordinary. A better question is: how many standard deviations above the mean is it? Divide the distance by the SD: 15 ÷ 10 = 1.5. That number is the z-score.
z = ( x − μ ) ÷ σ     (value minus mean, divided by SD)
Read the formula as a sentence: start from the value, subtract the mean to get the distance from center (in the data's units), then divide by the SD to convert that distance into “number of standard deviations.” Three facts to hold on to:
▶ A positive z means the value is above the mean; a negative z means below; z = 0 means exactly at the mean.
▶ The z-score has no units. The units of the distance and of the SD cancel. That is why z-scores from different measurements can be compared.
▶ The size of z tells you how far out you are. |z| near 0 is typical; |z| near 1 is a little way out; |z| beyond 2 is quite far out; beyond 3 is far out indeed. (Lesson 25.2 will put percentages on these.)
Why does this work? Subtracting the mean slides the bell so its center lands on 0. Dividing by the SD stretches or squeezes the bell so one SD becomes one unit. What is left is a bell centered at 0 with SD 1, no matter which normal curve you started with. That curve is the standard normal distribution, written Z ~ N(0, 1). Every normal curve can be rescaled to it, which means one single table (or one Excel function) is enough to answer questions about every normal distribution. You will use that fact all week.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 1: FROM A VALUE TO A z-SCORE
Problem. On a constructed quiz, scores follow N(75, 10). Find the z-score of a score of 90, and of a score of 62. Interpret each.
Teacher's voice: I am asked how many SDs from the mean each score is. So for each, I find the distance from the mean first, then divide by the SD of 10.
▶ Score 90. z = (90 − 75) ÷ 10 = 15 ÷ 10 = 1.5.
▶ Score 62. z = (62 − 75) ÷ 10 = −13 ÷ 10 = −1.3.
▶ Interpret. A 90 is 1.5 standard deviations above the mean. A 62 is 1.3 standard deviations below the mean. The negative sign is not a mistake; it records the direction.
▶ Sense check. 90 is above 75 so z must be positive; 62 is below 75 so z must be negative. Both signs agree.
Desmos check. Type (90-75)/10 and you get 1.5. Type (62-75)/10 and you get −1.3.
🔄 WORKED EXAMPLE 2: WORKING BACKWARD FROM z TO THE VALUE
Sometimes you know the z-score and want the value. Solve the formula for x: x = μ + z · σ (start at the mean, then move z standard deviations).
Problem. Constructed heights follow N(170, 8) centimeters. What height has z = −1.25? What height has z = 2.5?
Teacher's voice: One SD is 8 cm. A z of −1.25 means 1.25 of those steps below the center.
▶ z = −1.25. x = 170 + (−1.25)(8) = 170 − 10 = 160 cm.
▶ z = 2.5. x = 170 + (2.5)(8) = 170 + 20 = 190 cm.
▶ Check by going forward. (160 − 170) ÷ 8 = −10 ÷ 8 = −1.25 ✓. (190 − 170) ÷ 8 = 20 ÷ 8 = 2.5 ✓.
Always check an answer by running it forward through the original formula. It takes ten seconds and catches sign errors.
⚖️ WORKED EXAMPLE 3: COMPARING VALUES FROM DIFFERENT DISTRIBUTIONS
Problem. Lena scores 78 on a constructed Test A, where scores have mean 70 and SD 4. Marcus scores 85 on a constructed Test B, where scores have mean 80 and SD 10. Marcus has the higher raw score. Who did better relative to their own class?
Teacher's voice: The raw scores live on different tests, so I cannot compare them directly. A z-score measures each student against their own test's mean and spread, so I will convert both.
▶ Lena. z = (78 − 70) ÷ 4 = 8 ÷ 4 = 2.0.
▶ Marcus. z = (85 − 80) ÷ 10 = 5 ÷ 10 = 0.5.
▶ Compare. Lena is 2.0 SDs above her test's mean. Marcus is only 0.5 SDs above his. Relative to their own groups, Lena did far better, even though her raw score is 7 points lower.
▶ Why? Test A's scores are tightly bunched (SD 4), so 8 points above average is a long way out. Test B's are spread out (SD 10), so 5 points above average is barely noticeable.
This is the great use of z-scores: they put values from different measuring sticks on one measuring stick.
🔍 WORKED EXAMPLE 4: IS THIS VALUE UNUSUAL?
Problem. A constructed filling machine puts cereal in boxes with mean 500 g and SD 4 g. One box weighs 489 g. Is that unusual?
▶ z = (489 − 500) ÷ 4 = −11 ÷ 4 = −2.75.
▶ Interpret. The box is 2.75 standard deviations below the mean. If the weights follow this normal model, a value that far out is rare. As a working rule of thumb, a |z| larger than about 2 is worth a second look, and larger than 3 is very unusual. (Exact percentages come in Lessons 25.2 and 25.3. For the curious, the area below z = −2.75 is about 0.003, three boxes in a thousand.)
▶ But “unusual” is a question, not a verdict. It might be a rare random fluctuation, a machine that has drifted, or a mis-weighed box. The z-score tells you how far out; it does not tell you why.
⚠️ WHAT z-SCORES DO NOT DO
A z-score re-expresses a value on a standard scale. It does not change the shape of the data. If you convert a right-skewed dataset to z-scores, you get a right-skewed dataset of z-scores: same shape, new labels. Computing z-scores does not make data normal. Only if the data were roughly bell-shaped to begin with does the standard normal curve describe them, and that is a separate thing to check.
What is always true is that the z-scores of any dataset have mean 0 and SD 1 (when computed with that dataset's own mean and SD). What is true only for normal data is that they follow the standard normal curve, with its familiar areas.

📜 A MOMENT IN THE HISTORY OF MATHEMATICS · DE MOIVRE, GAUSS, AND THE “CURVE OF ERRORS”
The bell curve was found twice, by people asking different questions. Abraham de Moivre, a French-born mathematician working in London, was interested in the chances of games. If you toss a coin many times, the number of heads follows the binomial pattern you studied in Semester 1, and for large numbers the exact arithmetic becomes brutal. In 1733 de Moivre derived a result (circulated privately in Latin) showing that the binomial pattern approaches a smooth, symmetric bell shape as the number of trials grows. He later included it in The Doctrine of Chances, in its 1738 edition. Then, in the early 1800s, Carl Friedrich Gauss used the same curve for a different purpose: the small errors that creep into astronomical measurements. In his 1809 work on the motion of heavenly bodies he treated those errors as following the bell, and the curve became widely known as the “curve of errors” or the “Gaussian.” The name “normal” came later, around the nineteenth century, and the older names survive in many books. Notice what the two stories share: a gambler's coin tosses and an astronomer's measurement errors are each made of many small pushes, and many small pushes added together make the same shape. That is the wonder this module keeps returning to: order that grows out of randomness. This is a short sketch; historians add many details beyond it.
💻 EXCEL: z-SCORES WITH STANDARDIZE, CLICK BY CLICK
You will compute the z-scores of five test scores (constructed) from a test with mean 70 and SD 4. Keep this page open beside Excel.
▶ Open Excel and start a Blank workbook. (This one is practice; you do not upload it. If you keep it, save it as Wk25_L1_YourName.)
▶ Click cell A1, type Mean, press Enter. Click B1, type 70, Enter.
▶ Click A2, type SD, Enter. Click B2, type 4, Enter.
▶ Click A4, type Score, Enter. Click B4, type z (STANDARDIZE), Enter. Click C4, type z (by formula), Enter.
▶ Starting in A5, type the five scores, one per cell, pressing Enter after each: 78, 70, 66, 75, 82. They fill A5 through A9.
▶ Click B5 and type =STANDARDIZE(A5,$B$1,$B$2), then press Enter. You should see 2. The three inputs are the value, the mean, and the standard deviation, in that order. The dollar signs lock the mean and SD cells so they do not move when you copy the formula down.
▶ Click C5 and type =(A5-$B$1)/$B$2, Enter. You should see 2 again. This is the same formula you wrote by hand, which is why the two columns must always agree.
▶ Click B5, hold Shift and click C5 to select both. Then drag the small square in the bottom-right corner of the selection (the fill handle) down to row 9. Both formulas copy down.
▶ Check against the hand work. The five z-scores should be 2, 0, −1, 1.25, and 3. If a value is wrong, make sure the dollar signs are in place and the score cells are correct.
Honest note. Menu names and the look of the window differ a little between desktop Excel and Excel for the web, but typing a formula in a cell works the same in both. If you see a #NAME? error, check the spelling, and if you see #DIV/0!, check that the SD cell is not blank or zero.
💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · z-SCORES IN DESMOS AND EXCEL
Desmos Scientific does plain arithmetic, so type the formula the way you write it, with the subtraction in its own parentheses so the division applies to the whole difference. For Lena's score, type (78-70)/4 and you get 2. For Marcus, type (85-80)/10 and you get 0.5. For a value below the mean, type (62-75)/10 and you get −1.3. To go backward (z to value), type the arithmetic of x = μ + z · σ: for the height with z = −1.25 in N(170, 8), type 170+(-1.25)*8 and you get 160. A common slip is to leave out the parentheses and type 78-70/4, which follows the order of operations and does 70 ÷ 4 first, giving 60.5 instead of 2.

Excel does the list work: =STANDARDIZE(x, mean, standard_dev) returns the z-score of x from the mean and SD, and =(A2-$B$1)/$B$2 gives the same result. Use absolute references ($B$1, $B$2) for the mean and SD so you can fill the formula down a whole column. The reverse, from a z-score to a value, is =mean+z*sd.
🤖 AI TRAINING · PROMPT → PREDICT → VERIFY
Prompt. Ask an AI tool: “For a standard normal distribution, what is P(z > 1.5)? Show your steps.”
Predict. Before you read its answer, make your own estimate. You know z = 1.5 lies to the right of the center, so the area above it is less than half of the total area of 1. Is it closer to 0.5, to 0.1, or to 0.01? Write down your guess and your reason.
Verify. Check the AI's number with technology. In Excel, type =1-NORM.S.DIST(1.5,TRUE) and you should get about 0.0668 (the area to the left of 1.5 is about 0.9332, and 1 − 0.9332 = 0.0668). If the widget lets you enter your own mean and SD, you can also set 0 and 1 and shade above a at 1.5 to compare. Then judge the AI: Is its number correct? Did it find the area on the right side, or give the left-side area (0.9332) by mistake, a very common slip? Does it say which side of the curve it is finding? Flag anything wrong, vague, or too confident, and say what you would ask next.
⚡ TODAY'S ACTIVITIES (about 40 minutes total)
1
z-Scores by Hand, Forward and Backward
12 min
1
A constructed quiz has scores N(75, 10). Find the z-score of a score of 95 and of a score of 60. Write one sentence each saying what the z-score means. Show every step with the subtraction in parentheses.
2
Constructed heights follow N(170, 8) cm. Find the height that has z = 0.75 and the height that has z = −2. Check each by going forward.
3
Narrate: explain aloud to a partner (or to yourself) why a z-score has no units.
▶  Check
1. z(95) = (95 − 75) ÷ 10 = 2.0; z(60) = (60 − 75) ÷ 10 = −1.5. A 95 is 2 SDs above the mean; a 60 is 1.5 SDs below it. 2. z = 0.75: 170 + 0.75(8) = 170 + 6 = 176 cm; z = −2: 170 − 16 = 154 cm. Forward: (176 − 170) ÷ 8 = 0.75; (154 − 170) ÷ 8 = −2. 3. The distance from the mean and the SD are measured in the same units, so dividing one by the other cancels the units and leaves a pure number of standard deviations.
2
Compare Across Distributions
10 min
1
Priya scores 71 on a constructed Test P (mean 62, SD 5). Sam scores 166 on a constructed Test Q (mean 140, SD 20). Find each z-score.
2
Who did better relative to their own test? Explain in one sentence in plain words.
3
Challenge: Sam's raw score is more than twice Priya's. Why is that irrelevant to the comparison?
▶  Check
1. Priya: (71 − 62) ÷ 5 = 9 ÷ 5 = 1.8. Sam: (166 − 140) ÷ 20 = 26 ÷ 20 = 1.3. 2. Priya did better relative to her test: she is 1.8 SDs above her mean, while Sam is 1.3 SDs above his. 3. The two tests use different scales (different means and SDs); a raw score only means something compared with its own test's center and spread.
3
Predict, Then Move the Handle
10 min
1
In the widget (Shade an area, quiz scores N(75, 10)), choose below a. Before you drag, predict the z-score for the cutoffs a = 85, a = 60, and a = 95 using your own arithmetic. Then drag the handle to each and compare with the readout.
2
Switch to the cereal scenario (mean 500, SD 4) and put a at 492. Predict z, then read it. Where does the handle sit relative to the center?
3
Drag a until z is as close to 0 as you can get. What value is a, and why?
▶  Check
1. a = 85: z = 1.0; a = 60: z = −1.5; a = 95: z = 2.0. (The readout also shows the left-tail area P(X < a): about 0.8413, 0.0668, and 0.9772, which you will study in Lesson 25.3.) 2. z = (492 − 500) ÷ 4 = −2.0; the handle sits well left of center, 2 SDs out. 3. z = 0 when a equals the mean: 75 for quiz scores, 500 for cereal, 170 for heights.
4
Find the Mistake
8 min
1
A student is asked for the z-score of a score of 78 on a test with mean 70 and SD 4. The student writes: “z = 78 − 70 = 8, so the score is 8 standard deviations above the mean.”
2
Find the mistake, give the correct z-score, and say why 8 standard deviations above the mean would be an unreasonable claim for a quiz score.
3
A second student types 78-70/4 in Desmos and gets 60.5. What went wrong?
▶  Check
1. The student forgot to divide by the SD. The 8 is a distance in points, not in standard deviations. The correct z is 8 ÷ 4 = 2.0. 2. Eight SDs would put the score 32 points above the mean; on a test where almost everyone lands within a few SDs of the mean, that is not a plausible claim, and an unreasonably large z is a signal to recheck the arithmetic. 3. Without parentheses Desmos does the division 70 ÷ 4 = 17.5 first, then 78 − 17.5 = 60.5. The correct entry is (78-70)/4.
