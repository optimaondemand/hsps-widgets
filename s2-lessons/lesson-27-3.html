Lesson 27.3
Prediction: Interpolation vs. Extrapolation
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A line can answer questions it has no right to answer.
Use a fitted line to predict, decide whether the prediction is inside or beyond the data, and report it honestly with its limits.
Reading & work: Read each worked example, try the prediction tool, then build a prediction table in Excel. Plan on about 75 minutes. Every dataset and scenario on this page is constructed; none is real data.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to use a fitted line “y-hat = a + bx” to predict a value of y for a given x, and say what the prediction means in context.
2
I will be able to decide whether a prediction is an interpolation (x inside the range of the data) or an extrapolation (x beyond it).
3
I will be able to check whether a predicted value is even possible in context, and explain why extrapolation is risky.
4
I will be able to report a prediction honestly: as an estimate (“about”), with sensible digits, the range the data support, and no claim about cause.

✨ RESPONSIBILITY: A PREDICTION IS A PROMISE
A fitted line is a small machine: you feed it an x, it hands back a y. It never says “I do not know.” Type in 5 and you get a number. Type in 500 and you get a number just as confidently. The machine does not know that the second question was never part of what the data could answer.
That is where responsibility enters. Whoever reports a prediction owns the claim. An honest forecaster says how far the data reach, how rough the answer is, and what it does not show. Today you will learn the two checks that keep a prediction honest, and you will practice saying a prediction in a way you could defend.
📐 USING THE LINE TO PREDICT
In Lesson 27.2 you fitted a line to data. Its equation is written ŷ = a + bx, read “y-hat equals a plus b x.” The hat means predicted: the line gives a predicted y, which is not the same thing as a measured y. Here a is the intercept and b is the slope.
To predict, substitute your x and do the arithmetic. That is all. The care lies in what the answer means.
▶ A prediction is an estimate. Real points do not sit on the line; they scatter above and below it. So the predicted y is a typical value at that x, not a guaranteed one. Say “about” and mean it.
▶ A prediction does not show cause. The line describes a pattern in the data. It does not tell you that changing x would change y. Correlation is not causation, and prediction does not change that.
▶ Do not report more digits than you have earned. If the data were recorded as whole numbers, an answer like 11.1666667 claims a precision nobody has. Keep about as many digits as the data have, and say “about.”
🔄 TWO KINDS OF PREDICTION
Every data set has a smallest and a largest x. Those are the edges of what you actually observed.
▶ Interpolation means predicting for an x inside the range of the observed x values, from the smallest to the largest. If the line fits the data well, an interpolated prediction is reasonably trustworthy, because you are filling in between points you saw.
▶ Extrapolation means predicting for an x outside that range. It is risky, because you are assuming the pattern keeps going in a place where you have no data. It may not.
Two questions to ask every time:
▶ Check 1, the range check. Is x between the smallest and largest x in the data? If yes, interpolation. If no, extrapolation, and you should say so.
▶ Check 2, the sense check. Is the predicted y even possible in this situation? A negative number of cups, a test score of 140 out of 100, or a negative price means the line has run past where the real situation can follow it.
Passing both checks is not a guarantee. Failing either one is a warning you must report.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 1 — PREDICTING INSIDE AND BEYOND THE DATA
Constructed scenario, not real data. A made-up music student records the hours of practice x in a week and the number of mistakes y in one run-through of a piece. Six weeks of data:
Hours x123456
Mistakes y181513984
▶ Find the line. The mean of x is 3.5 and the mean of y is 67 ÷ 6 = 11.1667. The least-squares slope is b = −47.5 ÷ 17.5 = −2.7143, where 17.5 is the sum of the squared x-deviations and −47.5 is the sum of the products of the x- and y-deviations. The intercept is a = 11.1667 − (−2.7143)(3.5) = 20.667. So ŷ = 20.667 − 2.7143x. (Excel's trendline will show the same equation; you will check that below.)
▶ Say the slope. Each extra hour of practice goes with about 2.7 fewer mistakes, within these data. The intercept, 20.667, is the predicted number of mistakes at 0 hours, which is just outside the data.
▶ Range check. The observed hours run from 1 to 6. That is the interval we may interpolate in.
▶ Predict at x = 3.5 (inside the range). ŷ = 20.667 − 2.7143(3.5) = 20.667 − 9.500 = 11.17. Check 1: 3.5 is between 1 and 6, so this is an interpolation. Check 2: 11.17 mistakes is possible. Honest report: “about 11 mistakes.” (The data are whole numbers, so two decimals would claim too much.)
▶ Predict at x = 6.5 (just outside). ŷ = 20.667 − 2.7143(6.5) = 20.667 − 17.643 = 3.02. Check 1: 6.5 is beyond 6, so this is an extrapolation. Check 2: 3 mistakes is possible. Honest report: “the line suggests about 3 mistakes, but 6.5 hours is beyond the data, so treat this as a rough guess.”
▶ Predict at x = 8 (further out). ŷ = 20.667 − 2.7143(8) = 20.667 − 21.714 = −1.05. Check 1: 8 is beyond 6, so extrapolation. Check 2: fails. A music student cannot make −1.05 mistakes. The line gives an impossible value here.
▶ Predict at x = 12. ŷ = 20.667 − 2.7143(12) = −11.90. Even more absurd. The line reaches zero when 20.667 − 2.7143x = 0, that is at x = 20.667 ÷ 2.7143 = 7.61 hours. Past about 7.6 hours the line predicts negative mistakes, so it cannot be believed there.
▶ Say it responsibly. “For weeks with 1 to 6 hours of practice, the line predicts about 11 mistakes at 3.5 hours. I will not use it for 8 or 12 hours: the line gives negative mistakes there, so the pattern must change before then. This describes a pattern in six weeks of one student's practice; it does not show that practice is the only reason the mistakes fell.”
What went wrong at 8 hours? Nothing in the arithmetic. The line is straight forever; the real situation is not. Mistakes cannot drop below zero, so somewhere past 6 hours the real pattern must bend or flatten. The data never showed us where, because we have no data past 6.
⚠️ FOUR CONSTRUCTED CASES WHERE EXTRAPOLATION GOES WRONG
All four are made up to show the idea.
▶ A plant that stops growing. Suppose a made-up seedling is measured each week for weeks 1 to 10, and a line fitted to those weeks is ŷ = 6 + 2.5x (centimeters). At week 52 the line says 6 + 2.5(52) = 136 cm. But suppose this kind of plant stops growing at about 40 cm. Growth levels off, and the line, which is straight, cannot know.
▶ A price that goes negative. A made-up line for a used item's price falls by a fixed amount each year. Far enough to the right, it predicts a price below zero. Nobody sells something for a negative price. The item's value levels out near zero; the line does not.
▶ A score above 100. A made-up line predicts a test score from hours studied, with data from 1 to 5 hours. Push the hours high enough and the predicted score passes 100 on a test that cannot score above 100.
▶ A child's height predicting an adult's. Suppose a made-up line fitted to children aged 2 to 8 is ŷ = 75 + 6.2x (centimeters). At age 5 it gives 75 + 6.2(5) = 106 cm, a sensible interpolation. At age 18 it gives 186.6 cm, already a stretch. At age 40 it gives 75 + 6.2(40) = 323 cm, more than 3 meters tall. Growth in childhood does not continue at the same rate for life.
In each case the straight line was a fine description of the data range and a poor guide beyond it. The pattern changed; the line did not. That is the heart of the danger.
🎯 WORKED EXAMPLE 2 — REPORTING HONESTLY (RESPONSIBILITY)
Constructed scenario. Using the line from Example 1, a classmate writes a report. Compare two versions.
▶ Careless: “At 12 hours of practice a week you will make −11.9047619 mistakes.” Three problems at once: it is an extrapolation reported as fact, the value is impossible, and it has seven decimals the data cannot support.
▶ Responsible: “In the six weeks I recorded, with 1 to 6 hours of practice, more hours went with fewer mistakes: about 2.7 fewer per hour. At 3.5 hours the line predicts about 11 mistakes. The line should not be used beyond 6 hours; it would predict negative mistakes past about 7.6 hours. I only have six weeks from one student, and these data do not show that practice causes the drop.”
Notice the four habits in the responsible version: it states the range the data support, it says about, it refuses an impossible value, and it avoids a cause claim. Make those four habits yours.
📉 WHEN EVEN INTERPOLATION CAN BE POOR
Passing the range check does not make a prediction good. The line is only trustworthy inside the data if a line is the right shape for the pattern. If the points curve, a straight line will sit too high in some places and too low in others, even where you have data. A prediction there can be off by a lot, and the range check will say “interpolation” anyway.
You will see this in the tool below, on the Plant growth (curved) scenario. Studying this carefully, by looking at how far each point sits from the line, is the main idea of Week 28. For today, just notice the curve and keep in mind that a line can be the wrong shape.

💻 EXCEL: A PREDICTION TABLE, CLICK BY CLICK
You will build a prediction table that works for any line. Use the music-practice data from Worked Example 1 now, and reuse the layout in Assignment 27.3 with new data. Type formulas exactly as shown, with commas and no spaces.
Setup.
▶ Open a Blank workbook. Press Ctrl+S, name it Wk27_PredictionLesson_YourName.xlsx, and save it in your OneDrive Optima → Math folder.
▶ Double-click the tab Sheet1, type Data, and press Enter. Click the + by the tabs and name the new sheet Predictions.
Sheet 1: Data, and the trendline equation.
▶ In A1 type Hours and in B1 type Mistakes. Type the hours 1 to 6 in A2:A7 and the mistakes 18, 15, 13, 9, 8, 4 in B2:B7.
▶ Select A1:B7. On the Insert tab, open the scatter chart button and choose Scatter (markers only).
▶ With the chart selected, click the + (Chart Elements) beside it, open the arrow next to Trendline, and choose Linear. Then right-click the trendline, choose Format Trendline, and tick Display Equation on chart, as you did in Lesson 27.2.
▶ Read the equation on the chart. It should read about y = -2.7143x + 20.667. Write down both numbers; you will type them by hand in a moment. (Menu names differ slightly between desktop Excel and Excel for the web, and the equation may show a few more or fewer digits.)
Sheet 2: Predictions. Click the Predictions tab.
▶ A1: a (intercept); B1: type 20.667. A2: b (slope); B2: type -2.7143. These are the two numbers from the chart.
▶ A3: Smallest x in data; B3: =MIN(Data!A2:A7) (shows 1). A4: Largest x in data; B4: =MAX(Data!A2:A7) (shows 6). Typing Data! tells Excel to look at the Data sheet. Clicking the cells with the mouse while typing the formula works too.
▶ Headings in row 6: A6 x, B6 Predicted y, C6 Range check, D6 Sense check. Select A6:D6 and click B (Bold) on the Home tab.
▶ Type the x values to predict in A7:A10: 3.5, 6.5, 8, 12.
▶ B7: =$B$1+$B$2*A7. This is ŷ = a + bx. The dollar signs lock B1 and B2 so that when you fill down they do not move, while A7 becomes A8, A9, A10. (Pressing F4 with the cursor on a reference adds the dollar signs.)
▶ C7: =IF(AND(A7>=$B$3,A7<=$B$4),"inside the data range","beyond the data"). The IF formula checks that x is at least the minimum and at most the maximum, and prints the label. Use straight quotation marks.
▶ D7: =IF(B7>=0,"possible","impossible: negative"). This is the sense check; for these data a negative count of mistakes cannot happen.
▶ Select B7:D7 and drag the fill handle (the small square at the lower-right corner) down to row 10.
▶ Select B7:B10, and on the Home tab click Decrease Decimal until two decimals show.
▶ Expected values. Row 7 (3.5): 11.17, inside the data range, possible. Row 8 (6.5): 3.02, beyond the data, possible. Row 9 (8): −1.05, beyond the data, impossible. Row 10 (12): −11.90, beyond the data, impossible.
▶ Where does the line hit zero? In A12 type x where prediction reaches 0 and in B12 type =-B1/B2. Expect 7.61. (Solve a + bx = 0 for x.)
▶ Try it. Change B1 to 21 and B2 to -2.7 and watch every prediction update. Then put the original numbers back. This is why you typed a and b into cells instead of burying them inside every formula.
Honest note. You type a and b by hand from the chart this week; Week 28 shows Excel functions that compute them. If a cell shows #NAME?, check spelling and quotation marks. If the chart shows no equation, check that Display Equation on chart is ticked.
💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · EXCEL FOR THE TABLE, DESMOS FOR THE ARITHMETIC
Excel is the star. Put a and b from the trendline equation in two cells (here B1 and B2). Predicted value: =$B$1+$B$2*A7 filled down. Range flag: =IF(AND(A7>=$B$3,A7<=$B$4),"inside the data range","beyond the data"), with =MIN(Data!A2:A7) and =MAX(Data!A2:A7) in B3 and B4. Sense flag: =IF(B7>=0,"possible","impossible: negative"). Where the line reaches zero: =-B1/B2. Dollar signs (F4) lock the cells that must not move.

Desmos Scientific is for plain arithmetic only. Type 20.667-2.7143*3.5 and read about 11.17. Type 75+6.2*40 and read 323. Type 20.667/2.7143 and read about 7.61. Do the Desmos arithmetic first as a check on what Excel gives you.
🏭 WHERE PEOPLE USE THIS
Forecasters of weather, sales, and population all use patterns in past data to say something about the future. Careful ones state a time horizon and a range, not a single confident number, because predictions farther from the data they came from are usually less certain. (This is a general description; every number in this lesson is constructed.) A good habit for any forecast you read: ask how far beyond the observed data it reaches, and whether it admits that.
🤖 AI TRAINING · PROMPT → PREDICT → VERIFY (THE EXTRAPOLATION TRAP)
Prompt. Ask an AI tool exactly this: “A made-up study of students who studied between 1 and 6 hours produced the fitted line predicted score = 40 + 8 × hours, where the score is out of 100. What score does this predict for a student who studies 15 hours? Give me the number.”
Predict. Before you read its answer, decide for yourself. Is 15 inside the range 1 to 6? Is the result possible for a test out of 100? Write down what a responsible answer must include.
Verify. Your verified answer: 40 + 8(15) = 160. But 15 hours is far beyond the data (1 to 6 hours), so it is an extrapolation and should be flagged as one, and 160 is impossible on a test scored out of 100. (The line reaches 100 at (100 − 40) ÷ 8 = 7.5 hours, still beyond the data.) A responsible answer gives the arithmetic, flags the extrapolation, says that 160 cannot happen, and declines to trust the line there. Now critique the AI. Did it warn that 15 is outside the data? Did it report 160 as if it were fine, an impossible value given without comment? Did it use causal words such as “studying 15 hours will cause” or “will raise your score to”? Did it give one number with no “about” and no range? List each problem you found, and state your own verified answer.
