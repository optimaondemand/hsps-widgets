When the Normal Model Is the Wrong Model

🎯 WONDER: THE MODEL THAT IS TOO BEAUTIFUL TO TRUST BLINDLY
Last week you met something remarkable. Give a normal model a mean and an SD and it hands back percents, cutoffs, and counts: 68, 95, 99.7, and everything in between. The same curve describes fill weights, measurement errors, and quiz scores. It is one of the most beautiful ideas in statistics, and it is easy to fall in love with it.
Here is the discipline that love requires. A model is a claim about the data, and a claim can be false. The normal curve does not govern every list of numbers; it describes the lists that arise when many small, independent nudges add up. A list of repair bills, waiting times, or earnings in which a few people are far above the rest arose some other way, and a bell curve will describe it wrongly while sounding perfectly confident. A normal model fitted to such data can predict negative amounts, can claim that a range holds 95% of the values when it does not, or can name a center where almost no values sit.
So wonder here has two halves. First, awe that the normal model fits as often as it does. Second, the honesty to ask, every time: does it fit this data? Today you will learn five ways to ask, and you will find that none of them is a mechanical test. They are the habits of a careful scholar looking at evidence. All datasets in this lesson are constructed (made up) to teach the ideas, and the widget’s datasets are simulated.
🔍 FIVE WAYS TO ASK “DOES THE NORMAL MODEL FIT?”
Treat these as five questions you put to the data, in roughly this order. Sketch or view a histogram first, because it often answers the whole question at a glance.
▶ 1. Shape. Is the histogram single-humped and roughly symmetric, with the tails thinning out evenly on both sides? A pile-up at one end with a long tail on the other (skew), or two separate humps, is a strong warning.
▶ 2. Mean versus median. In a symmetric distribution the mean and median are close. A mean well above the median suggests a long right tail pulling the mean up; a mean well below it suggests a long left tail. “Close” and “well above” are judged against the SD, not against a fixed number.
▶ 3. The Empirical Rule check. Count the values within 1, 2, and 3 SD of the mean and turn them into percents. Compare with 68, 95, and 99.7. Small gaps are normal; large, systematic gaps are a warning.
▶ 4. Impossible values. Compute mean − 2 × SD. If the quantity cannot be negative (a time, a price, a count) and that number is below 0, a normal model is predicting values that cannot occur.
▶ 5. Outliers and heavy tails. A normal model expects about 0.3% of values beyond 3 SD. If a dataset has noticeably more than that, or a value that is wildly far from the rest, the tails are heavier than the model allows.
Two cautions before the examples. No single check settles it: a dataset can pass one check and fail another, as you will see. And small datasets wobble: with 20 values, one value is 5% of the data, so percents jump in big steps even when the model is fine.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 1: A RIGHT-SKEWED DATASET FAILS THE CHECKS
Constructed data. A made-up list of 24 repair-bill amounts, in dollars, sorted from smallest to largest:
8, 10, 11, 12, 12, 14, 15, 15, 16, 18, 19, 20, 22, 24, 27, 30, 34, 40, 48, 60, 75, 95, 130, 160
Question: does a normal model fit? Work the five questions.
▶ Shape. Picture the histogram: most bills sit between 8 and 40, then a thin tail stretches out to 130 and 160. Single hump at the left, long tail to the right: right-skewed.
▶ Mean versus median. The total is 915 and there are 24 values, so the mean is 915 ÷ 24 = 38.125, about 38.1. With 24 values the median is the average of the 12th and 13th: (20 + 22) ÷ 2 = 21. The mean is more than 17 above the median, a big gap next to a typical spread of about 40: a long right tail is pulling the mean up.
▶ The rule check. The sample SD is about 39.66 (Excel: STDEV.S). The table below counts values within each band.
BandInterval (mean ± k SD)Values insidePercent inside
Within 1 SD−1.5 to 77.821 of 2487.5% (rule: 68%)
Within 2 SD−41.2 to 117.422 of 2491.7% (rule: 95%)
Within 3 SD−80.9 to 157.123 of 2495.8% (rule: 99.7%)
▶ Impossible values. mean − 2 × SD = 38.125 − 2(39.66) = −41.2. A repair bill cannot be negative, so a normal model with this mean and SD would assign a real share of bills to negative dollar amounts.
▶ Outliers. The largest bill, 160, is (160 − 38.125) ÷ 39.66 = about 3.07 SD above the mean, beyond the 3 SD band, and 130 is about 2.3 SD above.
Verdict. Four of the five checks sound an alarm: shape, mean far above median, impossible values below zero, and a value past 3 SD. Notice that the rule check is not the loudest one. The percents inside 2 and 3 SD (91.7%, 95.8%) are only a few points under 95 and 99.7. The skew shows up more in the “within 1 SD” row (87.5%, far above 68%) and in the negative lower endpoints. This is why you ask all five questions instead of trusting one.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 2: A BELL-SHAPED DATASET THAT PASSES
Constructed data. Twenty-five made-up quiz scores, sorted:
62, 66, 68, 69, 70, 71, 72, 72, 73, 74, 74, 75, 75, 76, 77, 78, 78, 79, 81, 82, 84, 86, 88, 90, 94
▶ Shape. The scores pile up around 72 to 79, thin out on both sides, and run from 62 to 94. One hump, close to symmetric.
▶ Mean versus median. The sum is 1,914, so the mean is 1,914 ÷ 25 = 76.56. The 13th value in order is 75. A gap of about 1.6 next to an SD near 7.7 is small.
▶ The rule check. The sample SD is about 7.69. Within 1 SD (68.9 to 84.3): 18 of 25, or 72%. Within 2 SD (61.2 to 91.9): 24 of 25, or 96%. Within 3 SD (53.5 to 99.6): 25 of 25, or 100%. Compared with 68, 95, 99.7, every gap is a few points or less. With 25 values, each value is worth 4%, so 72% versus 68% is exactly one value.
▶ Impossible values. mean − 2 × SD = 76.56 − 2(7.69) = 61.2, well above 0, so nothing here is bounded in a way the bell curve would violate.
▶ Outliers. The smallest score, 62, is about 1.9 SD below the mean and the largest, 94, about 2.3 SD above. Nothing is wildly far from the rest.
Verdict. A normal model is a fair description of this dataset. Note the honest wording: fair, not proven. We have not shown the data are normal; we have found no evidence against the model. That is the most these checks can give you.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 3: TWO CLUSTERS, AND A WARNING ABOUT SUMMARY NUMBERS
Constructed data. Twenty made-up values, sorted:
22, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 66, 68, 69, 70, 71, 72, 73, 74, 76, 78
Imagine the histogram first: ten values between 22 and 32, then nothing from 33 to 65, then ten values between 66 and 78. Two humps with a gap between them. Now the summary numbers.
▶ Mean versus median. The sum is 991, so the mean is 991 ÷ 20 = 49.55. The median is the average of the 10th and 11th values, (32 + 66) ÷ 2 = 49. They nearly agree, so check 2 passes.
▶ The rule check. The sample SD is about 22.97. Within 1 SD (26.6 to 72.5): 12 of 20, or 60%. Within 2 SD (3.6 to 95.5): 100%. Within 3 SD: 100%. The 2 SD and 3 SD rows look healthy, and 60% is not wildly far from 68%.
▶ Impossible values. mean − 2 × SD = 49.55 − 2(22.97) = 3.6, which is above 0. Check 4 passes too.
Three of the numeric checks look fine, and the dataset is still plainly not a bell. The shape check catches it. Here is the sharpest way to see the failure: a normal model with mean 49.55 and SD 22.97 puts about 34% of its values within 10 of the mean (between 39.55 and 59.55), which for 20 values is about 6 or 7 values. This dataset has none there. The model puts the most values exactly where this data has the fewest.
Verdict. The normal model does not fit, and the mean is not a typical value at all: it describes a spot where no one lives. The honest description is two groups, each worth its own summary.
⚠️ MATCHING THE MEAN AND SD DOES NOT MAKE DATA NORMAL
Look at Examples 2 and 3 together. Example 3 has a mean of 49.55 and an SD of 22.97, and you can draw a normal curve with exactly that mean and SD over it. The curve will have the right center and the right width, and it will still be wrong, because the shape is wrong. The mean and SD are two numbers; the shape of a dataset is far more than two numbers. Many different shapes share the same mean and SD.
It follows that these diagnostics can never prove a dataset is normal. Real data are never exactly normal. What we ask is a more modest and more useful question: is the normal model a good enough description for what I want to do with it? There is no cutoff such as “the rule check must come within 3 points.” Three checks might pass and one fail; a small dataset may wobble; a model that is fine for estimating the middle 68% might be bad for claims about extreme values. That judgment is yours, and it should be written down with its reasons.
And then what? If the normal model does not fit, you do not give up on the data; you stop using the wrong tool. For skewed data you describe the center with the median and the spread with the IQR, as in Module 8, and you describe the shape in words. For two clusters you ask what separates the groups and describe each. Statistics has other models and methods built for data like this. We will not teach them here. Today the skill is the honest first step: noticing that the bell curve is the wrong model.

💡 WHAT THE REVEAL SHOWS (READ AFTER YOU HAVE COMMITTED)
When you press the reveal button, the widget tells you what each set was built to be:
▶ Set 1 is a true normal sample (mean 100, SD 15). Even so, its percents will not match 68, 95, and 99.7 exactly, because 200 values are a random sample. A few points of wobble is what a good fit looks like.
▶ Set 2 is an income-like right-skewed set: mean 43.4, median 37.3, SD 27.8. Within 1, 2, and 3 SD it has 78.5%, 95.5%, and 97.5%. Notice what that does and does not say. Within 2 SD, 95.5% is almost exactly the 95% the rule predicts, yet the model is wrong: the mean is well above the median, and mean − 2 × SD = 43.4 − 2(27.8) = −12.2 is below zero for a quantity that cannot be negative. The 78.5% inside 1 SD is far above 68%, and 97.5% inside 3 SD is below 99.7%. Skew pushes more values near the center and leaves a thin tail beyond.
▶ Set 3 is two clusters, near 40 and near 80. This is Example 3 again: read the mean and median from the widget and see whether they say anything about where the data actually are.
▶ Set 4 is mostly bell-shaped with heavy tails. It looks fine in the middle. Read its table and look at the red curve in the far left and right of the histogram to see where it disagrees.
A good record for this activity is not “I got four out of four.” It is “here is what I noticed and why I guessed as I did, and here is what I missed.” Misses are where you learn which check to rely on.
💻 EXCEL: RUN THE CHECKS ON A LIST, CLICK BY CLICK
You will enter the 24 repair-bill amounts from Worked Example 1 and have Excel run the mean, median, SD, and the rule check. Keep this page open beside Excel. This workbook is practice; you do not upload it. If you keep it, save it as Wk26_L1_YourName.
▶ Open Excel and start a Blank workbook.
▶ Click A1, type Amount, press Enter. In A2 type the first value, 8, press Enter, and continue down column A with the rest of the list in order: 10, 11, 12, 12, 14, 15, 15, 16, 18, 19, 20, 22, 24, 27, 30, 34, 40, 48, 60, 75, 95, 130, 160. The last value, 160, should land in A25.
▶ Click C1, type Mean, press Tab, then type =AVERAGE(A2:A25) in D1 and press Enter. You should see 38.125.
▶ Click C2, type Median, Tab, then =MEDIAN(A2:A25) in D2, Enter. You should see 21.
▶ Click C3, type SD, Tab, then =STDEV.S(A2:A25) in D3, Enter. You should see about 39.66.
▶ Click C5 and type these headings across row 5, pressing Tab after each: k, Lower, Upper, Count inside, Percent inside, Rule says. Press Enter.
▶ In C6, C7, C8 type 1, 2, 3 (one per cell). In H6, H7, H8 type 68, 95, 99.7.
▶ Click D6 and type =$D$1-C6*$D$3, Enter. Click E6 and type =$D$1+C6*$D$3, Enter. You should see about −1.53 and 77.78.
▶ Click F6 and type =COUNTIFS($A$2:$A$25,">="&D6,$A$2:$A$25,"<="&E6), Enter. You should see 21. (COUNTIFS counts the cells in A2:A25 that are at least the lower end and at most the upper end.)
▶ Click G6 and type =F6/COUNT($A$2:$A$25), Enter. You should see 0.875. The percent is a decimal until you format it: after the next step, select G6:G8 and click the % button in the Number group of the Home tab (then Increase Decimal once if you want 87.5%).
▶ Select D6:G6 (click D6, hold Shift, click G6) and drag the small square at the bottom-right corner of the selection down through row 8. Rows 7 and 8 should show counts 22 and 23, and about 91.7% and 95.8%.
▶ Read it like a scholar. Compare column G with column H. Compare D1 with D2. Look at the Lower values in D7 and D8: negative. Those three observations are checks 3, 2, and 4.
Why the dollar signs? They lock the mean cell, the SD cell, and the data range so that they stay put when you copy the formulas down. Why the quotation marks and ampersands? Inside COUNTIFS, a condition such as “at least this number” is written as text, and the ampersand joins the symbol to the cell’s value. Menu names differ a little between desktop Excel and Excel for the web, but typing a formula into a cell works the same in both.
💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · THE FIT CHECKS IN EXCEL AND DESMOS
Excel does the list work. With your values in A2:A25: =AVERAGE(A2:A25), =MEDIAN(A2:A25), =STDEV.S(A2:A25). To count the values within one SD of the mean, with the mean in D1 and the SD in D3: =COUNTIFS(A2:A25,">="&D1-D3,A2:A25,"<="&D1+D3), then divide by =COUNT(A2:A25). Change the 1 to a 2 or a 3 (type D1-2*D3 and D1+2*D3) for the wider bands.

Desmos Scientific only does plain arithmetic here, so type the numbers yourself. To test for impossible values with mean 38.125 and SD 39.66, type 38.125-2*39.66 and read about −41.2: below zero, so a normal model predicts impossible bills. To find how far an extreme value is from the mean in SDs, type (160-38.125)/39.66 and read about 3.07.
🌐 WHERE THIS MATTERS
Skewed data, such as incomes, home prices, and waiting times, are often described with the median rather than the mean, because a few very large values pull the mean upward and a normal model often fits such data poorly. When someone reports an “average” for this kind of data, a careful reader asks which average they meant and what the histogram looks like. The questions in this lesson are the ones a careful reader asks.
🤖 AI TRAINING · PROMPT → PREDICT → VERIFY
Prompt. Paste this exact constructed list into an AI tool and ask: “Here are 20 measured values: 3, 4, 4, 5, 5, 6, 6, 7, 7, 8, 9, 10, 12, 14, 18, 25, 40, 64, 90, 120. Does a normal model fit this data? Explain how you decided.”
Predict. Before you read its answer, decide yourself. Picture the histogram: most values are under 20, with a long tail to 120, so right-skewed. The mean is 457 ÷ 20 = 22.85 and the median is (8 + 9) ÷ 2 = 8.5, a huge gap. The sample SD is about 32.08, so mean − 2 × SD is about −41.3: if these values cannot be negative, the model predicts impossible values. Within 1, 2, and 3 SD there are 17, 18, and 19 values: 85%, 90%, 95%, versus 68, 95, 99.7. The largest value, 120, is about 3.03 SD above the mean. My diagnosis: a normal model does not fit.
Verify and grade. Did the AI look at the shape? Compare the mean and median? Run the rule check with actual counts? Check for impossible values? Or did it just say “yes, it looks roughly normal”? Recompute any numbers it quotes in Excel or Desmos. A weak answer says yes without checking or invents numbers; a better answer names several diagnostics and gets the arithmetic right; even a good answer should say that the checks are a judgment. Write two or three sentences grading the AI’s diagnostic and say what you would ask next.
⚡ TODAY'S ACTIVITIES (about 55 minutes total)
1
Read the Clues
12 min
1
Four constructed datasets are described below. For each, decide bell fits, does not fit, or fits in the middle but not the tails, and name the check or checks that decided it.
2
(a) A single, symmetric hump; mean 15, median 14, SD 3; about 70% within 1 SD, 95% within 2 SD, 100% within 3 SD.
(b) A quantity that cannot be negative; mean 60, median 35, SD 45.
(c) Two separate humps near 30 and 70; mean 50, median 50, SD 20.
(d) A symmetric hump with mean 100 and SD 15, but 6 of 200 values lie more than 3 SD from the mean.
3
Speak it: explain aloud to someone why check (c) cannot be settled by the mean and median alone.
▶  Check
(a) Fits: one symmetric hump, mean near median, rule check close to 68/95/99.7, and mean − 3 SD = 6 is above 0. (b) Does not fit: the mean is far above the median (skew) and mean − 2 × SD = 60 − 90 = −30 is impossible for a quantity that cannot be negative. (c) Does not fit: two humps, so the shape check fails; the mean and median agree only because the humps are symmetric about 50, where there are few values. (d) Fits in the middle, not in the tails: a normal model expects about 0.0027 × 200 = 0.54, so about 1, value beyond 3 SD, and the data have 6, so the tails are heavier than the model allows. A reasonable answer says the model is acceptable for the middle but not for claims about extremes.

Run the Check by Hand
15 min
1
A constructed list of 12 values: 20, 22, 23, 24, 25, 25, 26, 27, 28, 30, 34, 46. A spreadsheet reports mean = 27.5 and sample SD = 6.91. Find the median. How does it compare with the mean?
2
Write the intervals for 1, 2, and 3 SD (mean ± k SD). Count the values inside each and write the percents. Compare with 68, 95, 99.7.
3
Compute mean − 2 × SD with Desmos. Then state a verdict (fits, fits roughly, or does not fit), a reason, and one caution about having only 12 values.
▶  Check
The sorted values give a median of (25 + 26) ÷ 2 = 25.5, a gap of 2 below the mean, small next to an SD of about 6.9. Intervals: 1 SD is 20.59 to 34.41 (10 of 12 values, 83.3%; 20 falls just below and 46 above); 2 SD is 13.68 to 41.32 (11 of 12, 91.7%; only 46 is outside); 3 SD is 6.77 to 48.23 (12 of 12, 100%). Mean − 2 × SD = 27.5 − 2(6.91) = 13.7, above zero. A reasonable verdict is fits roughly: nothing is impossible and the mean and median are close, but the value 46 is a bit isolated, so a histogram check would help. With 12 values, one value is 8.3% of the data, so the percents move in steps of about 8 points, and 83.3% versus 68% is only two values’ difference.

Report It Honestly
13 min
1
A constructed report on made-up neighborhood home sale prices says: mean $410 thousand, median $330 thousand, SD $260 thousand. Prices cannot be negative. Use two of the five checks to decide whether a normal model with this mean and SD is sensible.
2
A classmate says: “Normal models fit everything, so I will tell people 95% of prices are between mean − 2 SD and mean + 2 SD.” Write two or three sentences replying, and say what you would report instead.
3
Optional: a normal model with this mean and SD would put about 5.7% of homes below zero. Check that with a z-score, z = (0 − 410) ÷ 260, and Excel’s =NORM.S.DIST(-1.58,TRUE).
▶  Check
Mean ($410k) is far above median ($330k): a right-skewed pattern. Mean − 2 × SD = 410 − 520 = −110 thousand dollars, an impossible price. So the normal model is not sensible here. Reply: the 95% claim is false because the interval would begin below zero, and the data are probably skewed. Report the median and describe the spread with the IQR (and the shape in words) rather than a normal interval. For the optional part, z = −410 ÷ 260 = −1.577, and the exact share below zero is about 5.7%, so a normal model would put about 1 home in 17 at a negative price; NORM.S.DIST(−1.58, TRUE) is about 0.057.
