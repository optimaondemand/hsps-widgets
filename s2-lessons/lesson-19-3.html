📊 THE TABLE BECOMES A PICTURE
A table is exact, but a picture lets the eye compare in a glance. For categorical data two displays do most of the work: the bar chart and the circle graph (also called a pie chart). Both start from the frequency table you built yesterday. Here is a small constructed table to carry through today — the favorite sport of 40 surveyed students:

📈 THE BAR CHART
A bar chart draws one bar per category, with the bar's length showing the frequency (or the relative frequency). The rules are few, and each one protects the reader:
▶ Equal widths, equal gaps. Only the length carries information.
▶ Start at zero. A bar's length is supposed to be proportional to its value; if the axis starts above zero, a bar twice as long no longer means twice as much. (Week 21 returns to this.)
▶ Label everything. A title saying what was measured and on whom, both axes with units, and every category named.
▶ Order the bars on purpose. If the categories have a natural order (grade levels, months), keep it. If not, ordering from tallest to shortest makes comparisons easy.

🍰 THE CIRCLE GRAPH
A circle graph divides a circle into slices, one per category, with each slice's angle proportional to its share of the whole. Since a full circle is \(360^\circ\), angle = relative frequency \(\times 360^\circ\).
Worked example. Soccer: \(0.35 \times 360 = 126^\circ\). Basketball: \(0.275 \times 360 = 99^\circ\). Swimming: \(0.20 \times 360 = 72^\circ\). Tennis: \(0.125 \times 360 = 45^\circ\). Volleyball: \(0.05 \times 360 = 18^\circ\). Check: \(126 + 99 + 72 + 45 + 18 = 360\). Every circle graph you draw has this check built in.

The circle graph shines at one job: showing how a whole divides into a few parts — and it only makes sense when the categories do not overlap and together make up the whole. With more than about six slices, or slices close in size, the eye cannot judge angles well, and a bar chart wins. Never use a circle graph when the percents do not total 100% because people could choose more than one answer.
✅ THE HONEST-DISPLAY CHECKLIST (KEEP THIS)
Before you show anyone a categorical display: (1) Does the title say what was measured and on whom? (2) Are the axes labeled with units, and the categories named? (3) Do the bars start at zero? (4) Is the sample size \(n\) stated? (5) If it is made-up or a classroom survey, does it say so? A display that fails these checks may still be pretty — it is no longer honest.

💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · ANGLES IN DESMOS
The circle-graph rule is one line. In Desmos, for soccer type 0.35*360 → 126. Or start from the frequency itself: 14/40*360 → 126. For basketball, 11/40*360 → 99. Add all five angles as a check: 126+99+72+45+18 → 360. If your total misses 360, find the slip before drawing.

In Excel you can already build the table's percent column (Lesson 19.2). Next week we will use it to let Excel draw the chart itself; for now, notice how much easier it is to catch a bad total in a column of numbers than in a finished picture.
🤖 AI TRAINING · PROMPT → PREDICT → VERIFY
Prompt. Describe three datasets to an AI tool and ask which display fits each, and why: (a) how a family's monthly budget divides among five spending categories; (b) the number of students in each of 12 school clubs; (c) how favorite pizza topping differs among 9th, 10th, 11th, and 12th graders.
Predict. Decide yourself. (a) A circle graph or a bar chart — it is parts of a whole with few categories. (b) A bar chart; twelve slices would be unreadable. (c) Side-by-side (grouped) bars for comparing groups, or a two-way table.
Verify. Challenge one recommendation: ask the AI to defend a circle graph for the 12 clubs, then judge whether its defense holds up. Notice whether its answer depends on the number of categories or whether it merely sounds confident either way.

▶  Check

Angles and the Circle
15 min
1
Compute every angle in Desmos using the pattern \(\text{relative frequency} \times 360\). Check that they total \(360^\circ\).
2
Using a protractor (or a printed circle), draw the circle graph and label each slice with its percent.
▶  Check
126°, 99°, 72°, 45°, 18° — total 360°. Percents on the slices: 35%, 27.5%, 20%, 12.5%, 5%.
3
Choose and Critique
15 min
1
For each situation, name the better display and say why in one sentence: (a) how a pizza's 8 equal slices were eaten by 3 people; (b) the number of absences on each of the 5 school days; (c) the percent of class time spent on 15 different activities.
2
A classmate shows a bar chart titled just “Results” with no axis labels, no sample size, and bars that start at 40. List every problem and say how you would repair each.
▶  Check
(a) A circle graph: a whole divided among a few parts. (b) A bar chart (days compared; order is natural: Monday to Friday). (c) A bar chart: 15 categories is too many for a circle. Critique: the title says nothing about what or who; axes are unlabeled; n is missing; and a baseline of 40 exaggerates differences. Repairs: informative title, labeled axes with units, stated n, zero baseline.
