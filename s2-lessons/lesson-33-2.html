Lesson 33.2
Margin of Error: How Far Can a Poll Miss?
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A poll is one draw from a big pile of polls we might have gotten, and the width of that pile is the margin of error.
Simulating many polls; margin of error as the distance that holds the middle 95% of poll results; the safe rule 1 ÷ √n and the formula 1.96 × √(p(1 − p) ÷ n); planning a sample size; reading a lead honestly; what the margin does not cover
Reading & work: Read every section in order, work each example on paper before you read its solution, use the Margin of Error Lab, follow the Excel steps, and complete the three activities at the bottom. Plan on about 75 minutes: roughly 40 minutes for reading, examples, the interactive, and the Excel steps, and about 35 minutes for the activities.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain, using a simulation of many polls, that the margin of error is the distance from the true value within which about 95 of every 100 poll results land.
2
I will be able to compute a margin of error two ways, the safe rule 1 ÷ √n and the formula 1.96 × √(p(1 − p) ÷ n), and say why the safe rule does not need to know p.
3
I will be able to plan a sample size for a target margin of error with n = (100 ÷ m)² (rounded up), and explain why cutting the margin in half takes four times as many people.
4
I will be able to read a poll honestly: decide whether a lead is clear or too close to call, and name what a margin of error does not cover.

✨ A WONDER TO START WITH
A made-up news story reads: “Rivera 52%, Okafor 48%. Margin of error: plus or minus 3.5 points.” Rivera has the bigger number. Does that mean Rivera is ahead? And what is that “plus or minus” doing there?
In Lesson 33.1 you saw that when you take many samples, the sample means (or sample proportions) pile up in a bell shape around the truth, and the width of that pile is the standard error. Today we turn that width into something a reader can use: a statement of how far one poll can miss. We will find it first by simulation, by running a thousand pretend polls and looking at the pile. Only after you have seen it will we write the formula.
Service, this module’s virtue, shows up here in a quiet way. A town deciding about a park, a school board choosing a schedule, and neighbors weighing a ballot measure all lean on numbers from polls. A person who can read those numbers honestly, without overselling a lead and without throwing out a useful poll, is doing a real service to everyone who has to decide.
A note on honesty. Every poll, percent, and candidate in this lesson is made up (constructed) or simulated by a computer. Rivera and Okafor are invented names. None of it is real polling data.
🎲 ONE POLL, ONE DRAW FROM A BIG PILE
Imagine a made-up town where exactly 40% of residents favor a new library-hours plan. That true percent is a parameter, and in real life nobody knows it. A pollster takes a simple random sample of n people and reports the sample percent, a statistic. Another random sample of the same size would give a slightly different percent, and another a third. This is the sampling variation you studied in Lesson 33.1 and in Module 11.
Here is the key move. Suppose we could run the poll a thousand times (a computer can, if we tell it the truth). The thousand results form a pile, centered near the true 40% with a bell shape. Now ask: how far from the truth do the middle 95 of every 100 results land? Call that distance d. If you knew d, then for any single poll you could say, “Polls like this land within d points of the truth about 95 times in 100.”
That distance d is the margin of error (MOE). Notice where the idea comes from: it comes from the pile of possible polls, not from a formula. The formula, which comes later today, is just a shortcut for finding the width of the pile without running a thousand polls.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 1: A THOUSAND SIMULATED POLLS
Problem. In a made-up town, 40% favor the library-hours plan. A computer script (fixed starting seed, so the run can be repeated) draws 1000 random samples at each of three sizes, n = 100, 400, and 1600, and records the percent who say yes in each. For each size, find the distance d that holds the middle 95% of the 1000 results, and compare the three distances.
Teacher’s voice: For each size I measure how far every result is from the truth, 40, ignoring direction. Then I sort those 1000 distances from smallest to largest and read the 950th one. Ninety-five percent of the polls are at least that close, so that distance is the simulated margin of error. (This is how the lab’s tile Simulated margin of error is defined.)
For n = 400, here is the pile of all 1000 results, in 2-point bins.
Simulated data: 1000 poll results, each from a random sample of 400 (true percent 40, made up). Each bar counts the polls in a 2-point bin. The shaded band is 40 ± 4.8.
The bars pile up in a bell shape centered at 40. The shaded band, 40 ± 4.8, holds 947 of the 1000 polls. That is 94.7%, close to 95%. A few polls missed badly: the lowest result in this run was 30.5 and the highest was 46.25.
Here are all three sizes together. The first column of numbers comes from the simulation, and the other two are formulas we will build in a moment.
Sample size nSimulated MOE (from 1000 polls)Formula 1.96 × √(0.4 × 0.6 ÷ n)Safe rule 100 ÷ √n

Look at the pattern. Going from 100 to 400 people (four times as many) cut the margin from about 10 points to about 5, which is half. Going from 400 to 1600 (four times again) cut it from about 5 to about 2.4. The simulated 2.25 sits a bit under the formula’s 2.4. That is not a mistake: poll results are whole-number counts, and 1000 polls is a modest pile, so a simulated value can wobble by a few tenths of a point from run to run. For this reason we say “about.”
Answer. In this simulated run the margins were about 10, 5, and 2.25 points for n = 100, 400, and 1600. Your run in the lab below will differ a little, because the polls are random. Four times the people, half the margin is the pattern to carry forward.

📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 2: THE FORMULA AND THE SAFE RULE
Problem. Find the margin of error for a random sample of n = 400 from a population whose true percent is p = 40%, first with the formula 1.96 × √(p(1 − p) ÷ n), then with the safe rule 1 ÷ √n. Then see how the formula changes with p.
Teacher’s voice: Where does the formula come from? In Lesson 33.1 you learned that the pile of sample proportions has a spread called the standard error, √(p(1 − p) ÷ n), and that the pile is roughly bell-shaped. In a bell curve, about 95% of the values sit within about 2 standard errors of the center. The more exact number is 1.96. So the margin of error is 1.96 standard errors. That is the whole idea, and it matches what the simulation found.
Formula. p(1 − p) = 0.4 × 0.6 = 0.24. Divide by n: 0.24 ÷ 400 = 0.0006. The square root is √0.0006 = 0.02449, which is the standard error. Multiply by 1.96: 1.96 × 0.02449 = 0.0480, or 4.8 percentage points.
Safe rule. √400 = 20, and 1 ÷ 20 = 0.05, so the rule gives 5.0 points. In the shortcut form, 100 ÷ √n points = 100 ÷ 20 = 5.
Why have a safe rule at all? The formula needs p, the very number the poll is trying to learn. The product p(1 − p) is biggest when p = 50%, so the formula’s margin is biggest there too. Look at the table.
True percent pp(1 − p)Formula MOE at n = 400 (points)
10%0.092.9
20%0.163.9
30%0.214.5
40%0.244.8
50%0.254.9
60%0.244.8
At p = 50% the formula gives 1.96 × √(0.25 ÷ 400) = 1.96 × 0.025 = 0.049, which is 4.9 points. Since 1.96 × √0.25 = 0.98, which is just under 1, the safe rule 1 ÷ √n is the worst-case formula value rounded up a hair. It never understates the formula’s margin, and it does not need p. That is why it is called “safe.” When the true percent is far from 50%, the safe rule is a little roomy (at 20% it says 5.0 where the formula says 3.9).
Answer. Formula at p = 40%: about 4.8 points. Safe rule: 5.0 points. The simulation in Example 1 found 5.0 for this size. All three agree to within a few tenths of a point. Remember the two rules: the formula needs the true p, and the safe rule needs only n.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 3: HOW MANY PEOPLE DO WE NEED?
Problem. A made-up town clerk wants a poll with a margin of error of no more than 3 percentage points. How many people should she survey? What if she wants 5 points? 2 points? What does each cost compared with the 5-point plan?
Teacher’s voice: Start from the safe rule, margin = 1 ÷ √n, and solve for n. Write the margin as a proportion m (so 3 points is m = 0.03). Then √n = 1 ÷ m, so n = (1 ÷ m)². If you keep the margin in points, the same formula reads n = (100 ÷ m)².
For 3 points: (100 ÷ 3)² = 33.33…² = 1111.1…. We must round up, never down, because 1,111 people would give a margin of 100 ÷ √1111 = 3.0002 points, a hair over the target, while 1,112 gives 2.9988, just under. So she needs 1,112 people.
Target margin (points)People needed n = (100 ÷ m)²Rounded up
10(100 ÷ 10)² = 100100
5(100 ÷ 5)² = 400400
3(100 ÷ 3)² = 1111.1…1,112
2.5(100 ÷ 2.5)² = 16001,600
2(100 ÷ 2)² = 25002,500
Compare the costs. Going from 5 points (400 people) to 2.5 points (1,600 people) cut the margin in half but cost four times as many people. Going from 5 points to 2 points costs 2,500 ÷ 400 = 6.25 times as many. Precision gets expensive quickly, because n sits under a square root: to halve the margin, quadruple the sample.
Answer. 3 points needs 1,112 people, 5 points needs 400, and 2 points needs 2,500. Each time the target margin is cut in half, the needed sample is multiplied by four. A planner should ask whether the extra precision is worth the extra cost.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 4: IS THE LEAD REAL?
Problem. Three made-up polls report two-candidate races. Poll X: Rivera 52%, Okafor 48%, n = 800. Poll Y: Rivera 58%, Okafor 42%, n = 800. Poll Z: Rivera 54%, Okafor 46%, n = 1600. For each, decide whether the lead is clear or too close to call. (All names and numbers are made up.)
Teacher’s voice: I will use the lab’s simple rule of thumb. First find the margin of error with the safe rule. Then compare the gap (the leader’s percent minus the other’s) with twice the margin. If the gap is smaller than about twice the margin, call it too close to call. If the gap is larger, the lead is hard to explain by sampling variation alone. Why twice? Each candidate’s percent wobbles, and the two wobble in opposite directions (when one rises, the other falls), so the gap between them swings about twice as far as either percent alone. This is a classroom rule of thumb, not the full method a professional would use, so treat it as a guide.
PollGapMargin 100 ÷ √nTwice the marginVerdict
X: 52 vs 48, n = 80043.547.07Too close to call (4 is less than 7.07)
Y: 58 vs 42, n = 800163.547.07Clear lead (16 is more than 7.07)
Z: 54 vs 46, n = 160082.505.00Clear lead (8 is more than 5)
Check Poll X: √800 = 28.28, and 100 ÷ 28.28 = 3.54. Rivera’s plausible range is 52 ± 3.5, from about 48.5 to 55.5, and Okafor’s is 48 ± 3.5, from about 44.5 to 51.5. The two ranges overlap a lot, so a 4-point gap is not enough to be sure who is ahead.
Answer. Poll X is too close to call. Polls Y and Z show clear leads. Notice Poll Z: its lead (8) is only half of Poll Y’s (16), yet it is still clear, because the poll is twice as large and its margin is smaller.
⚠️ WHAT THE MARGIN OF ERROR DOES NOT COVER
The margin of error measures one thing: how far a fair random sample can miss by chance. It says nothing about the other ways a poll can go wrong. In Module 11 you met these, and they do not shrink when the sample gets bigger.
ProblemWhat it doesDoes the margin of error cover it?
Bias in who is asked (for example, only people who phone a radio show)The pile of possible polls is centered away from the truthNo
Nonresponse (many people chosen never answer)The people who answer may differ from those who do notNo
Bad wording (a question that nudges the answer)Pushes answers one wayNo
Chance missesEven a perfect poll can land outside the marginIt describes them: about 5 polls in 100 miss by more than the margin by chance alone
That last row matters. A “plus or minus 3” does not promise the poll is within 3 points of the truth. It says that if you took many polls the same way, about 95 of every 100 would be. The other 5 would miss by more, through no one’s fault. And a huge poll built on a biased method is just a precisely wrong answer: a bigger n shrinks the margin of error, but it does nothing about a lean in the method.
⚠️ THREE COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS
“The poll is certainly within the margin of error.” No. About 95 of 100 polls taken this way would be; about 5 in 100 miss by more, by chance alone.
“A margin of error of 4% means a 4% chance the poll is wrong.” No. It is a distance, about 4 percentage points, not a probability. The 95 in “95 of 100” is how often polls land inside that distance.
“The candidate with the higher number is ahead.” Not necessarily. If the gap is smaller than about twice the margin, the poll cannot tell the two apart. Report such a race as “too close to call,” not as a lead.
🌎 WHERE YOU MIGHT SEE THIS: READING A POLITICAL POLL CORRECTLY
When a news story reports a poll, you can ask a short list of questions. These are good habits in general; practices differ from one polling organization to another, so treat the list as a guide, not a rulebook.
▶ Who was asked, and how were they chosen? A random sample is the goal. If people chose themselves (a call-in or click-in poll), the margin of error does not mean what it appears to mean.
▶ How many people? Use the safe rule, 100 ÷ √n points, to check the reported margin. Note that it describes the sample’s chance variation only.
▶ How big is the lead compared with the margin? A lead smaller than about twice the margin is best described as too close to call, as in Worked Example 4.
▶ How was the question worded, and how many people actually answered? Wording and nonresponse are not covered by the margin.
▶ Is this one poll or a pattern? A single poll can miss by chance. Several well-designed polls that agree tell you more than one.
Reading a poll this way is a service to your neighbors: you can say what a poll supports, what it does not, and why, instead of repeating a headline.

You will build a small margin-of-error calculator. Everything here is made up. This is a practice build; you do not upload it.
▶ Open Excel and start a Blank workbook. Save it as Wk33_L2_YourName. Rename the first sheet MOE (right-click the sheet tab at the bottom, choose Rename, type the name, press Enter).
▶ Type the headings. In A1 type n, in B1 type Safe rule, in C1 type p-hat, in D1 type Formula MOE, in E1 type Low end, and in F1 type High end.
▶ Type the data. In A2 type 100, in A3 type 400, and in A4 type 1600. In C2 type 0.4, then copy it to C3 and C4 (the same 0.4 in all three).
▶ Safe rule. Click B2 and type =1/SQRT(A2), then press Enter. Click B2 again and drag the small square at the bottom right corner of the cell down to B4. These are proportions, so format them as percents: select B2:B4, and on the Home tab, in the Number group, click the % button, then click Increase Decimal once. You should see 10.0%, 5.0%, and 2.5%. (Read each as percentage points.)
▶ Formula margin. Click D2 and type =1.96*SQRT(C2*(1-C2)/A2), press Enter, and fill down to D4. Format D2:D4 as percents with one decimal in the same way. You should see about 9.6%, 4.8%, and 2.4%.
▶ Interval endpoints. Click E2 and type =C2-D2. Click F2 and type =C2+D2. Select E2:F2 and fill down to row 4. Format E2:F4 as percents with one decimal. For n = 400 you should see 35.2% and 44.8%; for n = 100, 30.4% and 49.6%; for n = 1600, 37.6% and 42.4%.
▶ Sample-size planner. In H1 type Target (points), in I1 type m, and in J1 type People needed. In H2:H5 type 3, 5, 2, and 3.5 (the targets in points). Click I2 and type =H2/100, and fill down to I5 (this turns points into a proportion: 3 becomes 0.03). Click J2 and type =ROUNDUP((1/I2)^2,0), press Enter, and fill down to J5. You should see 1112, 400, 2500, and 817.
Check yourself. Change a value in column A (try 900) and watch B, D, E, and F update. Change C2 to 0.5: the formula margin for n = 100 should rise to about 9.8%, and the safe rule should stay put at 10.0%, because the safe rule never looks at p. ROUNDUP always rounds away from zero, so 1111.1 becomes 1112, never 1111.
💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · MARGIN OF ERROR IN DESMOS; INTERVAL ENDPOINTS AND SAMPLE SIZE IN EXCEL
Excel. =1/SQRT(n) gives the safe-rule margin as a proportion, and =1.96*SQRT(p*(1-p)/n) gives the formula margin. Interval endpoints are =p-moe and =p+moe (with cell references in place of the words). To plan a sample size from a target margin written as a proportion m (3 points is 0.03), type =ROUNDUP((1/m)^2,0). Format results as percents with Home > Number.
Desmos Scientific is plain arithmetic here, done in two small steps so you never have to nest a root inside a fraction. For n = 400, press the square-root button, enter 400, and read the result (20). Then enter 1 ÷ 20 to get 0.05, which is 5 points. For the margin around a poll result of 52% with a margin of 0.0354, enter 0.52 − 0.0354 and 0.52 + 0.0354 to get the two endpoints, 0.4846 and 0.5554. For a target of 3 points, enter 100 ÷ 3 to get 33.33…, then square it with the exponent key and round up to a whole person.
🤖 AI TRAINING · ASK AN AI FOR A MARGIN OF ERROR; CHECK THE NUMBER AND WHAT IT LEAVES OUT (PROMPT → PREDICT → VERIFY)
Prompt. Ask an AI tool: “A poll surveys a random sample of 400 people. What is the margin of error, and what does it mean? What does it not cover?”
Predict. Before you read its answer, decide what a careful answer must say: the margin is about 5 percentage points (1 ÷ √400 = 0.05; at p = 50% the formula gives 4.9); it means that about 95 of 100 polls taken this way would land within that distance of the truth; and it covers sampling variation only, not bias, nonresponse, or wording.
Verify. Check its number with the safe rule, then in the Margin of Error Lab set n = 400, press Run 1000 polls, and compare the Simulated margin of error tile. Then grade the meaning. Did it say “5% chance of being wrong” (a common slip, because the margin is a distance, not a probability)? Did it call the poll “accurate within 5 points” with no mention of the 95-in-100 idea? Did it leave out what the margin does not cover, or claim that a big sample removes bias? Rewrite its weakest sentence so that it is true. Verdict. A fluent answer is not a checked answer; the simulation and the formula are.
