📐 HOW SPREAD OUT IS THE DATA?
A center answers where the data sit. It says nothing about how far they stray. Two bus routes can both average 25 minutes, one running within a minute or two of that every day and the other swinging from 10 minutes to 50. If you report only the center, you have told the reader half the story — and left out the half that would change their plans. Today we measure spread, and we meet a rule for spotting values so far from the rest that they deserve a second look.
Four ideas from earlier lessons, in one line each. The median is the middle value of the ordered data. The first quartile \(Q_1\) and third quartile \(Q_3\) are the medians of the lower and upper halves (for an odd number of values the median itself is left out of both halves — the by-hand method, which we use unless a problem says Excel). The five-number summary is the minimum, \(Q_1\), median, \(Q_3\), and maximum. A box plot draws a box from \(Q_1\) to \(Q_3\) with a line at the median.
🛢️ A DATASET TO WORK WITH
A made-up pizza shop records the delivery time, in minutes, of 12 orders. Every dataset in this lesson is constructed to teach the idea; none is real. In the order the deliveries happened:
Order123456789101112
Minutes242227202347252126281830
Put them in order: 18, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 30, 47. The median is \(\dfrac{24 + 25}{2} = 24.5\) minutes. The lower half is 18, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, whose median is \(Q_1 = \dfrac{21 + 22}{2} = 21.5\); the upper half is 25, 26, 27, 28, 30, 47, whose median is \(Q_3 = \dfrac{27 + 28}{2} = 27.5\).
📏 THE RANGE — AND WHY ONE VALUE CONTROLS IT
The range is the largest value minus the smallest: \(\text{range} = \max - \min\). Here it is \(47 - 18 = 29\) minutes. It is quick to find and easy to say. But look at what it listens to: only the two most extreme values, and nothing else. Eleven of the twelve deliveries took between 18 and 30 minutes, yet one slow delivery makes the range 29. Remove that one order and the range becomes \(30 - 18 = 12\).
A statistic that one value can more than double is a poor description of “how spread out the typical delivery is.” We need a spread that ignores the extremes and looks at the middle of the data.
📦 THE IQR — THE SPREAD OF THE MIDDLE HALF
The interquartile range is the distance from \(Q_1\) to \(Q_3\): \(\text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1\). It is the width of the box in a box plot, and it tells you how wide the middle half of the data is. For the deliveries: \(\text{IQR} = 27.5 - 21.5 = 6\) minutes.
Why does it resist extreme values? Because the quartiles are found by counting positions, not by adding up sizes. Make the 47 into a 90 and it is still the biggest value, still in the top quarter, so \(Q_3\) does not move and the IQR stays 6. Remove the 47 entirely, and the eleven values 18, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 30 have median 24, a lower half 18 20 21 22 23 (\(Q_1 = 21\)) and an upper half 25 26 27 28 30 (\(Q_3 = 27\)): the IQR is \(27 - 21 = 6\) again.
With the 47: range 29, IQR 6   |   Without it: range 12, IQR 6
A statistic that barely changes when extreme values come and go is called resistant. The median and the IQR are resistant; the mean and the range are not. Here the mean is \(\dfrac{311}{12} \approx 25.92\) with the slow delivery and \(\dfrac{264}{11} = 24\) without it.
🚧 THE 1.5 × IQR RULE: FENCES FOR OUTLIERS
Some values are so far from the rest that we want a way to say so out loud. The most common rule uses the IQR as a ruler. Build two fences:
Lower fence \(= Q_1 - 1.5 \times \text{IQR}\)     Upper fence \(= Q_3 + 1.5 \times \text{IQR}\)
A value strictly outside the fences — below the lower fence or above the upper fence — is flagged as an outlier. “Strictly” matters: a value that lands exactly on a fence is not flagged.
Worked example, step by step. For the deliveries, \(\text{IQR} = 6\), so \(1.5 \times 6 = 9\).
▶ Lower fence: \(21.5 - 9 = 12.5\). Is any delivery below 12.5 minutes? The fastest is 18. No.
▶ Upper fence: \(27.5 + 9 = 36.5\). Is any delivery above 36.5 minutes? The slowest is 47. Yes — the 47 is flagged. The next largest, 30, is inside.
▶ Conclusion: one outlier, the 47-minute delivery.
Notice what we did not do: we did not look at the picture and decide by eye. The rule gives every analyst the same answer from the same data. That is its courtesy to the reader — and it is why a spreadsheet can apply it.
📦 DRAWING THE BOX PLOT WITH OUTLIERS
Now the box plot gets its finishing touch. In a box plot that shows outliers:
▶ Draw the box from \(Q_1\) to \(Q_3\) with a line at the median.
▶ Draw each whisker out to the most extreme value that is not an outlier — the smallest and largest values still inside the fences. (The whisker does not go to the fence itself, and it does not go to the outlier.)
▶ Draw each outlier as its own separate dot, past the whisker, with a gap to show that it stands apart.
For the deliveries: the box runs from 21.5 to 27.5 with the median line at 24.5; the left whisker reaches 18; the right whisker reaches 30 (the largest non-outlier); and the 47 is a dot. The dashed lines below show the fences — you do not draw those on a finished plot, but they explain where the whiskers stop.
Delivery Times, 12 Orders (constructed data)
Check the plot against the numbers. The box is narrow — the middle half of deliveries fall within 6 minutes of each other. The long gap between the right whisker (30) and the dot (47) is the story the range squeezed into a single number.
🤝 A FLAG IS A QUESTION, NOT A VERDICT
The fences tell you which values to look at. They do not tell you what to do with them. A flagged value might be a typing mistake (a 0 where a 40 belongs), a measurement that does not belong in this group (a bicycle delivery in a dataset of car deliveries), or a perfectly real, rare event (a delivery stuck behind a road closure). The first two may deserve a correction or a note; the third is a true part of the data, and deleting it would make the pizza shop look faster than it is. Find out why before you decide. That is courtesy to the data: you do not throw out a value for being inconvenient. And it is courtesy to the reader: whatever you do, say so, and report the summary both with and without the flagged value.

📑 A NOTE ON QUARTILE.INC AND THE FENCES
Excel's QUARTILE.INC finds quartiles by interpolating between positions, which can give slightly different \(Q_1\) and \(Q_3\) than the by-hand median-of-halves method. The fences therefore shift a little. For the deliveries, QUARTILE.INC gives \(Q_1 = 21.75\) and \(Q_3 = 27.25\), so \(\text{IQR} = 5.5\) and the fences are \(21.75 - 8.25 = 13.5\) and \(27.25 + 8.25 = 35.5\) — close to the by-hand 12.5 and 36.5. Same flag: the 47 is outside either way.
Usually the two methods agree on which values are flagged. Occasionally a value sits close to a fence and they disagree. Here are ten made-up quiz scores out of 40: 12, 25, 27, 31, 33, 34, 35, 37, 38, 39.
▶ By hand: \(Q_1 = 27\), \(Q_3 = 37\), \(\text{IQR} = 10\). Lower fence \(= 27 - 15 = 12\). The score 12 lands exactly on the fence, so it is not flagged (not strictly outside).
▶ QUARTILE.INC: \(Q_1 = 28\), \(Q_3 = 36.5\), \(\text{IQR} = 8.5\). Lower fence \(= 28 - 12.75 = 15.25\). Now 12 is below the fence and is flagged.
Neither answer is a mistake. The 1.5 × IQR rule is a convention for deciding what to look at, not a law of nature — a value near a fence is a borderline case under either method, and an honest report says which method it used. In this course, use the by-hand method unless a problem says to use Excel.
💻 EXCEL LAB: RANGE, IQR, FENCES, AND FLAGS
You will build the whole analysis in a spreadsheet so that the flag appears by itself. Keep this page open beside Excel. Excel work happens outside Canvas.
1. Start the workbook.
▶ Open Excel and start a Blank workbook. Save it as Wk22_L3_YourName in your OneDrive Optima → Math folder.
▶ Click cell A1, type Order and press Tab; type Minutes (cell B1) and press Tab; type Flag (cell C1) and press Enter.
▶ Click A2 and type the numbers 1 through 12 down column A (press Enter after each). Click B2 and type the twelve delivery times in the order they happened, pressing Enter after each: 24, 22, 27, 20, 23, 47, 25, 21, 26, 28, 18, 30. They do not need to be sorted; Excel does not care.

If a cell shows an error such as #NAME?, check the spelling of the function (QUARTILE.INC has a period) and that you typed the comma before the 1 or 3. The quartiles differ from our by-hand 21.5 and 27.5 because Excel uses QUARTILE.INC; that is expected.
3. The flag formula. We want Excel to write the word outlier next to any value outside the fences.
▶ Click cell C2.
▶ Type exactly: =IF(OR(B2<$F$9,B2>$F$10),"outlier","")  (a less-than sign, a greater-than sign, and at the end two straight quotation marks with nothing between them). Press Enter. The cell stays blank, because 24 is not an outlier.
▶ Read it aloud: if B2 is below the lower fence OR above the upper fence, show “outlier”; otherwise show nothing. The dollar signs in $F$9 and $F$10 lock those two cells so that they stay put when we copy the formula down.
▶ Click C2 so it is selected, then drag the small square at its lower-right corner down to C13. (Alternative: drag to select C2:C13, then press Ctrl + D to fill down.)
Look down column C: exactly one cell, C7 (next to the 47), should say outlier. To count flags automatically, click E12, type Number flagged, press Tab, and type =COUNTIF(C2:C13,"outlier") — you should see 1.
4. Change the data, watch the flag. Click B7 (the 47), type 35, and press Enter: the word outlier disappears, because 35 is below the upper fence of 35.5, and the range falls to 17. Now type 36: it is back, because 36 is above 35.5. The fence did not move — it is built from the quartiles, which count positions — so the flag switches exactly where the arithmetic says. Type 47 back when you are done.
💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · FENCES ON THE CALCULATOR AND IN EXCEL
Desmos Scientific handles the fence arithmetic when you already have \(Q_1\) and \(Q_3\). With \(Q_1 = 21.5\) and \(Q_3 = 27.5\): the IQR is 27.5-21.5 → 6; the lower fence is 21.5-1.5*6 → 12.5; the upper fence is 27.5+1.5*6 → 36.5. Desmos Scientific does not find quartiles of a list for you; do that by hand or in Excel.

Excel carries the list statistics: =MAX(B2:B13)-MIN(B2:B13) for the range, =QUARTILE.INC(B2:B13,1) and =QUARTILE.INC(B2:B13,3) for the quartiles, and typed formulas for the IQR and the fences. The flag formula =IF(OR(B2<$F$9,B2>$F$10),"outlier","") joins two comparisons with OR. Standard deviation is not on our list yet; that is Week 23.

🤖 AI TRAINING · PROMPT → PREDICT → VERIFY
Prompt. Paste these 11 made-up kiosk counts (customers per hour) into an AI tool: 18, 21, 24, 25, 28, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35, 47. Ask: “Is 47 an outlier?” Then ask it to show how it decided.
Predict. Predict the answer before you read it. A language tool will often say “yes, 47 is an outlier” because it sits 12 above the next-largest value (35): a conclusion based on looking far away, not on a rule.
Verify. Check by hand. The 6th value, 31, is the median; the lower half 18, 21, 24, 25, 28 gives \(Q_1 = 24\); the upper half 32, 33, 34, 35, 47 gives \(Q_3 = 34\). Then \(\text{IQR} = 10\) and the upper fence is \(34 + 15 = 49\). Since 47 is not above 49, it is not an outlier by the 1.5 × IQR rule. (Excel's QUARTILE.INC gives \(Q_1 = 24.5\) and \(Q_3 = 33.5\), so a fence of exactly \(33.5 + 13.5 = 47\); a value on the fence is not strictly outside, so the answer is the same.) Did the AI give a rule, show the quartiles, and say which method it used? A confident “yes” with no fences is a guess dressed up as an answer.

▶  Check
Original: range \(95 - 41 = 54\); the halves 41 43 44 46 and 48 50 52 95 give \(Q_1 = 43.5\), \(Q_3 = 51\), IQR 7.5. With 53 instead: range \(53 - 41 = 12\), IQR still 7.5. Fences: \(43.5 - 11.25 = 32.25\) and \(51 + 11.25 = 62.25\), so 95 is flagged. The IQR describes a typical day, because one value cannot move it; the range was controlled by the single 95.
