Shape: Symmetry, Skewness, and Clusters

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A summary is a courtesy only if it describes the data it stands for — and the shape tells you whether it does.
Shape words, how skew moves the mean, and choosing summaries that tell the truth

📷 A PICTURE BEFORE A NUMBER
Two data sets can share the same mean and the same median and still look nothing alike. The only way to see that is to look at the shape. Shape is the outline of a histogram: where the bars pile up, which way they trail off, whether there is one hump or two. Shape tells you which summary numbers are fair to the data, and it tells you what a summary leaves out.
Every data set in this lesson is constructed: made up to teach the idea, never presented as a real measurement. Each histogram below was drawn from the counts printed on its bars.
📈 FIVE SHAPES TO RECOGNIZE
1. Symmetric. The left side is roughly a mirror image of the right. The bars are tallest in the middle and fall away evenly on both sides. Here are the heights (cm) of 21 made-up seedlings:
Seedling heights, 21 plants (constructed data)
2. Skewed right. Most values pile up on the low side, and a long, thin tail stretches out to the right. Here are the waiting times (minutes) of 20 made-up clinic visitors:
Clinic waits, 20 visitors (constructed data)
3. Skewed left. The mirror image: most values pile up on the high side, and the tail stretches to the left. These are 20 made-up scores out of 50 on a very easy quiz:
Easy quiz scores out of 50, 20 students (constructed data)
⚠️ THE TAIL NAMES THE SKEW
We name a skew by the direction of the tail, not by where the tallest bars are. A skewed-right histogram has its tall bars on the left. Say it the other way around and you will describe the picture backward. Think of the tail as a finger pointing: “skewed right” means the finger points right.
4. Two clusters (bimodal). Two separate humps, often with a gap between them where no data live. Each hump is a peak. A histogram with one peak is unimodal; one with two is bimodal. These are 20 made-up ride lengths (minutes) on a city bus line, mixing short hops with long crosstown trips:
Bus ride lengths, 20 rides (constructed data)
5. Roughly uniform. The bars are about the same height, with no clear peak and no tail. A made-up spinner with six equal zones, spun 60 times, might give counts like these:
Spinner results, 60 spins (constructed counts)
Real data are messy and fall between these five. The words are a vocabulary for describing, not boxes the data must fit into. Two more words you will use: a cluster is a group of values close together, and a gap is a stretch of the axis with no values.
📦 WHAT A BOX PLOT SHOWS ABOUT SHAPE (AND WHAT IT HIDES)
A box plot shows five numbers, so it shows only part of a shape. For the clinic waits, the median is 6.5 minutes, \(Q_1 = 4.5\) and \(Q_3 = 13\) (median-of-each-half method), so \(\text{IQR} = 8.5\) and the fences are \(4.5 - 12.75 = -8.25\) and \(13 + 12.75 = 25.75\). The waits 26 and 34 are above the upper fence; the largest non-outlier is 21.
Clinic waits (constructed data)
▶ Clues to a skew in a box plot: the median line sits off-center in the box (here nearer \(Q_1\)), and one whisker is much longer than the other, often with outlier dots beyond it. The long side is the tail side.
▶ A box plot cannot show two clusters. For the bus rides the five-number summary is 4, 6.5, 16, 26.5, 31, and the box stretches from 6.5 to 26.5, across the very stretch where no ride falls (nothing between 10 and 22).
Bus ride lengths (constructed data)
This box plot reads as “one wide spread with the median in the middle.” Only the histogram shows that the data fall into two clumps. When shape matters, draw the histogram first.
⚖️ HOW SKEW PULLS THE MEAN
Recall that the mean is the balance point of the data and the median is the middle value. Moving one value far away changes the balance point a lot but changes the middle position not at all. So a long tail pulls the mean toward itself while the median stays with the bulk. Here are four of the distributions above, computed from the data behind each picture:

Worked example, narrated. The clinic waits are 2, 3, 3, 4, 4, 5, 5, 5, 6, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 12, 14, 17, 21, 26, 34. The median is the average of the 10th and 11th values, \(\dfrac{6 + 7}{2} = 6.5\). The 26 and 34 are far above the bulk, and the mean counts them at full size: the total is 201, so \(\text{mean} = \dfrac{201}{20} = 10.05\). The tail pulled the mean 3.55 minutes above the median. The easy quiz is the exact mirror image (each score is 50 minus a clinic wait), so the gap flips: the mean is 3.55 below the median.
🚫 A TENDENCY, NOT A LAW
This pattern is a tendency. It is a strong clue, and it fails often enough that you should not treat it as proof. Look at this made-up set: 0, 11, 11, 12, 12, 12, 18, 18, 19, 19, 20. One lone 0 sits far out on the left, yet the median is 12 and the mean is about 13.82, above the median, because most of the other values lump together above it. Small and lumpy data can break the rule. Check the histogram, not just the two numbers.
Look back at the bus rides: mean 16.7 and median 16 nearly agree, which a careless reader would take as “symmetric.” But look at the picture. Not one ride lasted between 10 and 22 minutes. The mean and the median both land in the gap, at a value that describes no actual ride. A summary can be correct and still describe nobody.
🧭 USING SHAPE TO CHOOSE A SUMMARY
Earlier lessons built two families of summaries. Shape tells you which family tells the truth.
Shape of the dataReport this centerReport this spreadWhy
Roughly symmetric, no far outliersmeanstandard deviation (or IQR)The mean and the median agree; the mean uses every value.
Skewed, or with outliersmedianIQRThese are resistant: a tail cannot drag them.
Two clustersno single center; describe both clustersthe range of each clusterA single center falls in the gap.
What a summary hides. For the clinic waits, the median (6.5 minutes) tells a patient what to expect, while the mean (10.05) overstates it because of two long waits. But the median alone hides those two long waits too, so a fair report mentions them. For the bus rides, a mean and SD would suggest rides cluster around 16.7 minutes; they do not. Whatever number you choose, ask: what would a reader miss if this were the only thing I told them? That question is courtesy to the data and to the reader.
This is why “the mean is the best center” is wrong. The mean is best for data it describes honestly. Using shape to pick the summary is not a trick; it is how you stay faithful to the data.
📝 THE DESCRIBE-A-DISTRIBUTION SENTENCE FRAME
When asked to describe a distribution, cover four things, always in the context of the data (what was measured, in what units):
▶ Shape: symmetric, skewed right or left, clusters or gaps, uniform-ish.
▶ Center: the median (skewed) or the mean (symmetric), with units.
▶ Spread: the IQR or SD, or the range of the middle of the data.
▶ Unusual features: outliers, gaps, clusters, anything a reader should not miss.
“The distribution of [variable, in context] is [shape], with a [center] of [value and units] and [spread]. [Unusual feature].”
Example. “The distribution of clinic waits (constructed data, 20 visitors) is skewed right, with a median wait of 6.5 minutes. The middle half of waits spans 8.5 minutes (IQR). Two waits, 26 and 34 minutes, are outliers by the 1.5 × IQR rule, far beyond the rest.” Notice it uses the median and IQR because the shape is skewed, and it names the outliers rather than hiding them.
💻 EXCEL LAB: READ THE SHAPE FROM NUMBERS
A histogram shows shape directly, but you can also read it from a few numbers. In this lab you put the mean and median side by side and look at the gap. Keep this page beside Excel. Excel work happens outside Canvas.
1. Start the workbook.

▶ Click A1, type Plan, press Tab, type GB, and press Enter. (A made-up phone company recorded the monthly data use, in gigabytes, of 12 plans.)
▶ Click A2 and type 1 through 12 down column A. Click B2 and type the twelve values, pressing Enter after each: 14, 5, 6, 38, 7, 5, 9, 22, 6, 11, 4, 8. Do not sort them.

3. Read the gap. The mean is 3.75 GB above the median, which is a third of the mean, and positive. That points to a right skew: a few big values pulling the mean up. Confirm it with a look at the data. Copy B2:B13 to column H, select it, and on the Data tab choose Sort Smallest to Largest. The last two values, 22 and 38, are far from the rest.
4. Decide what to report. The data are skewed right, so report the median and the IQR: “a typical plan used 7.5 GB, and the middle half of plans spans 6 GB.” Mention the heavy users too: with QUARTILE.INC the upper fence is \(11.75 + 1.5 \times 6 = 20.75\), so 22 and 38 are both flagged. The mean (11.25) is higher than 9 of the 12 plans, so it describes the typical plan poorly.
💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · MEAN, MEDIAN, AND THE GAP
Excel reads shape numerically with three functions side by side: =AVERAGE(B2:B13), =MEDIAN(B2:B13), and =STDEV.S(B2:B13). Subtract them, =F2-F3, to get the mean-median gap. A clearly positive gap suggests a right skew, a clearly negative gap suggests a left skew, and a gap near 0 is consistent with symmetric (but see the bus rides: a gap near 0 does not rule out two clusters, so look at the histogram).

Desmos Scientific does plain arithmetic only. To check the mean by hand, type 135/12 → 11.25 (the 12 values add to 135); the median is the average of the 6th and 7th ordered values, (7+8)/2 → 7.5; the gap is 11.25-7.5 → 3.75. Desmos Scientific does not take a list of data, so add the values yourself first.

🤖 AI TRAINING · PROMPT → PREDICT → VERIFY
Prompt. Paste these 12 made-up run times (seconds) into an AI tool: 30, 33, 35, 36, 38, 40, 41, 43, 44, 46, 48, 80. Ask: “If I remove the 80, what happens to the mean, the median, and the standard deviation? Give the numbers.”
Predict. Predict before you read the answer. The 80 is a high value in a set of 12, so removing it should pull the mean down by a noticeable amount, nudge the median down only slightly, and shrink the standard deviation a lot. A reply that says the median and the mean change by about the same amount is a red flag.
Verify. Check in the widget. Open the All tab, choose One far outlier, and write down the mean (42.83), median (40.5), and standard deviation (sample, 12.88). Click the dot at 80 and then the Remove selected button. The 11 values left are 30, 33, 35, 36, 38, 40, 41, 43, 44, 46, 48. You should now see mean \(\dfrac{434}{11} \approx 39.45\), median 40, and sample SD about 5.63 (population SD about 5.37). So the mean fell by about 3.38, the median by only 0.5, and the SD fell by more than half, from 12.88 to 5.63. Did the AI get all three numbers right, and say which SD it used (sample divides by \(n - 1\))? A tool that is confidently off on any of them has given you a number to check, not a fact.
