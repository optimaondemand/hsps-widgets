Bias: How Samples and Questions Go Wrong
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A survey can fail in two places: in who answers, and in what is asked. Learn to find the failure, name it, and say which way it pushes the result.
Define bias, sort its sources into “who answers” and “how it is asked,” see how a poll can read 75% when the truth is 52%, and rewrite a loaded question so it is fair.
Reading & work: Read the worked example and the question lab, then complete three activities (one in Excel). Plan on about 90 minutes. Every town, school, survey, and number on this page is constructed (made up).

2

✨ RESPONSIBILITY: HONEST QUESTIONS, HONEST ANSWERS
A survey is a conversation with a lot of people at once, and the person who writes it holds the power. Choose the wrong people, or phrase the question to nudge them, and the numbers will look scientific while telling a story the people never told. Nobody has to intend this. Most bias is an accident of convenience, hope, or haste.
The virtue here is responsibility: to ask fairly, to look for how your own method could mislead, and to say so plainly when it might. A responsible surveyor is the first person to hunt for the flaw in her own survey.
🎯 WHAT BIAS IS (AND WHAT IT IS NOT)
In Lesson 31.1 you saw that two honest samples from the same town give different answers. That wobble is chance variation. It points in no particular direction: some samples come out a little high, some a little low, and over many samples the misses cancel out.
Bias is different. A method is biased when it tends to miss the truth in the same direction every time you use it. Picture a bathroom scale (made up) that reads 2 kilograms too heavy. Weigh yourself five times and you get five readings that agree closely, and every one is wrong. Repeating the measurement does not help, because the error is built into the method.
Chance variationBias
Direction of the missSometimes high, sometimes lowLeans the same way each time
What happens with many repeatsMisses average out toward the truthAverage stays off the truth
Does a bigger sample help?Yes: estimates settle downNo: a bigger sample just leans harder on the same wrong spot
Where it comes fromThe luck of which people were drawnHow people were chosen, who answers, how questions are asked, or how things are measured
A bigger sample cannot repair bias. You saw it in the sampling simulator: the convenience method stayed off the truth even at n = 60. The 1936 magazine poll is a historical example of the same trap: a very large number of replies, but the way it reached people leaned one way. Size lowers chance error. It does nothing about a lean.
🔍 TWO PLACES A SURVEY CAN GO WRONG
Sort every bias you meet into one of two bins. Ask: is the trouble with who ended up in my results, or with what was asked and how the answer was recorded?
Bin 1: who answers (selection problems).
SourceWhat goes wrongA made-up example
Undercoverage (a sampling-frame problem)The list you draw from leaves out part of the population, so those people have no chance to be picked, even by a random draw.A town surveys its residents by drawing from the landline phone book. Households with only mobile phones are never on the list.
Voluntary response (self-selection)People choose themselves. Those with strong feelings answer; the rest stay silent.An open web poll about a new park: anyone may click, and people may click more than once.
NonresponsePeople were chosen fairly but many do not answer, and the ones who do may differ from the ones who do not.A random mailing of 300 questionnaires: only 60 come back, mostly from people who care about the topic.
ConvenienceAsking whoever is easiest to reach.Surveying the first 20 students who walk into the gym about how much students exercise.
Bin 2: how it is asked and recorded (response and measurement problems).
SourceWhat goes wrongA made-up example
Response bias (including social desirability)People give an answer that is untrue, often because they want to look good or avoid a clash.Asked aloud in front of classmates, “Did you copy anyone’s homework this month?” Fewer people say yes than did it.
Loaded or leading wordingWords or phrasing push toward one answer.“Don’t you agree that the cafeteria is far too crowded?”
Question orderAn earlier question changes how people hear a later one.See the order example below.
Double-barreled questionTwo questions in one, so a single answer cannot be read.“Should the school start later and add a longer lunch?” Someone who wants only one of the two cannot answer honestly.
Measurement biasThe tool or procedure itself reads off in one direction.A scale that always reads 2 kg too heavy; a thermometer left in the sun.
Notice a pattern: nearly every item in Bin 1 is about who and nearly every item in Bin 2 is about what. A single survey can fail in both bins at once.
🏭 WORKED EXAMPLE — HOW A CALL-IN POLL READS 75% WHEN THE TRUTH IS 52%
Constructed data, not real. A made-up town of 1,000 residents is deciding on a skate park. The local radio station asks, “Should we build it? Call in and tell us.” We know something the station does not. Suppose we could ask everybody. Then 520 of the 1,000 would say yes, so the true yes share is 520 / 1,000 = 52%.
Now the mechanism. Residents differ in how strongly they feel, and the people who feel strongly are far more likely to pick up the phone. Say the town splits into three groups, with a different chance of calling in:
GroupResidentsWould say yesWould say noChance of calling inCallsYes callsNo calls
Feel strongly3002802025%75705
Feel somewhat2001604010%20164
Feel little500804205%25421
Whole town1,00052048012% overall1209030
Each call count is the group size times the chance of calling in. For example, the strong group: 300 × 0.25 = 75 calls. Of those 300 residents, 280 would say yes, so 280 × 0.25 = 70 yes calls and 20 × 0.25 = 5 no calls. Do the same for the other two rows and add the columns.
▶ What the station sees: 120 calls, 90 of them yes. The station announces 90 / 120 = 75% in favor.
▶ What is true: 520 / 1,000 = 52% in favor.
▶ The miss: 75 − 52 = 23 percentage points too high, and it leans upward every time the station runs a poll this way.
▶ Where it came from: the “feel strongly” group is 30% of the town (300 of 1,000) but 62.5% of the calls (75 of 120). That group says yes about 93% of the time (70 of 75), so it drags the result up. The “feel little” group is half the town but only about 21% of the calls (25 of 120), and it mostly says no (21 of 25).
A repair, with a caution. Because we know the group sizes in this made-up town, we can undo the imbalance by weighting each group’s yes share by its share of the town: 0.30 × (70/75) + 0.20 × (16/20) + 0.50 × (4/25) = 0.28 + 0.16 + 0.08 = 0.52. That is 52%, the truth. But a real surveyor rarely knows each caller’s group, or the group sizes, so this is a thinking tool, not a rescue for a self-selected poll. The honest report is the narrow one: “Of the 120 people who called, 75% said yes. We cannot say what the town thinks.”
📝 THE QUESTION LAB: SIX PAIRS, LOADED AND NEUTRAL
All questions below are constructed examples. Read each loaded version and decide what is wrong before you read across to the right-hand column. Then check how the neutral version fixes it.
#Loaded versionNeutral versionWhat is wrong with the loaded one
1“Don’t you agree that our crowded cafeteria needs to be fixed?”“How satisfied are you with the seating space in the cafeteria? (Very satisfied, Satisfied, Neutral, Dissatisfied, Very dissatisfied)”Leading (begins “Don’t you agree”) and loaded (“crowded,” “needs to be fixed” assume the answer)
2“Do you support the wasteful new bus route?”“Do you support or oppose adding the new bus route?”Loaded word (“wasteful”)
3“Should the town build a new library and cut the park budget?”Two questions: “Should the town build a new library?” and, separately, “Should the town reduce the park budget?” (each: support, oppose, no opinion)Double-barreled
4“Do you join the responsible majority who recycle at home?”“At home, how often do you recycle? (Always, Usually, Sometimes, Never)”Loaded and pressuring: it makes one answer sound good and the other shameful (invites social desirability)
5“Experts say students get too much homework. Do you think you get too much?”“Compared with what you think is right, do you get too little, about the right amount, or too much homework?”Leading: it opens with an authority that points to one answer (and the unnamed “experts” cannot be checked)
6“You finish your homework every night, right?”“In the past 7 nights, on how many nights did you finish all your homework? (0 to 7)”Leading (“right?”) and invites a flattering answer; the neutral version asks for a count, which is easier to answer truthfully
What the neutral versions have in common. They ask one thing at a time. They use plain words with no judgment built in. They offer answer choices that cover every honest answer, in balanced order (satisfied and dissatisfied, support and oppose). And when the topic invites a flattering answer, they ask about something specific the person can report, not about who they are.
A quick test. Cover the question and read only the answer choices. Could a reader tell which answer the writer hopes for? If yes, rewrite.
🔄 QUESTION ORDER: AN EXAMPLE TO IMAGINE
This is a hypothetical, not a report of a real study. Imagine a made-up school survey with two questions. Version X asks: “(1) Do you think the school has too much homework? (2) How would you rate the school overall?” Version Y asks the same two questions in the opposite order.
Now imagine students who answer Version X. By the time they reach the overall rating, they have just been thinking about homework, so a complaint about homework may color their rating. Students answering Version Y rate the school first, with homework not yet on their minds. If the two versions gave different overall ratings, nothing about the school changed. Only the order did.
We cannot say how large such an effect would be in any real survey, and it will vary from survey to survey. The responsible habit is to keep general questions before specific ones when possible, to give every respondent the same order, and, when the order might matter, to use two versions and compare them.
🎲 WHAT RANDOM SELECTION FIXES, AND WHAT IT DOES NOT
A random method protects you from the chooser’s bias, and from the bias of people choosing themselves. It is a powerful tool, but it is not a cure for everything. Read the table and notice how many problems remain.
ProblemDoes a random draw fix it?Why
Convenience or judgment choosing (the picker leans toward the easy or the familiar)YesChance does the choosing, so no one’s habits steer who is picked.
Voluntary response (people select themselves)Yes, if you choose whom to ask instead of letting people opt inEveryone on your list has a chance, not just the eager.
Undercoverage (the list is incomplete)NoYou can only draw from the list you have. A fair draw from a list with missing people still misses them.
NonresponseNoChosen people can still say nothing, and those who stay silent may differ.
Loaded wording, order, double-barreled questionsNoThe question steers the answer even when the sample is perfect.
Response bias and measurement biasNoPeople still misreport, and a scale that reads high still reads high.
Chance variationNo, but it can be measuredA random sample still wobbles from sample to sample; chance lets us say how much.
The lesson is not “randomize and relax.” It is: randomize to protect against the chooser, then hunt for every other lean by hand. A well-run survey chases nonresponse (follow-up calls, short questions), checks the list against the population, and tests the questions before using them.
🏛️ WHERE PEOPLE USE THIS · PUSH POLLS AND SELF-SELECTED ONLINE POLLS
Two things you may meet in everyday life are worth knowing by name. A push poll is a call dressed up as a survey whose real aim is to spread a message. Its “questions” are loaded on purpose, such as a question that states a damaging claim about someone as if it were fact and then asks how the listener feels. Many people who study polling treat push polls as persuasion, not measurement. A self-selected online poll is one where anyone who sees the link may click, possibly more than once, and where the people most motivated to click are the ones who answer. Neither one is a reliable estimate of what a population thinks. When you meet a poll, ask the questions from this lesson: who was asked, who answered, how was it asked, and could anyone vote more than once? Details differ from poll to poll, so look up how a survey was done before relying on its numbers.
💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · EXCEL: A QUICK WEIGHTED CHECK ON A SELF-SELECTED POLL
Set up the table. Put these headings in row 1: A Group, B Residents, C Would say yes, D Replies, E Yes replies. Type one group per row in rows 2 to 4, and totals with =SUM(B2:B4) in row 5 (copy across to C, D, E).

Reply share by group. In F2 type =D2/$D$5 and fill down. This is each group’s share of the replies. Compare it with its share of the town, =B2/$B$5. A group that has a much bigger share of replies than of the town is over-represented.

Respondent-only percent versus whole-population percent. In one cell type =E5/D5 (yes among those who replied) and, in the next cell, =C5/B5 (yes in the whole population). Format both as percentages. The difference between them is the bias.

Weighted check. In H2 type =E2/D2 and fill down to H4 (each group’s yes share among its repliers). Then in a cell type =SUMPRODUCT(B2:B4,H2:H4)/B5. It multiplies each group’s yes share by the number of residents in the group and divides by the town total. This works only when you know the group sizes, so use it to understand a bias, not to excuse a bad poll.

Desmos Scientific is for plain arithmetic only. Type 90/120 to get 0.75, then multiply by 100 for 75%. Type 520/1000 for 0.52. The weighted check is plain arithmetic too: 0.3*70/75+0.2*16/20+0.5*4/25 gives 0.52.
🤖 AI TRAINING · ASK AN AI FOR AN UNBIASED QUESTION, THEN BIAS-HUNT IT (PROMPT → PREDICT → VERIFY)
Predict first. Before you open any AI tool, write your own neutral survey question about this topic: whether students should start the school day 30 minutes later. Keep it to one sentence with answer choices.
Prompt. Now give an AI tool exactly this: “Write one unbiased survey question, with answer choices, asking high-school students how they feel about starting the school day 30 minutes later.”
Verify with the five-flag bias hunt. Check the AI’s question against all five flags and record your result in your notebook:
▶ Flag 1, loaded words: any word that carries praise or blame (“much-needed,” “disruptive”)?
▶ Flag 2, leading frame: does the phrasing signal which answer is wanted (“Wouldn’t you agree…”, a reason offered for only one side)?
▶ Flag 3, double-barreled: is more than one question packed into one?
▶ Flag 4, unbalanced choices: are the answer choices lopsided, missing a middle or a “no opinion,” or not covering every honest answer?
▶ Flag 5, pressure to look good: does the question make one answer feel like the admirable one?
Record a table with one row per flag: tripped or clear, and the exact words that tripped it. Then report: how many flags did the AI’s question trip, what did you change, and did your own question trip any flag? Try the prompt a second time with different wording, and compare. An AI may sound confident while quietly adding a persuasive reason or a lopsided scale, so treat its first draft as a draft.
▶  Hints for the bias hunt
Things an AI sometimes does: (1) adds a justification such as “to improve sleep and learning” that supports one side; (2) combines two ideas, such as “later and more flexible”; (3) offers choices like “Strongly support, Support, Neutral” with nothing on the oppose side; (4) says “unbiased” in its own description without checking itself. A clean version might be: “How do you feel about starting the school day 30 minutes later than it does now? (Strongly support, Support, Neither support nor oppose, Oppose, Strongly oppose)” Your result may differ, and a clean first try is possible. If the AI’s question trips no flags, say so and say how you checked.
