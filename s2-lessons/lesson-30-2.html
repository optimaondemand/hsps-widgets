Random Sampling and Representativeness
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A random sample is trustworthy because of how it is chosen, not because it looks like the population.
Known chances of selection; why chance protects against the researcher's own bias; the simple random sample; drawing one with a random number list or Excel; duplicates; what “representative” honestly means; why a bigger sample does not cure a biased method
Reading & work: Read every section in order, work each example on paper before you read its solution, use the Sampling Simulator interactive, follow the Excel steps, and complete the three activities at the bottom. Plan on about 75 minutes.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain that a random sample gives every member of the population a known chance of being selected, and why that protects a study against the researcher’s own bias (the choices a person makes, even without meaning to).
2
I will be able to describe a simple random sample (every group of size n is equally likely) and carry one out with a random number list or the Excel function =RANDBETWEEN, handling duplicate draws by redrawing (or by the optional =RAND and sort method).
3
I will be able to say honestly what “representative” means: a random method is unbiased, but any single sample can still miss the population by chance.
4
I will be able to recognize that making a biased sample larger does not remove the bias.

✨ A WONDER TO START WITH
A cook wants to know whether a big pot of soup needs salt. She does not drink the whole pot. She stirs it well, and then she tastes one spoonful. The stirring is the important part. If the salt had all sunk to the bottom and she skimmed a spoonful from the top, her taste would tell her about the top, and not about the soup.
Sampling a population is the same problem with people instead of soup. We almost never ask everyone, so we taste a spoonful, a sample. The whole question is how the spoonful gets chosen. Last lesson you separated a parameter (a fact about the whole population) from a statistic (a fact about a sample). Today we ask what makes a statistic a fair guide to its parameter.
Responsibility, this module’s virtue, shows up here in a plain way: whoever chooses the sample decides whose voice is counted. Choosing carefully, and being honest about what a sample can and cannot tell us, is part of owning our claims.
A note on honesty. Every town, club, list, and number in this lesson is constructed (made up) to teach the idea, with one exception: the short history note below, which is hedged and which you should check for yourself.
🎲 EVERY GROUP, EVERY PERSON: WHAT “RANDOM” MEANS
A sample is random when chance, not a person’s choice, decides who is in it, and when we can say in advance how likely each member is to be chosen. Two ideas sit inside that sentence. The first is a known chance of selection for each member. The second is stronger, and it is the one statisticians use as a standard.
A simple random sample (SRS) of size n is a sample chosen so that every possible group of n members is equally likely to be the one chosen. Drawing names from a well-mixed hat, without putting any back, makes an SRS. A side effect is that every individual also has the same chance of being included. But the reverse is not true, as the next example shows.
Why chance protects against bias. When a person picks “typical-looking” members, or the ones nearby, or the ones who seem friendly, the person’s own habits and hopes steer the sample. A researcher who expects young people to like a plan may, without any dishonesty, reach for young people. A random device has no hopes. It cannot favor the people the researcher expected to agree. That is why random selection is the main defense of a study against the researcher’s own bias.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 1: WHICH METHODS ARE A SIMPLE RANDOM SAMPLE?
Problem. Four people, A, B, C, D, stand in a row (a constructed example). We want a sample of n = 2. Compare three methods. Method 1: write each possible pair on a card and draw one card. Method 2: flip a coin; heads gives the pair A, B and tails gives the pair C, D. Method 3: pick one of the seats A, B, or C at random and take that person and the person to their right. Which are simple random samples?
Teacher’s voice: First I list every possible group of 2. From four people there are 4 × 3 ÷ 2 = 6 of them: AB, AC, AD, BC, BD, CD. In Method 1 each card is drawn with chance 1/6, so every possible group is equally likely. That is an SRS. Each person appears in 3 of the 6 pairs, so each person is included with chance 3/6 = 1/2.
Method 2 also gives every person a 1/2 chance: A is in the sample exactly when the coin shows heads. But only two of the six groups can ever happen. AC, AD, BC, BD can never be chosen. Equal chances for people is not enough. This method is not an SRS.
In Method 3 the only possible groups are AB, BC, and CD, each with chance 1/3. Person A is in only AB, so A’s chance is 1/3, while B is in AB and BC, so B’s chance is 2/3 (and D’s is 1/3). The chances are known, but not equal, and many groups are impossible. This is not an SRS either.
Answer. Only Method 1 is a simple random sample. Method 2 is fair to each person but forbids most groups; Method 3 is not even fair to each person. (Methods like these have names, and you will meet them next lesson.)
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 2: DRAWING AN SRS FROM A RANDOM NUMBER LIST
Problem. A made-up club has 40 members numbered 01 to 40. Use this made-up list of random digits to draw an SRS of n = 5 members. Read the digits in pairs, left to right: 2 9 1 4 1 7 7 7 6 3 1 7 0 6 6 9 0 7.
Teacher’s voice: The pairs are 29, 14, 17, 77, 63, 17, 06, 69, 07. I take a pair only if it is a real member number, 01 to 40, and I take each member only once, because a person cannot be in a sample twice.
29 is a member, so I keep it. 14 is kept. 17 is kept. 77 is larger than 40, so I skip it. 63 is larger than 40, so I skip it. 17 was already chosen, so I skip it (a repeat). 06 is a member, so I keep it. I now have four. 69 is skipped, and 07 is kept, which makes five.
Answer. Members 29, 14, 17, 06, 07. Skipping out-of-range numbers does not hurt fairness, because every real number is still equally likely to come up at each step. Skipping repeats is what makes it a draw without replacement, which is how an SRS works.
📝 WORKED EXAMPLE 3: HOW CLOSE IS A RANDOM SAMPLE, REALLY?
Problem. A made-up club of 60 members has exactly 30 who own a library card and 30 who do not, so the true share who own one is 50%. An SRS of n = 8 members is drawn. (i) What is the chance that the sample share is exactly 50%, that is, 4 of 8? (ii) What is the chance of 3, 4, or 5 owners?
Teacher’s voice: I count samples. The number of ways to choose 8 of the 60 members is C(60, 8). The number of ways to choose exactly 4 owners and 4 non-owners is C(30, 4) × C(30, 4). Because an SRS makes every group equally likely, the chance is the ratio of the counts.
(i) P(4 of 8) = C(30,4) × C(30,4) ÷ C(60,8) is about 0.294. So only about 29% of samples hit 50% exactly; about 71% do not, and that happens even though the method is perfectly fair.
(ii) P(3 or 4 or 5) is about 0.226 + 0.294 + 0.226 = about 0.746 (the exact sum of the three counts, to three decimals). The other roughly 25% of samples have 0, 1, 2, 6, 7, or 8 owners, which is a share of 25% or less, or 75% or more.
Answer. A random sample of 8 usually lands near 50% but usually not on 50%, and sometimes lands well away. That is not a defect in the method. It is what chance does. So “representative” cannot mean “mirrors the population.”
💭 WHAT “REPRESENTATIVE” HONESTLY MEANS
People say a random sample is “representative.” The word is slippery, so use it carefully. There are two different claims:
▶ A claim about the method (true for random sampling). The method is unbiased: it does not systematically lean toward high values or low values, or toward one kind of person. If you could repeat the draw many times, the sample statistics would scatter around the true parameter and not pile up on one side of it.
▶ A claim about one sample (not guaranteed). The sample in front of you may or may not match the population in its mix of ages, or its share of “yes” answers. A single random sample can, by chance, miss. Worked Example 3 showed that most samples of 8 do not hit the exact percent.
Two phrases to retire, because they sound careful but are wrong: “the sample will look just like the population” and “a random sample is representative on average.” The first promises too much about one sample. The second mixes up the two claims: it is the estimates that are right on average over many samples, not a sample that is somehow an average of the population. An honest sentence sounds like this: “We chose the sample at random, so our method does not lean in any direction. Our estimate is probably close, but it is still an estimate, and a different random sample would give a somewhat different number.”
🎲 Try It Yourself: Random Versus Convenient
Four tasks. The town and every number in it are made up. Keep the tracked quantity on Average minutes of homework. (1) Under Method choose Simple random and set the sample size to n = 20. Press Draw a sample a few times and watch the estimate move around. Then press Repeat 100 times. The pile of dots should center near the same spot with the errors falling on both sides. (2) Choose Convenience (nearest the entrance) at n = 20 and press Repeat 100 times. The dots will bunch up tightly, but away from where the simple random dots were. (3) Press Reveal the true values. The true average is about 68.36 minutes. Over many draws, simple random is off by about 0.00 minutes on average, and convenience is about 14.69 minutes too low (a bias of about −14.69). Convenience is tight and wrong; simple random is wider and fair. (4) Press Try n = 60 with Convenience. Tripling the sample size leaves convenience still about 11.97 minutes too low. A bigger biased sample is still a biased sample. Write one sentence saying what you would tell a researcher who says “I will just ask more people at the entrance.”

⚠️ SIZE DOES NOT FIX BIAS
You just saw it in the simulator, and it is worth saying slowly. A larger sample cures chance error: the scatter of random samples around the truth shrinks as n grows. It does nothing for bias: a method that leans one way keeps leaning the same way no matter how many people it reaches. Think of a scale that reads two pounds heavy. Weighing more objects on it does not fix the scale.
So when you meet a claim like “our survey is reliable because 10,000 people answered,” the right first question is not “how many?” but “how were they chosen, and who never had a chance to be chosen?” Large numbers can hide a bad method; they cannot rescue it.
📖 HISTORY · A POLL THAT WAS BIG AND WRONG
In 1936 a popular U.S. magazine, the Literary Digest, mailed out a very large number of mock ballots before the presidential election and received back millions of responses. Its poll predicted that the challenger, Alf Landon, would defeat President Franklin Roosevelt. Roosevelt won in a landslide. Accounts of what went wrong usually point to two problems. First, the mailing lists are generally described as drawing heavily on sources such as telephone directories and automobile registrations. In the Depression years those lists leaned toward wealthier households, so many voters had little or no chance of being asked at all. Second, only some of the people who received a ballot mailed it back, and people who choose to reply are often different from people who do not (this is called nonresponse). Accounts also report that smaller polls that took more careful samples came closer to the actual result. The lesson is not about one number. Millions of responses could not repair a method that tilted who got counted: more of a biased sample is still a biased sample. Check this account yourself. This summary is hedged on purpose, and it comes from memory of how the story is usually told. Look it up in more than one reliable source before you repeat it, exactly the habit the AI task below asks of you.
🌎 WHERE YOU MIGHT SEE THIS: “MILLIONS OF VOTES” ONLINE
Open online polls, call-in votes, and “reply to this survey” links often announce big totals. Everyone who answered chose to answer. That is self-selection, and it is the same flaw as nonresponse: the people who respond may differ from the people who do not. A random sample of a few hundred, with careful follow-up, usually says more about a population than a self-selected crowd of a hundred thousand. When you read a poll, ask who was invited and how many of them actually replied.
💻 EXCEL: DRAW A RANDOM SAMPLE, CLICK BY CLICK
You will practice a random draw in a spreadsheet. Everything here is made up. Think of a made-up list of 200 numbered members and a value for each.
▶ Open Excel and start a Blank workbook. Save it as Wk30_L2_YourName (practice; you do not upload it).
▶ In A1 type ID and in B1 type Value. In A2 type 1 and in A3 type 2. Select A2:A3, then drag the small square at the bottom right corner of the selection down to A201. The IDs should run 1 to 200.
▶ Click B2 and type =MOD(37*A2,101)+20, press Enter, then drag the fill square of B2 down to B201. This builds a made-up value for each ID without typing 200 numbers. Check: B6 (ID 5) should show 104, and B2 should show 57.
▶ In D1 type Pick, in E1 type Repeats, and in F1 type Value. Click D2, type =RANDBETWEEN(1,200), press Enter, and drag the fill square down to D9. That is eight random IDs. Press F9 (on some laptops Fn + F9) to see a fresh draw.
▶ Check for duplicates. Click E2 and type =COUNTIF($D$2:$D$9,D2), press Enter, and fill down to E9. Each cell counts how many times that ID appears in the eight picks. If every cell shows 1, there are no duplicates. If any cell shows 2 or more, you drew someone twice: press F9 to redraw, and repeat until every cell is 1. (About 13% of draws of 8 from 200 have a repeat, so you may see it.)
▶ Freeze the picks. Select D2:D9 and press Ctrl + C. Right-click the same selection, under Paste Options choose Values (the clipboard icon marked 123), or use Home > Paste > Paste Special > Values. The formulas turn into plain numbers that no longer change.
▶ Pull the values. Click F2 and type =VLOOKUP(D2,$A$2:$B$201,2,FALSE), press Enter, and fill down to F9. (Another way: =INDEX($B$2:$B$201,D2) works here because each ID equals its position in the list.) In F11 type =AVERAGE(F2:F9) for your sample mean.
The RAND and sort alternative (optional). To avoid duplicates entirely, put =RAND() beside each of the 200 IDs, copy and paste those random numbers as values, sort the IDs by the random column (Data > Sort), and take the first 8. Every ID gets its own random number, so no one can be picked twice.
💻 CALCULATOR SKILLS · RANDBETWEEN, COUNTIF, AND VLOOKUP IN EXCEL; REPEAT-CHANCES IN DESMOS
Excel. =RANDBETWEEN(1,200) picks a whole number from 1 to 200, ends included. Copy it down for as many picks as you need. =COUNTIF($D$2:$D$9,D2) tells you how many times each pick appears, and =VLOOKUP(D2,$A$2:$B$201,2,FALSE) (or =INDEX($B$2:$B$201,D2)) fetches the value that goes with each ID.
Volatility warning. RANDBETWEEN and RAND recalculate every time anything in the workbook changes, and when you reopen the file. So a sample you drew and then analyzed can quietly change under you. Always copy the picks and use Paste Special > Values to freeze them before you compute anything from them.
Desmos Scientific is plain arithmetic here. The chance that 8 picks from 200 (draws that are allowed to repeat) contain at least one repeat is one minus the chance that all 8 differ. Type 1-(200*199*198*197*196*195*194*193)/200^8 and you should get about 0.132, so about 13%. The same idea with 60 IDs, 1-(60*59*58*57*56*55*54*53)/60^8, gives about 0.386.
🤖 AI TRAINING · ASK AN AI WHY RANDOM SAMPLING WORKS; CATCH “REPRESENTATIVE ON AVERAGE”
Prompt. Ask an AI tool: “Why does random sampling work? Explain in about five sentences for a high-school student, and say whether a random sample is representative.”
Predict. Before you read its answer, decide what a careful answer must say: chance, not a person, decides who is chosen; the method is unbiased; any one sample can still miss the population; and a larger sample shrinks chance error but does not fix bias.
Verify. Look for the wrong phrases: “representative on average” and “the sample will look just like the population.” Did it claim a random sample is guaranteed to mirror the population? Did it say a large sample fixes a biased method? Did it mention that results from different random samples will differ? Ask it to defend the exact phrase, then rewrite its worst sentence so that it is true. Verdict. A fluent answer is not a correct answer; you are the checker.

▶  Check
1. (a) “We chose 50 voters at random, so our method does not lean either way.
