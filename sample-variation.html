4. What to notice

Honest note. One sample can miss the true value just by chance, even when nobody did anything wrong and the sampling is completely fair. That is not bias. It is sampling variation, and it is exactly what the spread above is showing.

The question. Someone claims a chance process works a certain way. You have one result. If the claim were true, how often would chance alone give a result this far from what we expect? We simulate 1000 runs to find out. This is a judgment about consistency, not a proof.

3. Sticking with the model or doubting it

Sticking with the model. If the result is not unusual under the claim, chance alone could easily have produced it. We have no good reason to drop the claim. That does not prove the claim is right, because other models could fit the data too.
Doubting the model. If the result is unusual under the claim, either we saw a rare stroke of luck or the claim is off. The rarer the result, the more reason to doubt. It still does not prove the claim wrong, since rare things do happen.

“At least this far” means: the distance between a run’s count and the expected count (n times the claimed chance) is the same as, or bigger than, the distance for your observed count, on either side. The cutoff is a rule of thumb you can change, not a law.
